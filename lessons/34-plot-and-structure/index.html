Two lessons from Margaret Atwood’s class, back to back, and they belong together. The first is about plot: a story needs events and characters, and it starts when something breaks a pattern. It also needs a toolkit, the old stories that every new one is built from and played against. The second is about structure: the same plot can be told from the beginning, from inside the wolf, backwards from the body on the floor, or as a story within a story within a story. And when you’re starting out, Atwood’s advice is to learn the simple version first. Two labs: break a pattern, and tell one story eight ways.

Arc A · Something has to happen

3 moves
1

The breakfast play

A story needs events, and it needs characters

In the lectureWhen Atwood’s child and friends were five, they announced: “We’re going to put on a play.” Tickets were 25 cents each, so of course the grown-ups bought some and sat down to watch. The play was about breakfast. “Would you like some orange juice?” “Yes, thank you.” “Good. Here is your orange juice. I am having cereal. Would you like some cereal?” This went on for a while, until the audience asked: “Is anything else going to happen?” And they said: “No.” “In that case, we’re leaving and we’ll come back when something else is going to happen.” The lesson: “The story needs to have events and it needs to have characters.” Any story, even the most elementary ones, such as Aesop’s fables or jokes, has both.

The breakfast play had characters, dialogue, props and an audience that had paid. What it lacked was the one thing an audience will leave over: nothing could happen next that was different from what had just happened. The rest of this lesson is about where that difference comes from.

Elsewhere in these notesNeil Gaiman’s version of the same point is a game with a three-year-old: “What’s going to happen?” (topic 10, move 2), and the four words a storyteller wants to hear: “and then what happened?” (move 3).

2

A break in the pattern

A severed hand in the rose patch

In the lecture“A story needs a break in a pattern to get it going.” The break can be this: “one day Mavis, who was an avid gardener, went out to her rose patch and found a severed hand.” “If everything is perfect all the time, there isn’t a story.” Life is just wonderful every day, “so it doesn’t become a story until somebody kidnaps Rover the dog”. An event interrupts a pattern, and with that interruption the story is kicked off. A good plot has something happening in it “that is of interest to the reader and we hope to the characters. Or maybe I’ll put that the other way around. That is of interest to the characters and we hope to the reader.”

The correction Atwood makes mid-sentence is the point. The event has to matter to the characters first, because a reader’s interest is borrowed from theirs. A severed hand that Mavis shrugs off is a curiosity; a severed hand that Mavis recognises is a story.

Try it — break the pattern

Pick a settled life and a kind of trouble, the way Atwood does with John and Mary in the next move. The story starts at the break. Your notes stay in this browser only.

    3

    Threats from without, and from within

    John, Mary, a tentacled monster, and a vampire

    In the lectureJohn and Mary are living happily in their split-level with two cars. Then one day a strange green light is seen in the sky, a canister descends right behind their house, and out of it comes a tentacled monster: threat from without. Or: John and Mary are living in their split-level bungalow, but then Mary discovers that John is cheating on her: threat from within. Combine those: John discovers that Mary is mysteriously absent during parts of the night, “and has developed an alarming tendency to sleep in the bathtub with all the curtains drawn”. What are those strange white fangs? Could it be that Mary is a vampire? What is John going to do, “and what about the children?” All of these are events: “blood pressure increasing, suspense building plot devices to make us want to know what is going to happen next.”

    The three versions differ in where the trouble comes from, and so in what the characters must do. A monster from outside unites John and Mary against it. Cheating sets them against each other. The vampire does both at once: the danger is outside the marriage and inside it, and the children make it everyone’s problem.

    Drill — three breaks

    Write a settled life in three sentences: who, where, what happens every day. Then write three one-paragraph openings that break it: once from without, once from within, once with both.

    Read them back and notice which one makes you most want to know what happens next. That is usually the one where the trouble reaches into a relationship.

    Arc B · The toolkit

    4 moves
    4

    A giant Lego set

    The building blocks of story

    In the lectureThe building blocks of story in Western civilisation will be somewhat different in other cultures, but each has its own set: the toolkit of stories. “Think of it as a giant Lego set”, from which you can build your own structures, because the pieces fit together. For Western cultures: Greek and Roman myths; folk tales, the Grimm collections and those that followed, like Andrew Lang’s collections of fairy tales from around the world; more recently, Indigenous stories from North and South America, and, as people move around the world, African stories. And the Bible: “I hate to use the word fundamental. It has some essential stories that people should know”, because it’s almost impossible to read English literature written before, say, 1940 without meeting biblical references and the modes of thought that come from them. If you want to write and want a large toolkit, these are building blocks that served a great many writers before you.

    Beyond the lectureThe Grimms’ Children’s and Household Tales first appeared in 1812. Andrew Lang’s are the twelve “coloured” Fairy Books, from The Blue Fairy Book (1889) to The Lilac Fairy Book (1910), tales gathered from many countries and published by Andrew Lang and Leonora Blanche Lang, a married couple (Wikipedia). All are long out of copyright and free to read online.

    5

    Just read it

    Not a seminar: a storyteller’s reading

    In the lectureIf you pick up one of these pieces of the toolkit, “I suggest you just read it.” You’re not in a university classroom in which you have to analyse it, study it and come up with a theory about it. For a storyteller, the point is to learn how these kinds of stories go.

    It’s a different kind of reading from the one school trains. The storyteller is collecting moves, not interpretations: how a fairy tale sets its terms in a line, how a myth makes a punishment fit a crime, how a parable stops. The theory can come later, if ever; the moves have to be in your hands.

    6

    To get the joke, know the original

    Cinderella, and its variants

    In the lecturePeople make fun of these stories all the time, “but in order to get the joke, you have to know the original.” The Cinderella story is “probably the oldest extant story that we know about”: it’s known in all kinds of cultures, with all kinds of variants, but it always ends with the Cinderella figure coming out on top. A satire might leave Cinderella sweeping up the ashes while one of the bad sisters goes off with the prince. A modern version might have the prince turn up and Cinderella decide that’s not what she wants. “But unless you know the original, you don’t understand why that’s an interesting variant.”

    Beyond the lectureThe earliest known version is usually taken to be the Greek story of Rhodopis, told by the geographer Strabo, writing between 7 BC and AD 23: a Greek slave girl whose sandal an eagle snatches and drops in the lap of the king of Egypt, who searches for her and marries her. A Chinese version, Ye Xian, with a magic fish and a golden shoe, was written down around 860 (Wikipedia).

    Elsewhere in these notesNeil Gaiman did the same with Snow White: after perhaps a thousand readings, noticing how strange the story is, and turning it round (topic 5, move 5). You can only turn a story round if you know which way it faced.

    7

    Maleficent, and the gun in the sand

    Expand your frame of reference

    In the lectureTake the film Maleficent: the Sleeping Beauty story. In the standard version an evil witch appears at Sleeping Beauty’s birth and predicts that she will prick her finger and sleep for a hundred years, to be woken only by true love’s kiss, and the true love is a prince. In Maleficent the prince comes along, “but he’s a dud”: nothing happens, he’s too self-centred. The one who really loves Sleeping Beauty is her surrogate mother, the initially evil witch, who has had a conversion experience. “But if you don’t know the original, what’s happening? This is weird, you will simply say to yourself.” Some writers have played on the originals brilliantly. In Samuel Beckett’s play Happy Days a woman is being steadily buried in sand, and in her handbag there is a gun. Chekhov famously said that if you put a gun on the desk in the first act, it has to go off in the third. But by the end she is so buried she can’t reach it, though you keep expecting her to: “So if you didn’t know about the Chekhov comment, this would make less sense to you.” So: “expand your frame of reference”, because it will give you a lot more depth in thinking about stories.

    Watch forThree small slips in the telling. In Perrault’s and the Grimms’ Sleeping Beauty the princess pricks her finger on a spindle (Wikipedia); in Maleficent (2014) the curse is broken by Maleficent’s kiss on her forehead (Wikipedia). And Happy Days has two acts, not three. Winnie is buried to the waist in the first and to the neck in the second, where her revolver, which she calls Brownie, lies beside her, out of reach (Wikipedia).

    Beyond the lectureChekhov made the point more than once, which is why it has several wordings. In a letter of 1889: “One must never place a loaded rifle on the stage if it isn’t going to go off. It’s wrong to make promises you don’t mean to keep.” And as a memoirist remembered it in 1911: “If you say in the first act that there is a rifle hanging on the wall, in the second or third act it absolutely must go off” (Chekhov’s gun). Beckett’s joke is a promise made and pointedly not kept.

    Arc C · Story and structure

    3 moves
    8

    What happens, and how you tell it

    Little Red Riding Hood, the simple version

    In the lecture“The story is what happens, so the plot. And the structure is how you tell the story.” A simple illustration: Little Red Riding Hood, simple version. You begin at the beginning, go on with the events in sequence, and end at the end. A little girl’s mother has made her a red cloak and hood, so everyone calls her Little Red Riding Hood. One day her mother says that her grandmother is very ill, and she must carry a basket with bread and a bottle of wine through the forest, “but don’t stray off the path because there are wolves in the forest.” She sets off, sees some beautiful flowers, thinks what a good idea it would be to pick a bouquet for her grandmother, and steps off the path. Out from behind a tree steps “a rather hairy looking gentleman” who asks what she’s doing, and she tells him: gathering flowers for her grandmother, who lives on the other side of the forest. “You know the rest.”

    Beyond the lectureThe Russian Formalists of the early twentieth century, Vladimir Propp and Viktor Shklovsky among them, gave the distinction a pair of names: the fabula, the events in the order they happen, “the raw material of a story”, and the syuzhet, the order and way in which they’re told (Wikipedia). The lab in the next move shows both at once.

    9

    It was dark inside the wolf

    The same plot, started somewhere else

    In the lectureStart it a different way: “It was dark inside the wolf.” The grandmother, gobbled whole, couldn’t say a word, because it was quite stifling “and full of old chicken parts and plastic bags that the wolf had eaten by mistake”, and she had to listen in silence as the wolf put on her nightgown and nightcap, climbed into her bed and started a terrible imitation of her. But luckily, along came… “You know the rest.” Or tell it from the wolf’s point of view. Or as a flashback, every time the grandmother remembered the awful time she had inside the wolf. Or begin as a detective story might: there on the floor lay one corpse, the wolf’s, or two, because in some versions the grandmother doesn’t come out of it so well. “What had caused this double murder?” Or with a jump ahead: little did Little Red Riding Hood know that in two weeks’ time she would be looking back on one of the most defining events of her life. Where you start and what order you tell the events in is variable. “The plot underneath it, however, it’s the same story.”

    Try it — one story, eight tellings

    The list is the story: what happens, in the order it happens. Pick a telling: the numbers below it show the order in which a reader meets the same events, and anything the telling leaves out is greyed in the list. The plot underneath never changes.

    What happens

      Drill — start somewhere else

      Take a story everyone knows: a fairy tale, a myth, a family legend. Write its first paragraph three ways: from inside the middle of it, from the villain’s side, and with the ending laid out on the floor as a mystery.

      Keep the plot exactly the same. Only the way in changes, and with it what the reader wants to know.

      10

      Rashomon, and two endings

      Three versions of one murder; a choice of ends

      In the lectureThere’s also the Rashomon approach: there is a story there somewhere, but we hear different versions of it. Rashomon is Kurosawa’s film in which the same story is told involving a murdered man, a robber and the murdered man’s wife. Each has a different version of how the murder came about; the murdered man speaks through a medium who goes into a trance. Each of the stories is different, and the viewer is left wondering which is the real one. Another device gives the reader two possible endings, as Charlotte Brontë did in the novel Villette: on the one hand, maybe it ended this way, but on the other, dear reader, if you’re feeling more hopeful, you might prefer this other version. There are many ways of telling stories that have been used for generations, “but I would never dare to say that there was nothing new under the sun, because as soon as you say that, then there is.”

      Watch forRashomon (1950), based on Ryūnosuke Akutagawa’s story “In a Grove”, actually has four accounts: the bandit’s, the wife’s, the dead samurai’s through the medium, and last a woodcutter’s, the witness who seems the most objective (Wikipedia). The film gave its name to the “Rashomon effect”.

      Beyond the lectureBrontë’s narrator never writes the sad ending out. As the storm rises over the ship carrying the man she loves home, she stops: “Here pause: pause at once. There is enough said. Trouble no quiet, kind heart; leave sunny imaginations hope. … Let them picture union and a happy succeeding life” (Villette, 1853, on Project Gutenberg). And “nothing new under the sun” is from the toolkit itself: Ecclesiastes 1:9.

      Arc D · Pull it apart

      2 moves
      11

      Like knitting

      Not an opera singer’s bad note

      In the lectureUnderstanding how the structure is going to work can take a while. “In that respect, it’s a bit like knitting”: you can pull it all apart and start again if things have not been working out. It’s one of the great things about being a writer: “You can always just pull it apart.” You’re not stuck out on the stage like an opera singer who’s just sung a bad note: you can’t take that back, everybody heard it. “But with the writing, it’s under your control until you decide to show it to somebody else.”

      Elsewhere in these notesAtwood’s own working method leaves room for exactly this: draft fast by hand, and only at about fifty or sixty pages start thinking about structure, with a lot of backtracking and re-vision after (topic 13, move 8).

      12

      Two novels in the drawer

      200 pages, and nothing had happened

      In the lectureAtwood got quite far into two novels that then had to be set aside. In the first there were too many characters, eight of them: “I knew what they had for breakfast. I knew what kind of socks they wore”, and 200 pages in, nothing had happened. “There wasn’t any story”: into the drawer. The second had too many time levels and too many locations, and the same problem: about page 150, and nothing had happened. “So if nothing is happening by page 10, you’re in trouble because you don’t know what the story is.” How do you strike the balance between something too complicated that doesn’t work and something just complicated enough that does? “You can’t tell anybody how to do that because it is hands in the mud.”

      Both failures are the breakfast play of move 1 at novel length. Knowing everything about eight characters is preparation, not plot; a story needs the break in the pattern, and two hundred pages of socks and breakfasts never got to it. The page-10 test is cheap and brutal: if nothing has happened yet, you don’t know what your story is.

      Elsewhere in these notesAn earlier novel of Atwood’s had come to nothing before The Edible Woman, which a publisher accepted and then mislaid (topic 13, move 6).

      3 moves
      13

      Scheherazade’s frame

      A thousand and one nights

      In the lectureFor a full compendium of ways to tell stories, “you could do worse than read” The Thousand and One Nights, usually known as the Arabian Nights. They were originally uncollected stories circulating around the ancient world for a long time, until “some genius put them together in a frame story”, which is another way of telling stories: within the frame, you can tell many different stories. The frame: a sultan whose wife had been caught cheating on him was so disillusioned and angry that he decided to marry one woman a day, and the next morning cut off her head. Scheherazade decided to stop this and volunteered to be the next bride, asking one favour: that her younger sister should sleep under the bed, because she was in the habit of telling her a story every night. She begins a story, and it isn’t finished by dawn, and the sultan is so eager to hear the end that he lets her live another day. She finishes that story and begins another, for a thousand and one nights. On the last, he realises he’s been a bad person, reforms, and stops cutting off girls’ heads. Within the frame come all the other stories, some with yet other stories embedded in them.

      Elsewhere in these notesNeil Gaiman found one of the Nights’ recurring moments, travellers separated in the crush at a city gate, finally made sense in old Jerusalem (topic 27, move 5).

      14

      Cliffhangers

      Tune in next week

      In the lectureThe stories Scheherazade tells are told in a certain order, to bring the sultan to a different frame of mind, but also to save her own life, “because each of those stories has to be interesting enough so that he wants to hear the end of it.” Like Charles Dickens, she ends each instalment with a cliffhanger: “What’s going to happen? Tune in next week. That’s exactly what soap operas do today.”

      Scheherazade is the purest case of the structural question: the same stories, told in a different order or stopped at a different moment, would get her killed. Where a telling breaks off is part of its structure, and so is what the listener is left needing to know.

      Beyond the lectureDickens’s novels first came out in parts, monthly or weekly, so each instalment had to end with a reason to buy the next. The Grimms and Lang are move 4’s toolkit; the Nights belong in it too, and their frame comes from an older Persian collection, the Hezār Afsān, “a thousand tales” (Wikipedia).

      15

      Four levels in The Blind Assassin

      A story within a story within a story within a story

      In the lectureIn Atwood’s own The Blind Assassin, the main character is “kind of a mean old lady”, not a kindly, nice, polite person, but quite prickly and annoyed, and she narrates both the present and the past. Then there’s another layer, a novel called The Blind Assassin, and within that novel, a story called “The Blind Assassin”, told by one of its characters to another. And there’s a layer of newspaper and magazine articles, a counterpoint to the actual story: the public version of the events going on behind the scenes. The stories told by each of these kinds of narration are all different, but related. Four levels of narration: “It’s not just a story within a story, it’s a story within a story within a story within a story.” “So it’s fancy footwork, granted. And some people follow the clues and others don’t.”

      Beyond the lectureThe Blind Assassin (2000) won the Booker Prize that year (Wikipedia). “Some people follow the clues” is a fair warning: the novel’s layers are where its secrets are kept, which is a good reason to read it before reading about it.

      Arc F · Twinkle before Beethoven

      1 move
      16

      Start simple

      You can’t play a Beethoven sonata first

      In the lecture“If you’re just starting out, forget everything I said about all of these different structures and just do a simple telling of your story to see if you can master that part.” Atwood’s first novels were pretty simple in structure. The Edible Woman, the first published, begins at the beginning and goes on until it reaches the end; the only change is a part in the middle that’s in the third person, “but the events are told in order.” It’s like any other skill: “You can’t suddenly play a Beethoven sonata until you first learn to play Twinkle Twinkle Little Star.” If you can get a simple thing to work, then you can start with the variations. With any skill, with any craft, “it is hands-on learned by doing”, and also by seeing what other people have done: what are their skill sets, how did they do it? “But also what are my skill sets and what am I up to doing right now, today?”

      Beyond the lectureThe Edible Woman came out in 1969. Its shift from first to third person and back again isn’t decoration: the narrator loses hold of her own story in the middle of the book, and takes it back at the end (Wikipedia). Even Atwood’s simple structure was doing something.

      Drill — Twinkle first

      Before trying a frame, a flashback or a second narrator, write your story once in strict order on a single page: beginning, events in sequence, end.

      If that page works, you know your plot, and every fancier telling is a choice. If it doesn’t, no structure will save it: go back to move 2 and find the break in the pattern.
