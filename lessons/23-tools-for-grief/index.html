A mother’s perfume

“Use Sensory Exposure to Break Down Barriers”

The recording picks up partway through the lesson, in the middle of a story. Harry once watched veterans in a therapy room where smells were being piped in — the smells of combat, recreated to take each veteran’s senses, body and experience back to “the point of contact”. What struck Harry was that the mind can’t stop that happening: whether you like it or not, your body takes you back there. Smells and the other senses unlock memories, feelings and experiences — perhaps the very ones the body’s and mind’s defences had shut away completely.

Many years later, on a therapist’s advice, Harry got hold of Diana’s perfume and tried it. It was like being transported to another time, and it unlocked everything that had been locked away. The wall was still there — the emotional and mental wall Harry had built, with everything good and bad on the other side — but spraying that scent was like knocking a few bricks out, so that Harry could see through and some memories and experiences began to seep back.

O’Connor explains why smell is special. Our other senses, taste and sound included, are routed through the thalamus; scent goes directly to the amygdala and the hippocampus, so it can open up memories that the other senses don’t. There are moments in grief when it’s genuinely hard to remember someone — what they looked like, what they sounded like — and scent cuts through all of it. A mother’s perfume kept on the dresser is an immediate way of conjuring the person.

And here is what matters about it: in grief, what exposure exposes you to is that the person is not coming back — that they really are gone, and that you are going to be okay.

Is it something to try at home? Many people are naturally drawn to a loved one’s clothes or pillowcase, O’Connor says, and that can be useful if the goal is to understand that the person is gone. Going into the room of someone who has died to pretend they’re still coming back, still going to get into that bed again, is a very different kind of exposure from going in and saying: this person, of whom I have such vivid memories, shaped my life, and I’m carrying that legacy forward — knowing they aren’t coming back. “So very much intention?” Harry asks. Very much intention.

Beyond the lectureThe class doesn’t name the therapy Harry saw, but it sounds like Bravemind, a virtual-reality exposure therapy for PTSD built at the University of Southern California’s Institute for Creative Technologies by a team led by Albert “Skip” Rizzo. Veterans revisit a simulated memory while “directional 3D audio, vibrations and smells are delivered into the simulation”. Exposure of this kind — facing the memory in a safe setting rather than avoiding it — is the principle part 1 ended on: incorporating rather than avoiding.

Watch forSmell’s shortcut is real but simplified here. Smell is the only sense whose main route to the cortex skips the thalamus: the olfactory bulb projects straight to the piriform cortex, the amygdala and the entorhinal cortex. The hippocampus is one step further on, through the entorhinal cortex, and there is a thalamic route as well, running alongside the direct one (reviews from 2013 and 2015). “Directly to the amygdala and hippocampus” is a fair shorthand for why a smell can bring a memory back with such force.

2

Permission to grieve

Therapy, and cleaning the windscreen

One of the greatest things Harry got from therapy was permission to grieve: sitting in a room with a complete stranger, and having the ability, the confidence and the trust to open up. Harry learned more in two or three sessions than in the previous twenty years.

The therapist’s first step was to help Harry “accept and acknowledge that I was grieving, and that it was okay to grieve”, and that what had happened was really hard. Once you can acknowledge it — once you can name it — you can start the process of recovery and healing.

Harry’s image for what therapy does is cleaning the windscreen. Life’s experiences and traumas show up as filters that distort how we see people, experiences and our everyday relationships. The work is to clean the windscreen and see life as it actually is.

(The lesson, and with it “Break the Old Patterns”, ends with the two of them joking about who has to look at whom while they film.)

Nothing, then everything

“Upgrade Your System” begins: home from war, and no middle ground

The class’s third heading, “Upgrade Your System”, opens with a promise: your body and mind already have what it takes to reset; you just need the right tools to activate them. Its first lesson, “Reboot the Hard Drive”, starts with Harry coming home from war.

When Harry finally got home from Afghanistan in 2013, there was no other mission to focus on. After years of feeling nothing, Harry went to feeling everything — and remembers wishing for a middle ground, some way to come back in a slow, steady state instead of jumping from one extreme to the other. (Part 1, move 7 told the start of this story.)

As far as everyone else was concerned, you’d just come back from war, so you must either be fine or be suffering from post-traumatic stress. Harry gave the impression of being absolutely fine, withdrew from friends and from socialising, and was far more comfortable staying at home.

Harry kept looking for someone or something to help. Unfortunately that meant a series of GPs who weren’t equipped to: it was some advice, or “take this pill”. Harry was strongly against taking a pill, took the advice, found it made no difference — and kept looking for something that would work.

4

Disorder or injury?

The word that tells you whether you can heal

Once there was a safer, more acceptable environment for veterans to talk about their invisible injuries, Harry noticed, “PTSD” started to roll off the tongue. For civilians and the media, a psychological injury in the forces came to mean you most likely had PTSD.

That has sat badly with Harry, because most of the people Harry has met don’t have what Harry would call a disorder. And what are you telling someone when you diagnose them with a disorder? That’s it: you can’t get better. A simple reframing from disorder to injury both gives you an incentive to get better and tells you that you can heal. Harry finds the distinction — post-traumatic stress the disorder against post-traumatic stress the injury — really helpful, both personally and in conversations with veterans.

Words are so important, O’Connor agrees. What matters is that the people living with these experiences — these “flavours of experience” — have their views valued: if injury is the word that works for them, injury is the word that should be used. It is post-traumatic, and the response to the injury has developed in a disordered way, so the work is to find the barriers and deal with the complications. But to O’Connor, the disordered response is not permanent. Most important of all is that we keep talking about it.

Beyond the lectureHarry isn’t alone in this. In 2011 General Peter Chiarelli, then the US Army’s vice chief of staff, called for dropping “disorder”, and in 2012 the psychiatrists Frank Ochberg and Jonathan Shay asked the American Psychiatric Association to rename the condition post-traumatic stress injury. The association debated it that May and decided against a change (PBS NewsHour, 2012). It is still a “disorder” in the DSM-5-TR (2022) and in the World Health Organization’s fact sheet. In Canada, though, veterans’ services use a non-diagnostic umbrella term, “operational stress injury”, for any persistent psychological difficulty that results from operational duties. In a 2023 survey run by a clinic that campaigns for the change, over two-thirds of 1,025 respondents said “injury” would reduce stigma.

5

Before the battlefield

What sets it off isn’t always what caused it

We talk about combat as the cause of post-traumatic stress, the class goes on, but research tells a different story. Many people who enlist are already carrying something before they see a battlefield: broken homes, childhood trauma, unresolved pain, grief. The military often draws in exactly that profile. Yale researchers found that younger veterans are three times as likely to develop post-traumatic stress as older ones, and that 90% of that gap comes down to things like childhood trauma, loneliness and strained relationships — not the combat itself. Combat is often what sets it off; a lot of the time, the pain was there long before.

Harry recognises that. The trauma could be pushed to one side — but Harry was a young kid, “adrenalised” since the age of twelve or thirteen, and it was never going to be suppressed to the point where it simply vanished.

Nor is it only a military problem. Post-traumatic stress shows up in civilians at nearly the same rate as in veterans, the class says. We think of war as what causes PTSD, O’Connor adds, but motor-vehicle accidents are a huge cause — something nearly everyone comes close to, by being in one or by witnessing one. Being in one is very traumatic, and witnessing alone can be traumatic too.

Beyond the lectureThe Yale study is Hathorn and colleagues (2025), from the National Health and Resilience in Veterans Study of 4,069 US veterans. 14.7% of those under 50 screened positive for PTSD, against 4.9% of those aged 50 and over. About 90% of that gap was accounted for by other factors: general psychosocial difficulties (43%), loneliness (24%), avoidant coping (10%), childhood adversity (9.5%) and, smallest, how severe their combat had been (4%).

The point about enlistment has support too. Blosnich and colleagues (2014) surveyed 60,598 US adults and found that men who served in the all-volunteer era reported more of all eleven kinds of adverse childhood experience measured than civilian men — “preliminary support that enlistment may serve as an escape from adversity for some individuals”.

On civilians, the US National Center for PTSD puts lifetime PTSD at about 7% of veterans and 6% of all US adults, though it is far higher in some groups — 29% of veterans of the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan. And in a classic survey of 1,000 adults, Norris (1992) found that motor-vehicle crashes had “the most adverse combination of frequency and impact” of the traumas studied.

Watch forThe class rounds the Yale numbers against combat. “Three times as likely” is right (14.7% against 4.9%), but it measured a current positive screen at one point in time, not people developing PTSD. And the 90% isn’t mostly childhood trauma: the biggest shares were general psychosocial difficulties and loneliness, childhood adversity was under a tenth, and combat was in the mix too — about 4%, small but not zero.

6

Panic, not fear

Is losing someone like witnessing something terrible?

Harry asks the question many people will have: do the mind and body react differently to losing a loved one than to a traumatic experience? The research is working very hard on that right now, O’Connor says, and we don’t know the answer. But O’Connor thinks different neural systems are involved:

Separation and loss

  • Feels much more like panic.
  • The neuroscientist Jaak Panksepp showed that in animals you can see a panic response tied to separation from a loved one.

Witnessing trauma

  • Feels more like fear.
  • A response to danger, rather than to someone missing.

O’Connor gives an example from closer to home. The very first time O’Connor stayed overnight alone, a massive panic attack followed. People assumed it was fear of someone breaking in — which would make sense if it were fear. It wasn’t: O’Connor doesn’t think the door was even locked. It was the separation, the abandonment: being alone in that house was terrifying. And that may actually be a separate system in the brain. (It’s the same “protest” panic as the toddler lost in the supermarket in part 1, move 10.)

Beyond the lectureJaak Panksepp mapped seven basic emotional systems deep in the mammalian brain, and two of them match O’Connor’s split: FEAR, and a separate PANIC system — later “PANIC/GRIEF” — of separation distress, which makes young animals cry out when they lose contact with those who care for them (Affective Neuroscience, 1998, chapter 14). Panksepp’s team found that small doses of opioids quieted the distress cries of isolated puppies, and the same chapter names oxytocin and prolactin as calming it too — the chemistry O’Connor comes back to with crying, in move 11.

7

Nine hours on the ice

Walking to the poles, and rebooting the hard drive

In 2011 Harry joined a team of severely injured veterans heading for the North Pole, helping them haul their sledges over snow hills, ridges and crevasses. In 2013 came the South Pole, again to support the veterans and share the experience with them. Every single day meant marching for nine hours. No distractions, no signs, no traffic, no birds or wildlife — nothing. The only thing to focus on was putting one foot in front of the other, consistently, for nine hours.

What Harry didn’t expect to get from the trip was a moving therapy. Thoughts, memories and ideas would come up and cycle through, and Harry could deal with things that hadn’t been dealt with for many years — and then drop them and move on.

Harry’s description is a computer’s: the hard drive was taken out, dusted off, almost changed, and rebooted. All the stuff that had been stuck, that Harry had been unable to process over the years, cleared — and for two or three weeks after getting back to the UK, the calm and the clarity stayed.

Here’s the good news, the class adds: you don’t need to go to the South Pole to experience moving meditation.

Beyond the lectureBoth were Walking With The Wounded expeditions. In April 2011 Harry trained with the North Pole team in Svalbard and walked with them for five days, then left for military training; the team, four wounded soldiers among them, reached the Pole on 16 April (Ministry of Defence). In December 2013 Harry went the whole way with the British team of the South Pole Allied Challenge — planned as a race against American and Commonwealth teams until the race was dropped for safety — and reached the Pole on 13 December after about 200 miles, roughly two weeks on the ice (AFP report).

8

Flow

When attention has one place to go

So what is going on in the brain when someone reaches the kind of flow state Harry found in Antarctica? The psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi studied flow in artists, musicians and athletes. When a situation needs all your attention for whatever comes next, you can enter a flow state, and O’Connor lists what it gives us:

  • A physical discharge for all the adrenaline that’s pumping.
  • A sense of mastery over your environment: I am doing the thing I set out to do.
  • Room for thoughts without suppressing them. Because attention has a focus, thoughts can come to you, simply be true, and pass away again. You don’t have to do anything with them; they release on their own, and that brings a sense of peace.

Some activities make you focus on the one thing you’re doing. That’s why joining a choir is so good: you’re with other people, all directing your attention to exactly the next note that needs to come out of you. Artists describe the same with sculpting or painting — focusing entirely on it lets the mind relax, so thoughts can come and go without being controlled.

Beyond the lectureCsikszentmihalyi’s research began with a study of painters absorbed in their work. Beyond Boredom and Anxiety (1975) added chess players, rock climbers, dancers and surgeons, and later studies paged ordinary people at random moments of the day to ask what they were doing and how it felt (University of Chicago Magazine). The book that made the idea famous is Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience (1990). Flow tends to come when a task’s challenge roughly matches your skill and it gives clear, immediate feedback — the next step on the ice, the next note in the choir.

Samu and the snow globe

Meditation in the middle of the washing-up

The lesson’s tool for ordinary days — one its speaker uses almost every day — draws directly on the Buddhist practice of samu, or work practice: meditation in the midst of activity. Here’s how it works:

  1. When you’re doing something routine — washing dishes, drying glasses, cleaning — don’t let the mind wander off into grief or rumination.
  2. Bring your full attention to the physical sensations of what’s in front of you. Feel the water. Notice the movements of your hands.
  3. When the mind wanders — and it will — simply return to that present sensory experience. Don’t judge yourself; just redirect.

That’s a way into the same flow that musicians, athletes and artists describe. And for many people, especially in grief, it’s much easier than formal sitting meditation, because it anchors attention in something concrete and physical rather than asking the mind to find stillness on its own.

The image to keep: think of the mental loops we get stuck in as a snow globe. Every time you shake it, you restart the storm. The answer isn’t to stop the storm; it’s to put the whole snow globe down.

Try it — samu, with a snow globe

Take this to the sink, or to any routine job. Pick a length and start: the globe begins shaken up, and a cue appears every thirty seconds to bring your attention back to your hands. Press “My mind wandered” whenever you notice you’ve drifted — each return is the practice. Press “Shake it” to see what a loop of rumination does.

Pick a job, and a length.

Beyond the lectureSamu (作務) is the Japanese Zen word for work done as practice: in Zen monasteries, cleaning, cooking and gardening are part of the training, not a break from it. One Zen community puts it that such work “points as directly to the mind as does zazen”, sitting meditation (Diamond Sangha; see also the Soto Zen glossary).

There’s a small experiment on exactly this. In Hanley and colleagues (2015) — the title borrows a line from the Zen teacher Thich Nhat Hanh — 51 students read either a short passage on washing dishes mindfully or a plain description of dishwashing, and then washed dishes. The mindful group came away more mindful, more inspired and less nervous (about a quarter each way, by Florida State University’s account), and they overestimated how long they had spent at the sink.

Drill — one job, every day

Pick one routine job you do daily — the dishes, the coffee, the shower — and make it your samu for a week. Each time, notice three physical sensations before the mind runs off, and count your returns rather than your wanderings.

Relaxing the beliefs

“Rethink Psychedelic Therapy, Crying and Coping”

The second lesson of “Upgrade Your System” starts with psychedelics. Having seen how psychedelics are helping veterans and others who are suffering, Harry is excited that support for the research is gaining traction across party lines in the US. The VA, Harry says, is running trials at nine facilities — something not done since the 1960s — and the VFW, Heroic Hearts and the Wounded Warrior Project are all backing legislation to expand the research further.

For Harry, the biggest benefit of these newer therapies is that they open up your emotional capacity. There are medicines that can assist with that, and the two together — therapy and medicine — can be transformational. Harry recalls a US trial some five or six years ago with, Harry thinks, 28 veterans, after which some were diagnosed free of post-traumatic stress; some of them had tried therapy, and it “wasn’t their thing”. Combining conventional therapy with these medicines breaks down walls and barriers, so you can open up and go deeper than you’d ever dare. To Harry, psychedelic-assisted therapy is no longer a fringe idea: it’s becoming mainstream.

O’Connor calls it a powerful medicine for the right context, and explains a theory of how it works from Robin Carhart-Harris and Karl Friston, called REBUS — relaxed beliefs under psychedelics:

  • The brain is a prediction machine, always trying to predict the world and work out how to get through the day using the least energy.
  • That leads to really rigid beliefs.
  • With the medicine, psychedelic-assisted therapy can biochemically relax some of those connections. You’ll recognise the reports of smelling the music or hearing a feeling: psychedelics connect parts of the brain that wouldn’t ordinarily connect — a chance to think about grief, and the death of a loved one, in a new way, and about how to move forward without the automatic, rigid beliefs we’ve held.

“Gives you the bigger picture,” Harry says. Yes, O’Connor agrees: it makes you aware that your self, your way of viewing the world, isn’t the most important thing in that moment. Harry has heard it explained as a snow shower: snow comes in and covers the old tracks, and then new tracks are made. Exactly, O’Connor says — and it’s doing that at a physiological, biochemical level.

The fear is that people will use psychedelics of any kind recreationally, at a vulnerable moment, where what they experience could lead them somewhere bad. There are real risks, O’Connor says, and psychedelic-assisted therapy has worked out ways to deal with some of them: doing this in a supportive environment.

Beyond the lectureWhere things stand, as of October 2026. In December 2024 the US Department of Veterans Affairs funded its first psychedelic study since the 1960s: MDMA-assisted therapy for veterans with PTSD and alcohol use disorder. By late 2025 VA trials were running at nine sites, mostly with outside funding, and by August 2026 the VA said it was involved in about twenty, including a psilocybin trial for treatment-resistant depression. The VFW and the Wounded Warrior Project back a bipartisan bill, the Innovative Therapies Centers of Excellence Act, which would set up VA centres for these treatments; Heroic Hearts backs a different bill, the Breakthrough Therapies Act. Both bills were still in committee.

The trial Harry half-remembers is probably Mithoefer and colleagues (2018): 26 people — 22 veterans, three firefighters and a police officer — given MDMA-assisted therapy for chronic PTSD. Symptom scores fell by 58 and 44 points on the two active doses, against 11 on a low dose. The theory O’Connor describes is Carhart-Harris and Friston’s “REBUS and the Anarchic Brain” (2019). The snow image comes from Mendel Kaelen, then at Imperial College London’s psychedelic research group: the brain as a snowy hillside, thoughts as sleds that wear trails into it, and psychedelics as fresh snow that flattens the tracks for a while (quoted in Michael Pollan’s How to Change Your Mind, 2018).

Watch for“Becoming mainstream” is ahead of the regulators. In August 2024 the US Food and Drug Administration declined to approve MDMA-assisted therapy and asked for another trial, after its advisory committee voted 9 to 2 that the evidence didn’t show it worked. A psilocybin treatment is now under review, but as of October 2026 no psychedelic is approved for PTSD or for grief, and outside trials and a few US states’ licensed programmes these drugs are illegal. They can also be dangerous for some people — one reason trials screen out anyone with a personal or family history of psychosis, and why O’Connor stresses a supportive setting. None of it is a case for self-medicating.

11

Different tears

Crying as the brain’s own way of updating

Does the brain have the same, or similar, mechanisms of its own, Harry asks? O’Connor believes there is something we do naturally in grieving that can help us gain insight and change the neurochemistry of the brain: crying.

It may shock you, O’Connor says, that there are zero studies of crying in bereaved people — which is ridiculous, and says a lot about how avoidant of crying our society is. But we do know that emotional tears contain hormones — prolactin and opioids — while the irritant tears you get from chopping an onion don’t. It’s a different function. And O’Connor’s belief, in O’Connor’s words “untested”, is that it’s like falling in love or nursing an infant, when those hormones flood the brain: crying may be how the brain makes the connection — updates and understands the reality of the loss.

As someone who doesn’t cry often, and struggled to cry for so many years, Harry says, it feels amazing to cry. Our emotions are encoded in the brain, O’Connor replies, and our relationships in the hormonal patterns invoked in us when we’re with our loved ones. O’Connor believes bereavement is a hormonal event in the same way that falling in love is — sad and very difficult, but it explains how the relationship has changed in a physiological way, not only an emotional one.

The key point? Don’t go looking to cry by cutting onions: you’ll cry a different kind of tears. (Crying as a pressure valve was Harry’s theme in part 1, move 2.)

Watch forBoth of the class’s crying facts are overstated, as O’Connor half-signals by calling the idea untested. “Zero studies” isn’t quite right: there are a few interview studies, such as one of soldiers who had lost a comrade in combat, though no controlled or physiological study of crying in bereaved people turned up. The tear chemistry rests on thin ground. The classic study, Frey and colleagues (1981), found only that emotional tears carried about 24% more protein than onion tears; the idea that tears carry prolactin and other stress chemicals was William Frey’s later hypothesis. A 2014 review from Ad Vingerhoets’ group says the evidence “seriously challenges” it, and treats opioids as a possible reason crying soothes — something in the brain, not something measured in tears — and a 2022 study found no prolactin in tears at all. A 2011 experiment in which men who sniffed women’s emotional tears showed lower arousal and testosterone is still argued over after a failed replication.

12

Two ways to grieve

Do men and women grieve differently?

The research is extremely mixed on this, O’Connor says. It’s clear that our emotions are gendered: boys are taught from an early age not to cry, that it’s unacceptable. Men are often trying to restore a life for themselves, which is an important part of grieving — it becomes a problem when it’s done to the exclusion of dealing with the emotions and changes of the loss.

What O’Connor sometimes sees, in a heterosexual couple for instance, is one person reacting one way and the other person differently. The one who is crying looks at the other and says: why aren’t you crying — aren’t you feeling grief? But the expression of grief can look very different.

The example from O’Connor’s own life: the morning after O’Connor’s father died, O’Connor woke to find that O’Connor’s sister had made centrepieces for every table at the memorial — beautiful flower arrangements. “I haven’t cried,” the sister said. O’Connor’s answer: “This is the expression of your grief.”

Beyond the lectureThe grief researchers Terry Martin and Kenneth Doka describe two grieving styles, on a continuum rather than split by sex. Intuitive grievers feel and show grief as emotion; instrumental grievers work through it by thinking and doing — arranging, building, fixing, making centrepieces. Most people blend the two, and the styles are linked to gender without being decided by it (Men Don’t Cry… Women Do, 2000; revised as Grieving Beyond Gender, 2010). Where average differences between the sexes do appear, it is often in health: across studies, bereavement’s toll on illness and death is relatively greater for widowers than for widows in the early months (Stroebe, Stroebe and Schut, 2001).

13

Grief literacy

When therapy isn’t within reach

Therapy is expensive and not accessible to everybody, Harry points out — so what other tools are out there?

There’s now a movement in bereavement research towards a public-health approach to bereavement, O’Connor says. There’s no way that everyone who loses a loved one could do psychotherapy, and it isn’t necessary. What we need is better grief literacy. Many bereavement support organisations exist, built on the idea that simply being together with people who have lived experience of grief — sharing your own experience, and getting to hear others’ — can be very healing in and of itself.

Beyond the lectureThe public-health model comes largely from Australia. It sorts bereaved people into three tiers: most need only information and the support of family and friends, some benefit from community and peer support, and a few need specialist help. When Aoun and colleagues (2015) tested it on 678 bereaved Australians, 58% were at low risk, 35% at moderate risk and 6% at high risk — close to the model’s predicted 60, 30 and 10. Grief literacy is the name Breen and colleagues gave to the knowledge and skills a community needs to support people through loss, in their call for “compassionate communities”.

14

Two lists, and a bigger life

The dual process model, and oscillating between the lists

A really helpful model, which a lot of clinicians use and O’Connor finds very helpful in understanding O’Connor’s own life, is the dual process model of Maggie Stroebe and Henk Schut. Many of us think only about the loss itself; Stroebe and Schut pointed out that bereavement brings two kinds of stressor:

Loss stressors

  • The emotions and the overwhelm.
  • The looping, endless thoughts.
  • The physical feelings.

Restoration stressors

  • Doing the taxes, if your partner always did the taxes.
  • Parenting a child you always thought you’d raise together.
  • What retirement will look like; whether to sell the house — restoring a meaningful life for yourself.

Here’s the kicker: mental health is being able to oscillate — to move back and forth between the loss stressors and the restoration stressors. In the morning you might be going through the closet, overwhelmed, deciding which of your loved one’s clothes to give away and which to keep. In the afternoon you might be at your kid’s soccer game, out in the world doing the things you need to do to go on, putting one foot in front of the other like we all have to.

Those two lists won’t necessarily get smaller. But we know that life gets bigger around them. Grief can still be with you; it doesn’t have to be in the driver’s seat.

Try it — life grows around grief

Drag through time. The dark circle is the grief: it stays the same size. The outer circle is the rest of life. The times are only for illustration — grief keeps no schedule.

Beyond the lectureThe model is Stroebe and Schut’s dual process model (1999), which part 1 met briefly in move 3. Its other key idea is dosage: taking breaks from grieving — distraction, rest, an afternoon at the soccer game — is part of healthy coping, not a failure of it. The picture of life growing around grief comes from the grief counsellor Lois Tonkin, who described it in 1996 after a client whose child had died drew it: the grief stayed the same size while life grew around it (Tonkin, Bereavement Care, 1996; Cruse Bereavement Support).

Drill — write both lists

Write your own two lists for this week: loss tasks (a box to sort, a call to make, a place to visit) and restoration tasks (a form, a skill, a plan, an outing). Put one from each list on the same day, and notice the swing between them — the swing is the point.

Part 2 in eight lines:

1

Exposure, with intention

A scent can unlock what was walled off. In grief, exposure means facing that they are gone — and that you will be okay.

2

Name it

Permission to grieve, and naming the loss, start recovery; therapy cleans the filters on the windscreen.

3

Injury, not disorder

The word you use can tell you whether you can heal. Use the one that works for the person.

4

Panic, not fear

Losing someone may run on a separation system — panic — rather than the fear system of trauma.

5

Move, and pay attention

Walking, singing, sculpting or washing up: when attention has one place to go, thoughts come and go. Put the snow globe down.

6

Relaxed beliefs

Psychedelic-assisted therapy may loosen rigid predictions — in the right setting, with real risks.

7

Different tears

Crying may be how the brain updates to a loss — and grief has more than one way of showing.

8

Oscillate

Move between the loss and rebuilding a life. The grief doesn’t shrink; life grows bigger around it.
