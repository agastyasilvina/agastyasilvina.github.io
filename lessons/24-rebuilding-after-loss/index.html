1

One load-bearing wall

“Rebuild Who You’re Becoming” begins: an identity with more than one support

The class’s fourth heading opens with an image. Imagine your whole life is built around one load-bearing wall. The house is solid for now — but if that wall ever goes, everything else goes with it. The work of building a resilient identity is adding more walls, to give yourself a more solid foundation. And when old identities fall away, something better can take their place.

Harry went into the army in Harry’s early twenties really without an identity — or rather, with the one other people supplied: “the prince that lost his mom at the age of 12”. So wearing a uniform, the same uniform as everybody else, was not only a relief: it gave Harry the purpose and grounding needed at the time. It’s the same, Harry thinks, for everyone who joins the military. You learn things about each other you would never reveal in any other circumstance, and you are tested within an inch of your life — training that, Harry says, has equipped Harry for the rest of life.

So does everyone need military training — to be broken in order to be made again? No, Harry says. You all have experiences that will break you, moments when you’ll feel broken, and those moments, collectively, are giving you more resilience than you ever had before.

Having an identity connected to multiple things is the best way to live. If you lose your job and your identity goes with it, what are you left with? Without an identity, you can end up in a very dark place, with the wrong people. Life’s losses aren’t only of someone — a family member, a friend; they’re also losses of identity. Is there research behind that, Harry asks?

Beyond the lecturePsychology has a name for the “more walls” idea: self-complexity. Patricia Linville (1987) found that people whose sense of self had more distinct parts went through less depression, stress and illness after a stressful stretch, as if the other parts cushioned the blow. Later work is more mixed: a 2002 review of the research found “little support” for self-complexity as a stress buffer in general — but grief research has come back to it, as move 6 shows.

2

The “we” in the brain

Why losing a role can feel like losing a person

“Yes, absolutely,” O’Connor says. We evolved to treat the death of someone close in a very special way in the brain, because of the way the brain encodes a “we”. It encodes you, and me — and then it encodes this “we”. The psychologist Arthur Aron calls it the inclusion of others in the self. Think of circles: if I ask how close you are to your best friend, you might draw two circles next to each other, but overlapping. How big is the “we” portion of your connection?

Think of the words son, daughter, spouse, best friend. They describe me — but they imply two people in the world: “daughter” suggests there is another person. So O’Connor thinks the brain understands other losses as a loss of a sense of ourselves, too. If O’Connor retires, being a professor is a big part of who O’Connor is and what O’Connor does every day; leaving the military changed everything about Harry’s day-to-day life. That kind of loss is like the one we face after a death: how do I be a good daughter in the world if my mother has died? If my child has died, am I a parent?

We wrestle with those questions of identity after the death of someone close — but also after a divorce, after our children fly the coop, after a loss of ability, such as an accident in which we lose a limb or our sight. In each, the loss changes something fundamental about who we are and how we operate in the world.

Try it — how much “we” is there?

Think of one person — or a role, like your job. Which picture best describes the relationship? The pictures are drawn after the seven in Aron’s scale: one circle for you, one for them.

youthem

Pick a picture.

Beyond the lectureAron’s scale is the Inclusion of Other in the Self Scale (Aron, Aron and Smollan, 1992): a single question showing seven pairs of circles, labelled “self” and “other”, from just touching to almost completely overlapping, and you pick the pair that best describes the relationship. The idea that a loss shakes your sense of who you are has been tested on break-ups too. In three studies by Slotter, Gardner and Finkel (2010), a break-up changed what people believed about themselves and left their sense of self less clear — and that loss of clarity predicted how distressed they were.

3

Do it first

A concert, and a set of purple towels

How can someone start to rebuild an identity if they don’t really know who they are any more? O’Connor’s answer may be surprising: you have to start doing things first, and your identity comes along later. You might think: what do I want to do this weekend? Nothing — I want to stay at home. But I saw this concert, so I’m going to go. You do it first, and then you notice: gosh, I really enjoyed that; I should do it again. It’s only by doing things — getting up off the couch and out into the world — that we discover what we like and don’t like, what we’re good at, what feels meaningful, and why we connect with people.

“What brings you joy,” Harry adds: find the motivation to go out and start trying things. And this is the tricky bit, O’Connor says: you have to go out and do it, and the motivation comes later. It’s also about trying new things, and seeing what you like and don’t.

After O’Connor’s mother died, O’Connor and O’Connor’s father went shopping, and the father needed new bathroom towels. “Great,” said O’Connor, “we’ll get yellow towels.” No, came the answer: purple towels. “Dad, you don’t want purple towels — they’re not going to match the bathroom!” Then O’Connor realised the father was saying something about who the father had become since the death. It turned out the father had always hated yellow; nobody knew, because it hadn’t mattered then. The bathroom was repainted purple, and it was beautiful — so lovely to see who the father genuinely was inside, and the father had taken the risk of buying purple towels.

Deep purple, Harry asks, or lavender? Deep purple. “He was not messing around.” That, Harry agrees, is bold.

Beyond the lecture“Act first, and the feeling follows” is the core of behavioural activation, a therapy first developed for depression that works “from the outside in” (Kanter, Baruch and Gaynor, 2006): you plan and do things that matter to you whatever your mood, rather than waiting to feel like it. It has been tried for grief too. In a small trial by Papa and colleagues (2013), about twelve weeks of behavioural activation brought large drops in prolonged grief, PTSD and depression symptoms compared with waiting, and 70% of those who finished it responded.

Drill — one experiment this week

Pick one thing you’d normally skip — a concert, a class, a walk somewhere new, a colour you’d never buy. Do it before you feel like it, then write one line: did I enjoy it, and would I do it again? Each yes is a brick for a new wall.

4

Service, and a glass jar

Purpose, protection, and “No, I can do this”

In the military, Harry was so connected and attached to that identity that leaving could have been very hard — but Harry was fortunate to have another identity to fall back on. Both were rooted in service, and service to others is, for Harry, a seriously energising thing.

Purpose and meaning are a strong force in Harry’s life, and Harry encourages everybody to find theirs if they don’t have it: find your people, the ones you can function with, and find a cause larger than yourself, “because you will find drive and energy and commitment like you have never discovered before”.

One visualisation Harry’s therapist offered is to imagine being inside a glass jar. All the negativity, everything you don’t want to let in, bounces off the sides. The lid is open, and all the good stuff comes in. You are in control: you decide what comes in over the top.

And you learn resilience pretty quickly, Harry says, when you’re asked to do things you simply know you can’t do. Once you’ve done them, each time gets easier — and every time your state of mind shifts from “I can’t” to “No, I can do this”, you build your capacity to be resilient to whatever else comes your way.

Beyond the lectureHarry’s two service identities: ten years in the army, from Sandhurst in 2005 to leaving in June 2015, with tours of Afghanistan in 2007–08 and 2012–13; and the Invictus Games, the international games for wounded, injured and sick service members and veterans that Harry launched in 2014, first held in London that September with over 400 competitors from 13 nations.

5

Post-traumatic growth

Growth that sits beside grief, not instead of it

Harry has heard a lot about post-traumatic growth and sees it regularly: people who have managed to grow from their injuries. It’s extraordinary, because what you don’t want is to be in the place of saying “because of my injuries, I am now this”. Having seen so many people take pride in their growth, Harry quotes Rumi: “The wound is the place where the light enters you.” It made Harry think of a whole community of people — of the scar on the skin from something many years ago. The pain may have gone, but it tells a story, and you have inevitably grown from that story.

Post-traumatic growth is something psychology can measure, O’Connor says, and a challenge to talk about, because it’s not something you can force. We would never want those wounds to happen. But given that they have, and that people have taken on the difficult work of going on, many describe having learned something:

  • “Gosh, I’m stronger than I realised I was.”
  • “Life is so short — carpe diem, we have to seize the day.”
  • “I feel so much empathy for others now, because I’ve had this experience.”

That is what researchers call post-traumatic growth. It can emerge after a loss or a tragedy when people have support around them to learn those lessons — but not in a forced way. And research shows you don’t get one or the other: growth isn’t instead of grief. In fact, it’s often the people with the most grief, or even the most post-traumatic stress, who also show the most growth. It isn’t easy to put a finger on, but it is often the lifeblood after a tragedy.

Harry has never heard anyone say “I’ve achieved post-traumatic growth”. It’s more of a reflection — something a group of people can recognise in what someone has achieved.

Beyond the lectureThe psychologists Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun named and measured it. Their Posttraumatic Growth Inventory (1996) covers five areas — new possibilities, relating to others, personal strength, spiritual change and appreciation of life — and O’Connor’s three examples sit in three of them. In bereavement, growth and grief do come together: in a study of 412 bereaved adults (Eisma and colleagues, 2019) the two were linked, but neither predicted the other over six months, which led the authors to call treatments aimed at boosting growth “premature” — the research version of “you can’t force it”.

Watch forTwo things here are looser than they sound. First, “the most grief, the most growth”: across 42 studies (Shakespeare-Finch and Lurie-Beck, 2014), growth rose with distress up to a point and then fell away at the highest levels, and the bereavement study above found the same upside-down U. So moderate distress, not the most, tends to go with the most growth. Second, the Rumi line is a modern paraphrase. It condenses Coleman Barks’s free rendering of a passage in the Masnavi (“Keep looking at the bandaged place. That’s where the light enters you”), and in a literal translation the healing light comes from the healer’s salve on the wound, not from the wound itself (Masnavi, book I, around verse 3225).

6

Twenty “I am”s

Rebuilding a self, one line at a time

One of the surprising difficulties of grieving is that we have to rebuild a sense of who we are in the world: what we like and dislike, what we do day to day. If you’ve been caregiving for a long time, your whole day has been wrapped up in doctors’ appointments, monitoring symptoms and caring for this person — and suddenly all of it is ripped away. Quite literally: what are you doing with your day? All of that has to be rebuilt.

The grief researchers Bellet and McNally studied this with a really simple task: write twenty statements that start with “I am”. O’Connor’s might begin: I’m a woman, I’m a sister, I’m a professor, and I like to knit. What was fascinating was that people who were really struggling with grieving struggled with this task: they couldn’t necessarily work out who they were now.

You don’t have to be in a study to use it. Stop right now and write “I am” twenty times — you might surprise yourself with what comes out. Work out what your life looks like, what’s important to you, and what you want to spend your time doing. Then save the list, and do it again in six months, and see how the lists differ: grieving is really a form of learning, and you may see change over time.

Try it — twenty “I am”s

Write as fast as the lines come, and don’t edit. Save the list, then come back in six months, write a new one, and compare: lines that are new since the list before show in bold. Everything stays in this browser on this device; nothing is sent anywhere.

Beyond the lectureThe study is Bellet and colleagues (2020), with Richard McNally, using the Twenty Statements Test devised by Kuhn and McPartland in 1954. Of 77 bereaved adults, the 38 with probable complicated grief wrote fewer “I am” statements than the rest (about 12 against 16) — but the bigger difference was variety. Their statements fell into fewer kinds (about 3.4 categories against 4.7), with fewer about activities, and that narrowness tracked how severe the grief was. The authors conclude that identity confusion in grief “may result from diminished self-complexity” — the one-load-bearing-wall problem of move 1.

Drive without adrenaline

From panic on public engagements to a ten-day rule

There was a time when public engagements created absolute panic for Harry, from beginning to end and across the whole spectrum. Putting on a suit jacket and tie and suddenly feeling suffocated. Getting from A to B, and out of the car in front of all the cameras and the clicks. Then another layer: going into the room, not knowing who would say what. The biggest anxiety of all was the fear of sweating — of people seeing Harry looking dishevelled, and of not being able to explain in one short sentence what was actually going on. Afterwards Harry would feel like Harry had just been in a boxing match.

Some public engagements still involve adrenaline, Harry admits, but it’s more appropriate adrenaline, right for the circumstance. And Harry has found the same drive that adrenaline gave without needing adrenaline. The biggest factor is the energy, joy and happiness of being with family, especially the children, and of being on the ground for the work — and the key to that balance is being no more than ten days away from Harry’s wife and kids. That’s the balance of work and its purpose with being a dad and a husband: the “multi-identity elements coming together”. (The superpower and the crash were part 1, moves 6 to 8.)

Harry remembers a big moment during Covid, around the second lockdown, Harry thinks: what was termed the Great Resignation, when people decided to take the risk of leaving a job they’d been unhappy in for years and start something new. It takes a lot of guts to leave a financially secure job because it doesn’t fit you. For Harry, it’s about what you can build with all the bricks that are thrown at you: what beautiful structure, what foundation you can stand on, built from all the experiences — the negativity, the bad breaks, whatever hurts — so that every experience, negative or positive, can get you where you want to go.

Beyond the lectureThe phrase “the Great Resignation” was coined by the organisational psychologist Anthony Klotz, then at Texas A&M University, in a Bloomberg Businessweek interview in May 2021: “The great resignation is coming” (Texas A&M). It named the wave of people quitting their jobs in the US through 2021 and 2022.

Carrying Diana

Shaped by the people who loved us

Everything Harry does, everything Harry puts energy into, Harry hopes Diana would be proud of. Trying to continue and protect Diana’s legacy, Harry didn’t realise at first that the work Harry chose — or was drawn and attracted to — was so similar to Diana’s own work.

With grief comes not so much a responsibility as a carrying of the person you’ve lost, throughout your life. Carrying Diana is what energises Harry for the work, and for many years now, especially the last six, Harry has felt “so much of my mother within me”.

Is there science behind that, Harry asks? O’Connor is “absolutely astonished” by it: because we loved this person, and because they loved us, that has shaped our neural architecture. They are encoded in the synapses of our brain. Is it nature or nurture, Harry wonders — and if you lose a parent in your early years, how many years did they need to be alive to have that influence? The research suggests to O’Connor that love encoded during a developmental phase is always with you: always part of that neural architecture. The way you see the world is shaped because you saw the world with your mother. It’s the way you parent now, the way you choose which projects to take on, the way you share a story or hear someone else share one. They are physically encoded in us, and that will never go away.

O’Connor knows it personally: O’Connor’s mother died in 1999, when O’Connor was 26. Still connected? “I feel so connected to her.” As O’Connor got older and greyer, suddenly the mother was there in the mirror — O’Connor had only ever known the mother with grey hair. At 26 O’Connor didn’t look much like the mother; now O’Connor sees that face every day, and some days says to the mirror: “this one’s for you. You wouldn’t believe what I’m doing. You would be so proud of me. You would have always wanted this for me.”

Beyond the lectureO’Connor has told the rest of the story in interviews: O’Connor’s mother was diagnosed with stage IV breast cancer when O’Connor was thirteen, and lived for thirteen more years (Huberman Lab, 2025). The science behind “they shaped our neural architecture” is attachment research — the bonds of early life shaping how we relate to others for good — and it runs through O’Connor’s books The Grieving Brain (2022) and The Grieving Body (2025).

Watch forRead “encoded in each synapse” as a figure of speech. Memories and attachments are spread across networks of neurons; there is no synapse per person. The point — that the people who raised and loved us shaped how our brains work, for good — doesn’t depend on it.

9

Continuing bonds

When Harry feels closest to Diana

When does Harry feel closest to Diana, O’Connor asks? It takes work, Harry says: it depends on how aware of Diana Harry is. A lot has happened in Harry’s life in the last six years, and Diana has “turned up” at moments where it made complete sense — fleeting moments that make so much sense they sometimes make Harry laugh out loud: this is not a coincidence; there’s no version of this where it’s anything but you. Harry feels it as an energy, and visually, as a sign. And when times are tough, Harry asks: “Hi, I need some help here — guide me in the right way.” Harry knows that with more work, the connection could come more often than in those fleeting moments.

These are continuing bonds, O’Connor says: still feeling you can ask your mother for advice. Of course you can. That is the transformation that comes with grieving when you give it attention: you find you can bring them with you into today. Not because you’re expecting to see them again, not because you still think they’re out there, but as advice-givers, and reminders of what’s important to you.

After an important person in your life dies, they are still with you, in the sense that you still have an internal relationship with them. What matters is that you can keep nurturing that relationship — it can continue to grow even though they’re gone. (Part 1’s “gone but also everlasting” is the brain science underneath: part 1, move 14.)

Beyond the lectureThe term comes from an edited book, Continuing Bonds: New Understandings of Grief (Klass, Silverman and Nickman, eds., 1996), which challenged the older advice to “let go”. Later research found the kind of bond matters. In a study of 502 bereaved adults (Field and Filanosky, 2010), internalised bonds — drawing on the person who died as a secure base, their values and example carried inside — went with personal growth, while externalised bonds — illusions or hallucinations of their presence — went with violent deaths and with feeling responsible for the death. A 2014 review concluded that whether a bond helps depends on whether it feels comforting or distressing, on the relationship before the death, and on beliefs about an afterlife.

A letter you don’t send

Moving it from your head onto the page

The lesson’s tool comes from evidence-based cognitive behavioural therapy for grief, developed by the researchers Maja O’Connor and Paul Boelen: write a letter to the person, or the identity, you’ve lost — or to the version of yourself who was there when the loss happened. The instructions are simple. It’s about moving it from inside your head to outside, onto the page:

  • Tell them what you wish you’d said, and what you want them to know about who you’ve become.
  • What you’re sorry for, and what you’re grateful for.
  • You could ask for forgiveness, or you could forgive them.
  • You can tell them you love them — and, perhaps most importantly, you can say goodbye.

What’s fascinating, the class says, is that writing the letter still activates the same pathways the relationship would. The point isn’t so much to send it as to have the experience: imagining both offering something and how it would be received. It has real healing power for the mind.

Try it — a letter you don’t send

Write to the person or the identity you lost, or to who you were when it happened. Tap a prompt to start a new paragraph with it. The letter stays in this browser, on this device, until you clear it; nothing is sent anywhere. Untick the box if you’d rather it vanished when you leave.

Beyond the lectureThe letter comes from a treatment manual by Paul Boelen (Utrecht) and Maja O’Connor (Aarhus), Cognitive Therapy for Transdiagnostic Complicated Grief (2025): twelve sessions — exposure to the loss first, then cognitive work on trust in oneself, other people and the future, then rebuilding activities and goals. In the exposure phase clients keep “an ongoing farewell letter to the deceased”, writing for half an hour at least four times a week; the therapist reads it only if invited, and one suggestion is to read it aloud to an empty chair or a photograph. In a 2026 trial with 113 bereaved people aged 65 and over, the therapy cut grief symptoms sharply, in groups as well as one to one.

Writing seems to help most when it’s structured. Free “write about your feelings” exercises didn’t help widowed people in two studies by Stroebe and colleagues (2002). Guided writing has done better: an internet-based therapy (Wagner, Knaevelsrud and Maercker, 2006) built on ten writing tasks, ending in a letter, had large effects on complicated grief; and in Lichtenthal and Cruess (2010), bereaved students who wrote about their loss had fewer grief symptoms three months later than those who wrote about neutral topics, though the group was small and not very distressed.

Watch forThat the letter “still activates the same pathways the relationship would” is the class’s claim; no study showing it turned up. What the trials show is that the letter, as part of structured therapy, helps — not how it works in the brain.

11

Every night, most mornings

Photographs, mirrors, and a normal experience

Does Harry have a ritual, a point in the day for connecting with Diana? Pretty much every night, and most mornings — which is interesting, Harry says, because as a child, even with photographs in the bedroom at school, Harry distinctly remembers not thinking about Diana, because thinking about something that sad was never going to bring Diana back.

There is one photograph in which Harry can actually get eye contact with Diana, with a cheekiness in the smile and the eyes. Harry feels very connected, finds Harry wondering what Diana is thinking about what Harry is doing right now — and looks to see whether the expression changes. It never does.

O’Connor resonates with that. O’Connor’s photograph is from O’Connor’s parents’ wedding: they look so expectant and hopeful, and O’Connor thinks O’Connor looks like the mother in it. O’Connor never knew the mother at that age, but the photograph holds such hopes for what could be — “and I know that I am one of the things that she hoped there could be.” It makes O’Connor feel close, and also sad, still, to this day.

The mother died in 1999, and the fact that O’Connor still has grief now, O’Connor says, doesn’t mean there’s anything wrong. O’Connor hardly ever cries any more, but grief sticks with us. Someone so important brings the loss along with us, and it keeps motivating us — because when we talk about these things, other people understand that they are having a normal experience too.
