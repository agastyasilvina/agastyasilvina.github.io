Arc A · Groundwork

3 moves
1

Harnessing quanta

The chapter’s job, its two worries, and the bridge

The lecture opens by defining the job. Building a quantum computer means harnessing quanta — the discrete packets of energy that give quantum physics its name — so that they process information. The tools for that are qubits, which, unlike classical bits, use superposition.

Straight away it names two worries that run through the rest of the chapter:

  • Economics. For each way of building a quantum computer: what does it cost to build and to keep running, how much power does it need, and how common is it? (The lecture’s answers are in moves 18 and 19.)
  • Fragility. Quantum systems are like delicate instruments, thrown off by temperature changes, electrical fluctuations, even cosmic rays. That’s why quantum error correction is the safeguard the chapter keeps coming back to (moves 14, 20 and 21).

Then its first image, a bridge. A classical bridge is built to fixed, predictable laws, with supports at exact intervals. The quantum version, the lecture says, can exist in several configurations at once, its supports both present and absent, and instead of joining two banks it links many destinations. Three principles do this work: superposition, entanglement and interference. The lecture’s real point is practical: these aren’t theory decorating the machine. Every component in the lab — each path, gate and junction — exists to tap one of them.

Beyond the lecturePhysicists have a standard checklist for what “building one” has to deliver, set out by David DiVincenzo in 2000. It’s a useful lens for every platform in this chapter:

  1. Qubits that are well defined, and a way to have many of them.
  2. A way to start every qubit in a known state, such as all 0.
  3. Coherence that lasts much longer than one operation takes.
  4. A set of gates that can build any computation.
  5. A way to measure each qubit individually.

Keep the five in mind: when the lecture describes a laser, a fridge or a detector, it’s nearly always serving one of them.

Watch forThe opening says qubits “hold vast amounts of information and process extensive datasets simultaneously”. That’s the claim topic 17 took apart: n qubits carry 2n amplitudes but give up only n bits when read, and speed-ups come from interference, which this chapter names but never explains. See topic 17, move 6.

2

The quantum highway

Qubits as lanes, gates as traffic lights, operators as the rulebook

The chapter’s main metaphor arrives here: a quantum computer is a highway system, built in layers from bedrock to road surface, each layer with its own job.

  • Qubits are the lanes. They carry quantum information the way lanes carry traffic. A classical bit is a one-lane road where a car goes one way at a time, 0 or 1. A qubit is a multi-level highway where a car can float between levels in a combination of all its possible states. The lecture is careful to add that qubits are physically real objects, handled with magnetic fields, electric fields and lasers.
  • Gates are the traffic signals and junctions. They direct the flow, making qubits behave in specific ways. On paper they’re abstract, like the logic gates of a classical computer, but in the machine each one is a controlled interaction between qubits, and the kind of qubit decides how that interaction is made. So every gate has two faces: a line in an algorithm and a physical event.
  • Operators are the rulebook. The lecture sorts them by how many lanes they touch: unary for a single lane, binary for a junction of two, ternary for a three-way intersection. In hardware an operator becomes a precise sequence of laser pulses or finely tuned electromagnetic fields — for example, light of one exact frequency shone on two qubits so that they can “talk to each other”.

Beyond the lectureThe usual names: one-qubit gates (the X, Z and H of topic 17, move 3), two-qubit gates (such as CNOT, which makes entanglement), and three-qubit gates (such as the Toffoli, a controlled-controlled-NOT). A useful fact the lecture doesn’t mention: one- and two-qubit gates are enough to build any computation. Hardware therefore only ever does one- and two-qubit operations, and a three-way “intersection” is compiled into several two-lane ones — a Toffoli costs six CNOTs plus some single-qubit gates.

Watch forThe floating car “moving in many directions simultaneously” is the both-at-once picture again. A qubit does sit in a combination of states, but you can never drive every lane and collect the results: reading it gives one lane. See topic 17, move 1.

3

Circuits in the lab

Positioning, gating, and the photonic workbench

A quantum circuit, in the highway picture, is the planned road network. City planners lay out roads and junctions to keep traffic moving; circuit designers lay out gates to do one computation. And as a badly planned road makes jams, a badly designed circuit wastes effort.

The lecture then describes how circuits become real:

  • Positioning the qubits. Qubits must sit with pinpoint precision in a stable environment. For superconducting qubits especially, that means cryogenic chambers close to absolute zero.
  • Gating by laser. With trapped ions, a finely tuned laser pulse aimed at one ion changes its energy state or makes it interact with a neighbour.
  • Gating by electrical pulse. Superconducting qubits are steered by short, controlled bursts delivered through carefully designed wiring.
  • Gating by magnetic resonance. In nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) machines, magnetic fields act on the spins of atomic nuclei (move 5).

Then photonics, which the lecture pictures as high-speed light rail: photons are the vehicles, travelling on tracks of light. A photonic lab needs:

EquipmentIts job, in the lecture
Optical tableKills vibration, so lasers and optics stay rock-steady.
LasersMake single photons or entangled pairs; colour and pulse length are tuned to the state wanted.
Beam splitters, mirrorsSteer photons along their paths, like traffic lights for light.
Optical fibreThe dedicated track, carrying photons to where they’re needed.
Photon detectorsRead the photonic qubits at the end and turn them into data.

Its summary: building a quantum computer is more than assembling parts. Like the best highway systems, it has to work as one interconnected whole.

Beyond the lectureTwo things make the traffic metaphor more literal than the lecture lets on. First, most chips can only run a gate between qubits that are physically next to each other, so a circuit written for any pair must be “routed”: extra swap operations shuffle states around, and each one adds error — that’s the real traffic jam. Second, a 50/50 beam splitter acts on a photon exactly as the H gate acts on a qubit. Two beam splitters in a row make an interferometer in which the photon always leaves by the same exit: the H-then-H experiment from topic 17, move 4, done with light.

Watch forOptical fibre doesn’t deliver photons “without external interference”. Fibre loses light: over a hundred kilometres, the great majority of photons are simply absorbed or scattered away. For a classical signal you amplify. For a quantum one you can’t, which is why the quantum networks in move 14 need something cleverer.

The compass inside

Spin: the property most qubits are built from

Spin, the lecture begins, isn’t literal spinning, the way a top turns or the Earth rotates. It’s a built-in property of a particle, “like a fingerprint”. The picture it offers: a tiny magnetic compass inside every electron, proton and neutron, giving the particle an innate orientation that decides how it behaves in a magnetic field.

Put the particle in a strong external field and its spin either lines up with the field — spin up — or against it — spin down. The two orientations have different energies, and that difference, the lecture says, is one of the foundation stones of quantum computing. With magnets and magnetic-resonance equipment, researchers can detect spins and, by adjusting the strength and direction of the field, persuade them to flip. Read and write: that’s exactly what a memory and a processor need, which is why spin can be “the very core” of a quantum computer.

Beyond the lectureThe energy gap between up and down grows in step with the field, and it sets the frequency needed to flip the spin. For an electron in a one-tesla field that’s about 28 GHz — microwaves. For a proton (a hydrogen nucleus) it’s about 43 MHz — radio waves, because a nucleus is a far weaker magnet. That’s why electron-spin qubits are driven by microwaves and nuclear spins by radio pulses (move 5). And measuring a spin along any direction gives only one of two answers, which is what makes it a natural qubit: see Stern and Gerlach in topic 17, move 13.

Watch for“Either aligned or opposite” describes the two possible readings. Between readings a spin can be in any superposition of up and down — pointing, in effect, in any direction — and that’s what makes it a qubit rather than a switch.

5

NMR, the maestro

Nuclear magnetic resonance, and the first quantum computers

Nuclear magnetic resonance is the lecture’s maestro: a way to probe and play the spins of atomic nuclei. Picture an orchestra where each instrument is a nucleus with its own tone; NMR keeps them playing in harmony rather than as noise. In practice a sample sits in a powerful magnetic field, which makes its nuclei resonate at particular frequencies, and radio-frequency pulses then play those nuclei like notes.

The instrument is an NMR spectrometer, in four parts:

  1. The magnet — usually superconducting — makes a strong, even field. The stronger it is, the sharper the resonances, like a well-tuned instrument.
  2. The probe holds the sample and picks up the nuclei’s tiny magnetic responses.
  3. The radio-frequency transmitter sends short pulses that make chosen nuclei resonate.
  4. The receiver catches the signals the nuclei give off as they settle back.

For computing, each nucleus’s spin — with the field or against it — is a qubit. Radio pulses can put nuclei into superpositions and, with carefully built pulse sequences, entangle them. The lecture then revisits both ideas through spin (“spin’s dance”): a pulse nudges a nucleus into a balance of both states, which it credits with the parallelism behind quantum computing’s promise; entangled spins make one nucleus’s state depend on another’s; and sensitive detectors confirm whether the spins are in superposition or entangled. With the spectrometer, it concludes, we’re not reading about quantum principles but wielding them.

Beyond the lectureNMR really was where quantum computing started. Two groups proposed NMR quantum computers in 1997, the first quantum algorithms ran on them in 1998, and in 2001 IBM used seven nuclear spins in a single molecule to factor 15 with Shor’s algorithm (topic 17, move 11). Then the approach stalled, for a reason the lecture doesn’t give. An NMR sample is more than a billion billion molecules at room temperature, their spins almost entirely random, and the usable signal shrinks exponentially as you add qubits — roughly a dozen is the practical ceiling. It’s the same physics as a hospital MRI scanner, which is an NMR machine tuned to the hydrogen in your body.

Watch forEntanglement is the weak spot. In 1999 a group of physicists (Braunstein and colleagues) showed that the states in those room-temperature NMR experiments were so close to random that they couldn’t have been entangled at all. So the lecture’s detectors “confirming” entanglement in NMR are wishful. Its phrase “even if they’re spatially separated” also misleads: the nuclei are neighbours in one molecule, and entanglement doesn’t make one nucleus’s state depend on the other’s in any way that sends a signal (topic 17, move 7).

6

Spin at work

Five places spin is already doing a job

The lecture’s five concrete examples of spin in use:

  1. Quantum memory. The spin of an electron or nucleus can store a qubit — like classical memory, but able to hold a superposition. Keeping it usually needs ultra-cold conditions, from dilution refrigerators “colder than outer space”, with magnetic fields or focused lasers to write the spins.
  2. Spin-based logic gates. Flip an electron’s spin with a carefully calibrated magnetic field and you have a quantum NOT gate. The tools are superconducting magnets and precisely timed microwave pulses.
  3. Quantum cryptography. Quantum key distribution (QKD) measures entangled photons to create encryption keys the lecture calls “virtually unhackable”. The pairs come from nonlinear crystals pumped by a laser; superconducting detectors pick up the photons at the far end.
  4. Quantum sensors. Because spin states shift with tiny changes in magnetic field, they make exquisite sensors. SQUIDs — superconducting quantum interference devices — are extremely sensitive magnetometers used to pick up those shifts.
  5. Spintronics. Not quantum computers, but devices that use spin to store and move data: semiconductors engineered to respond to electron spin, ferromagnetic electrodes that inject or detect spins, and polarised light to read and write them.

Beyond the lectureTwo of these are already in your life. Spintronics is how hard drives got big: the “giant magnetoresistance” effect in their read heads won Albert Fert and Peter Grünberg the 2007 Nobel Prize in Physics, and MRAM memory chips use spin today. SQUIDs are built from the same Josephson junctions as the superconducting qubits in move 10, and they’re sensitive enough to measure the magnetic fields made by your brain. On cryptography: the most common QKD scheme, BB84 (topic 17, move 5), doesn’t use entanglement at all; the entangled-pair version is Artur Ekert’s, from 1991. And what’s measured on a photon is its polarisation, the photon’s version of spin.

Watch for“Virtually unhackable” is true of the maths, not of the machines. The QKD protocol is provably secure, but real devices have been hacked through their hardware — for example by blinding the detectors with bright light, demonstrated in 2010. Security agencies generally recommend post-quantum cryptography, ordinary software, over QKD.

The circuit model

Brick by brick: gates in sequence

Before the hardware, the lecture sorts quantum computers by model — the way the computation is organised — the way an architect might choose between a skyscraper, a bungalow and a cottage depending on purpose, terrain and materials. Each model has its own hardware, tools and methods.

The circuit model is the traditional blueprint. Operations run in a sequence, like the logic flow of a classical electrical circuit: a network of quantum gates, each acting on some qubits, adding up to a quantum circuit. Running one takes precise control — laser pulses, microwave bursts or magnetic interactions, depending on the qubit. For superconducting qubits the lab holds a dilution refrigerator, oscillators and pulse generators to time the interactions, and high-precision measuring equipment to read the results. It’s a step-by-step method, the lecture says, like laying one brick on another, each placed to keep the whole structure standing.

Beyond the lectureThis is the model everything in topic 17 used: start at |0⟩, apply gates such as H and CNOT, measure. It was formalised by David Deutsch in the 1980s, and it’s what almost every company building general-purpose machines runs today — superconducting, trapped-ion and neutral-atom alike. It’s also the one you can try for free: IBM put a small quantum computer on the cloud in 2016, and you can still write a circuit in a browser and run it on real hardware.

8

The one-way computer

Measurement-based computing: entangle everything first, then measure

The measurement-based model, also called the one-way quantum computer, is the lecture’s “twist”. If the circuit model is a blueprint, this one is a building made of prefabricated rooms, whose final shape and use depend on how you connect and enter them.

It starts by preparing a large, highly entangled state called a cluster state — a web of connected qubits, each holding possible paths for the computation. The computing is then done by measuring qubits one at a time, in a chosen order and a chosen way; as each is measured, the rest adjust. In the lab this needs a very accurately prepared cluster state and precise measuring tools. Photons are the favourite, because they’re easy to entangle and measure, so the key equipment is beam splitters to divide light paths and single-photon detectors, often cooled to cut noise, whose results steer the measurements that follow. The lecture calls it a dynamic puzzle: you start with a vast connected potential, and your choices during measurement sculpt the answer.

Beyond the lectureRobert Raussendorf and Hans Briegel proposed it in 2001. It’s called “one-way” because each measurement uses up part of the entanglement, so the computation can only run forwards. The detail that makes it work is the one the lecture calls feedback: each measurement is chosen according to the results of earlier ones (“feed-forward”). With that, it’s exactly as powerful as the circuit model, just organised differently. Photonic companies such as PsiQuantum build on a version of it, because photons are hard to make interact directly but easy to measure.

9

The adiabatic model

Slow and steady: let the answer settle out

The adiabatic model works by gradual change rather than steps. The lecture’s image is a bridge built by starting on one side of a chasm and slowly extending it until it reaches the other, the structure evolving with the landscape instead of being programmed move by move.

The system starts in a simple, well-understood state. An outside influence is then changed slowly, so slowly that the system stays in its lowest-energy state — its ground state — the whole way through. At the end, that ground state is the answer to the problem. The equipment:

  1. Magnetic fields and superconducting loops. The loops often serve as the qubits, and small changes in the fields guide the evolution.
  2. Control units that change the outside influence slowly and predictably.
  3. Cryogenic systems to keep noise down.
  4. Monitoring equipment to follow the state in real time.

The lecture closes the models with a quick look at two more — quantum cellular automata, which compute through local interactions on a grid, and the topological model, which uses braids in special materials (move 12) — and a reminder that the field is young and moving fast: researchers are like gold miners, chipping away in the hope of the next big find. Circuit, measurement or adiabatic, it says, each is a different way of harnessing the same quantum mechanics.

Beyond the lectureThe catch is in the word “slowly”. How slow depends on how close the lowest energy level comes to the next one during the change; if that gap shrinks very fast as the problem grows, the run time explodes, and for many hard problems it does. Edward Farhi and colleagues proposed the adiabatic algorithm around 2000, and in 2004 Dorit Aharonov and colleagues proved that an ideal adiabatic computer can do anything the circuit model can. The machines people actually buy are quantum annealers, D-Wave’s since 2011: a noisier, looser relative that uses the same superconducting loops (topic 17, move 14).

Watch forYou can’t monitor a quantum state “in real time” while it evolves. Watching is measuring, and measuring disturbs it (topic 17, move 2). An adiabatic run goes unobserved from start to finish; you read the qubits once, at the end. What labs monitor in real time is the classical equipment around the qubits — temperatures, currents, fields.

Superconducting circuits

The chandelier: electric currents with no resistance

From models to machines. The lecture picks three of the most studied platforms, starting with superconducting circuits: a microscopic racetrack where current runs with no resistance at all, because the materials, cooled to just above absolute zero, become superconductors.

  • Principle. The superconducting circuit is the container for the qubit. The key part is the Josephson junction, a gatekeeper that lets current pass between two superconductors.
  • In the lab. The first thing you see is the dilution refrigerator, which looks like a large metal chandelier and cools the qubits below the temperature of outer space. Around it stand machines that generate precise microwave pulses to give the qubits their tasks, and measuring devices to read the results. Running it, the lecture says, is like conducting a symphony: scientists send commands, the machines turn them into exact microwave sequences.
  • Hardware and materials. Thin layers of superconducting metal — usually aluminium — on a silicon or sapphire base. The Josephson junction is a thin non-superconducting barrier, insulating or semiconducting, sandwiched between superconducting layers.
  • Tools. Electron-beam lithography to draw the circuits at nanometre scale; the dilution refrigerator; calibrated microwave pulse generators.
  • Software. An ordinary computer runs the programs that design the algorithms, send them to the hardware and interpret the results — the bridge between people and qubits.

And the lecture’s scorecard:

PropertyWhat the lecture says
What they areArtificial atoms made of capacitors and Josephson junctions, running on the flow of current.
StabilityStrong, thanks to their macroscopic size, but sensitive to electromagnetic noise.
CoherenceTens to hundreds of microseconds “as of my last update” — short, but enough for a reasonable number of operations.
ScalabilityA strong case, with Google and IBM investing heavily; crosstalk between qubits is the challenge, answered by better chip design and error correction.

Beyond the lectureThe lecture’s “tens to hundreds of microseconds” is out of date. In 2025 two labs passed a millisecond: Aalto University reported a transmon keeping its phase for just over 1 ms (July), and Princeton, building on tantalum instead of aluminium, reported lifetimes above 1 ms — about fifteen times today’s commercial chips (November). The scaling story has moved too. Google’s 105-qubit Willow chip (December 2024) gave the first convincing proof that error correction pays: each time the surface code grew, from a 3-by-3 patch to 5-by-5 to 7-by-7, the logical error rate fell by about half. IBM unveiled its 120-qubit Nighthawk and its Loon test chip in November 2025, and aims for Starling — 200 logical qubits running 100 million operations — by 2029.

Watch forBeing “macroscopic” is not a source of stability. A circuit you can see under a microscope has far more ways to couple to its surroundings than a single atom, which is exactly why superconducting qubits lose their states in microseconds while trapped ions (move 11) keep theirs for seconds. The lecture itself contradicts the claim in move 13, where it calls them “extremely sensitive to their environment”.

11

Trapped ions

The crown: single atoms held in electric fields

The second titan is a dance floor suspended in space, where ions — atoms with an electric charge — are held and choreographed one at a time.

  • Principle. Each ion is a qubit, held in place by electromagnetic fields. Its internal state — which way it “spins” — carries the information, and lasers change that state.
  • In the lab. The eye-catcher is the ion trap, often made of gold or another metal and shaped like a tiny, intricate crown. Lasers on adjustable mounts target each ion; scientists tune their frequency and intensity so the light makes the ions jump between states. Afterwards, detectors read the ions’ “afterglow” to decode the result.
  • Materials. The ions themselves — ytterbium, calcium and barium are favourites — suspended in free space. Nature does the manufacturing: their electrons already have the right quantum properties.
  • Tools. Electrodes that make the trapping field (a Paul trap), finely tuned lasers, and photodetectors and cameras that catch the light the ions give off.
  • Software. Programs that sequence the laser pulses to match the algorithm, sometimes with real-time feedback that adjusts the lasers on the fly.
PropertyWhat the lecture says
What they areSingle ions in electromagnetic fields, the state stored in the electrons around the nucleus.
StabilityNaturally stable, and the trap isolates them from disturbance.
CoherenceAmong the longest of any qubit: often many seconds.
ScalabilityThe hard part: controlling many ions at once, and building bigger trap arrays.

Beyond the lectureWolfgang Paul shared the 1989 Nobel Prize in Physics for the ion trap. The lecture’s “many seconds” is modest: in 2021 a Tsinghua team kept a single ytterbium ion’s qubit coherent for over an hour (about 5,500 seconds, estimated). How ions get entangled at all: ions in a trap push on each other, so they share a motion, and a laser pulse that ties each ion’s state to that shared motion becomes a two-qubit gate. The price is speed — ion gates take tens to hundreds of microseconds, against tens of nanoseconds on a superconducting chip.

The scaling problem the lecture names is being attacked by moving ions between zones of a chip rather than cramming them into one line. Quantinuum’s Helios, launched in November 2025, holds 98 barium ions and its two-qubit gates succeed 99.92% of the time. IonQ has reported 99.99% on prototypes (October 2025, a company claim).

12

Topological qubits

The knot: information stored in a braid

The third titan is a city’s roads imagined as knotted overpasses and underpasses, where the shape of the whole network is what matters, so a small disruption in one place leaves the overall structure intact. Topological qubits use “knots of quantum states” in the same way to guard against errors.

  • Principle. Topology studies properties that survive continuous deformation. Here, braided paths in a two-dimensional space determine the qubit’s state, so the information isn’t stored in one place but in the twists of the braid, and local errors can’t easily reach it. It’s like trying to remove a knot from a twisted rubber band without untwisting it.
  • In the lab. Specialised equipment to make and study two-dimensional materials such as quantum wells, in which quasiparticles called anyons appear — with fractional quantum numbers, and the heart of the method. Cryogenic cooling, and high-resolution microscopes such as scanning tunnelling microscopes, which the lecture says observe and manipulate the braids directly, letting scientists “weave” the knots.
  • Materials and tools. Ultra-pure samples in which anyons can emerge; information stored by moving anyons around one another.
  • Software. Still emerging: tools to design and simulate braiding and to check for errors.
PropertyWhat the lecture says
What they areA newcomer, using anyons in special two-dimensional materials.
StabilityHighly stable: local errors don’t easily disturb them.
CoherenceEarly findings suggest long coherence, possibly beyond trapped ions — not yet established.
ScalabilityExcellent in principle, but largely untested in practice.

Beyond the lectureWhere it actually stands. Alexei Kitaev proposed topological quantum computing in 1997, and Microsoft has pursued it longest, looking for “Majorana” modes at the ends of tiny wires of indium arsenide and aluminium. The record so far is rocky. A landmark 2018 paper from Delft claiming Majorana signatures was retracted in 2021. In February 2025 Microsoft announced Majorana 1, billed as an eight-qubit topological chip, but its own Nature paper said the measurements did not by themselves show the states were topological, and a formal critique appeared in Nature in June 2026. Microsoft showed a “Majorana 2” the same month; many physicists remain unconvinced. As of September 2026, nobody has independently confirmed a working topological qubit.

Watch forTwo claims to discount. Nobody weaves quantum braids with the tip of a scanning tunnelling microscope: in the leading designs, anyons would be moved or “braided” by switching voltages on electrodes, or by a sequence of measurements, and STMs have only been used to look for their signatures. And “early findings suggest long coherence” is ahead of the evidence — as the box above explains, a working topological qubit hasn’t yet been convincingly shown.

The titans compared

Strengths, Achilles heels, and what each is for

All three rest on the same quantum principles, the lecture says, but each exploits different physical properties with different tools. Its one-line verdict: superconducting circuits ride the wave of industrial investment, trapped ions offer integrity and stability, and topological qubits are promising but largely uncharted. Then the side-by-side:

StrengthsLimitationsBest suited for
SuperconductingQuick and efficient; a head start from being the most mature.Extremely sensitive to their environment.General-purpose computing, backed by major industry.
Trapped ionsHigh precision, very long coherence.Scaling up: more ions means cluttered interactions.Specialised tasks needing precision and long runs, but not many qubits.
TopologicalUnmatched resistance to local errors, which could simplify error correction.Still research; making and controlling anyons is hard.Stable, low-error computing — once it leaves the lab.

It ends the comparison looking ahead: the race is very much alive, new approaches may appear, and one day quantum computers might even help design their own successors.

Its own figures say otherwise: tens to hundreds of microseconds for superconducting qubits against seconds for ions. Speed is their real strength — their gates are far faster, so they fit many operations into that short window.

Arc E · Beyond the titans

Fault tolerance and quantum networks

Scaling up means more errors, and linking machines means sending qubits

The lecture turns to scale. The bigger a quantum system gets, the more chances for error: a stray photon, a slight temperature change or a bit of electromagnetic noise can knock a qubit off course. Classical computers handle errors with redundancy — it gives the example of several transistors standing for one bit. Quantum systems need something subtler, called fault-tolerant quantum computing. The aim isn’t to prevent errors but to detect and correct them without measuring the qubits, since measuring would destroy the quantum state.

How it’s done: special quantum error-correcting codes notice when qubits stray and guide them back without collapsing their superpositions. For that the computer needs extra qubits, called ancilla qubits, whose job is to watch the qubits doing the computation; special gates entangle the ancillas with the working qubits so that errors show up and can be fixed. The price is hardware: many more physical qubits for every error-corrected “logical” qubit.

Then networks. A scalable quantum network would link many quantum devices into one system, like today’s internet but running on quantum principles. The lecture says that needs quantum repeaters, devices that “receive, amplify and retransmit” quantum signals without destroying their quantum nature, plus ways to convert quantum signals into forms that travel far — usually photons.

Beyond the lectureThe trick behind “detect without measuring” is to measure only relationships between qubits — “do these two agree?” — never the value itself. An ancilla is entangled with a pair of data qubits and then measured, and its answer reveals whether one of them flipped without revealing whether the data is 0 or 1. The lab in move 21 lets you run the simplest version.

The repeater scheme the lecture gestures at was set out by Hans Briegel, Wolfgang Dür, Ignacio Cirac and Peter Zoller in 1998: entangle short links, store the pairs in quantum memories, join the links by entanglement swapping, and “purify” noisy pairs into fewer, cleaner ones. In 2024 Harvard and Amazon entangled two diamond-based memories 35 km apart over ordinary Boston fibre, holding the entanglement for about a second.

Watch forA quantum repeater can’t “amplify” anything. Amplifying means copying, and an unknown quantum state can’t be copied — the no-cloning theorem (topic 17, move 2). Real repeater designs share entangled pairs over short hops, store them in quantum memories, and join the hops with entanglement swapping, the same trick as teleportation (topic 17, move 8). Classical computers don’t mainly rely on duplicate transistors either; they use error-correcting codes, as in the ECC memory of servers.

15

NV centres in diamond

A flaw in a diamond that works as a qubit

An emerging approach to both computing and networking, the lecture says, comes from a natural defect in diamond. In a nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centre, a nitrogen atom takes the place of a carbon atom, right next to a gap where a carbon atom is missing. The defect has an electron spin that can be controlled and read with light, which makes it a qubit candidate.

  • Hardware. Diamond chips containing NV centres.
  • Control. Green lasers act on the spin state; microwaves control the spin.
  • Readout. The centre gives off red light as it returns to its ground state, and that light reveals its quantum state.
  • The rest of the kit. The setup is compact, but needs precision optics, stabilised lasers and sensitive photon detectors.

Because an NV centre is so sensitive to electric and magnetic fields, it has also been proposed as a quantum sensor: in biology to image processes inside cells, in geology to detect mineral deposits. The lecture closes the section with the big picture: keeping errors in check and linking systems into scalable networks moves us closer to a quantum internet.

Beyond the lectureNV centres already have a place in this story. The 2015 Delft experiment that closed the loopholes in Bell’s test (topic 17, move 8) used NV spins in two diamonds 1.3 km apart. In 2021 the same group linked three NV nodes into the first entanglement-based quantum network, passing entanglement between the outer two by entanglement swapping — the repeater trick from move 14. As sensors they have one rare advantage: they work at room temperature, and are used to map magnetic fields and even measure temperature inside living cells. One detail the lecture blurs: green light mostly resets and reads the spin while microwaves do the steering, and the reading works because the centre glows more brightly in one spin state than in the other.

16

Neutral atoms and tweezers of light

Uncharged atoms, held and moved by laser light

Next, neutral atoms. Unlike ions or electrons, which push and pull on each other through their electric charge, neutral atoms interact only weakly — which brings its own advantages and its own problems. Alkali atoms such as rubidium have distinct energy levels that serve as the qubit’s 0 and 1, and their interactions can be used to make gates. The toolkit:

  1. Optical traps — “tweezers of light”. Lasers tuned so that their intensity forms tiny traps, a landscape of dimples, each holding one atom.
  2. Magnetic fields to fine-tune the atoms’ states.
  3. Laser pulses that make atoms interact in a controlled way — the gates.
  4. High-resolution imaging that catches light from the atoms to read out the answer.

The lecture pictures it as a high-tech light show, ultra-cold atoms dancing under lasers. Then it zooms in on the traps themselves, “a triumph of marrying quantum mechanics with photonics”. The principle: light exerts a force on matter. An atom or small particle meeting a focused laser beam is pushed or pulled by its photons and so held in place. The tools are tunable lasers (their intensity and focus set the size and depth of the trap, for anything from single atoms to small biological molecules), microscope objectives to focus the beams, and feedback systems that adjust the laser in real time so nothing escapes. The image: a futuristic game of marbles, each marble held still by light.

Beyond the lectureThe weak interaction is switched on when needed. A laser lifts an atom into a Rydberg state, where its outer electron sits so far out that the atom swells enormously and strongly affects its neighbours — so strongly that a nearby atom can’t be lifted at the same time (the “Rydberg blockade”). That conditional behaviour is a two-qubit gate; Harvard ran it at 99.5% fidelity on 60 atoms at once in 2023. The tweezers themselves come from Arthur Ashkin, who shared the 2018 Nobel Prize in Physics for them.

Since the book, neutral atoms have grown faster than any other platform: 48 logical qubits made from 280 rubidium atoms (Harvard, QuEra and MIT, 2023); 24 entangled logical qubits from Atom Computing and Microsoft (2024); a 448-atom error-corrected design working below threshold (Harvard, 2025); and 6,100 caesium atoms held in a single tweezer array (Caltech, 2025). Google started its own neutral-atom team in 2026.

What it costs

Capital, running costs, scale, and the payoff

The lecture steps out of the lab. A quantum computer, it says, sits where feasibility, economics and the environment meet — the kind of down-to-earth talk the physicist Leonard Susskind and his co-author Art Friedman might have over coffee. However marvellous the science, getting into everyday use depends on economic viability. Its five considerations:

  1. Capital investment. Not just a lab full of scientists but the materials, the specialist equipment — dilution refrigerators, laser systems — and an environment where quantum effects can survive. Far beyond a typical start-up’s costs.
  2. Operating costs. The spending doesn’t stop once it runs: cooling for superconducting qubits, precision control for trapped ions, error correction for topological qubits, and the often-forgotten cost of training people to run and maintain the machines.
  3. Economies of scale. Costs usually fall as production grows, and quantum hardware probably will too — but it’s early, and scaling brings its own problems.
  4. Making money from it. The payoff lies in applications — financial modelling, drug discovery, optimisation — but how fast industries adopt them, and pay for them, is an open question.
  5. The long view. High costs now, possibly huge returns later, and a geopolitical and market edge for the nations and companies that lead.

Its analogy is the silicon revolution: the first chips were expensive, did little, and lived in room-sized computers, yet visionaries saw where they were going.

Beyond the lectureSome numbers the lecture doesn’t give. A dilution refrigerator costs from roughly $150,000 for a small research unit to over $2 million for a large one (a vendor’s figures, so treat them as rough). Power is lower than you might guess: Google’s 2019 Sycamore drew about 26 kW, mostly for the refrigerator’s compressor and chilled water, and the same whether computing or idle; D-Wave quotes 12.5 kW for its current annealer. A top supercomputer draws around 20 megawatts. The economics have also moved from buying to renting — IBM put a five-qubit machine on the cloud in May 2016, Amazon and Microsoft followed in 2020 and 2021 — so most users never pay for a fridge. And the money is arriving: McKinsey counted about $2 billion invested in quantum start-ups in 2024 and $12.6 billion in 2025, when quantum computing companies together earned over $1 billion.

Watch forThe lecture lists financial modelling, drug discovery and optimisation as areas where quantum computers “can outshine” classical ones. None has a demonstrated practical advantage yet; simulation of molecules is the best-founded hope, while optimisation and finance remain unproven (topic 17, move 12).

Who’s buying in

Six kinds of adopter, from Silicon Valley to oil companies

Quantum computing, the lecture says, reaches well beyond the tech giants. Its six groups of adopters:

  1. Tech powerhouses — Google, IBM, Intel and Microsoft have built their own processors, hoping to strengthen their core businesses, from cloud computing to artificial intelligence.
  2. Universities and research institutes — from MIT to the University of Queensland — supplying theory and building prototypes, often with industry.
  3. Aerospace and defence — drawn by code-breaking, logistics and simulation; the lecture names Lockheed Martin and Airbus.
  4. Finance and banking — exploring portfolio optimisation, risk analysis and fraud detection.
  5. Pharmaceuticals and healthcare — molecular simulation for drug design; it names Roche and Pfizer.
  6. Energy — energy storage, grid optimisation and new energy sources; it names Shell and ExxonMobil.

And a trend: the equipment looks confined to specialist labs today, but as the technology matures, the lecture expects quantum processors to spread out, “humming away in the control rooms” of industries from pharmaceuticals to finance.

Beyond the lectureChecking the lecture’s names. Lockheed Martin: yes — it bought the first commercial quantum computer, a 128-qubit D-Wave One, in 2011, reportedly for about $10 million. Airbus: launched a Quantum Computing Challenge in 2019. ExxonMobil: in January 2019 it became the first energy company in IBM’s quantum network. Shell: a five-year collaboration on quantum-chemistry algorithms with Leiden University and VU Amsterdam, from 2020. Roche: partnered with Cambridge Quantum on Alzheimer’s research in 2021. Pfizer: no early flagship partnership that I could find; it co-authored a 2026 study run on Quantinuum’s Helios. The best-documented early pharma deal is one the lecture misses, Boehringer Ingelheim with Google (2021). In finance, JPMorgan and Quantinuum used a 56-qubit machine to generate certified random numbers (Nature, 2025) — one of the first finance-linked results on real hardware.

Watch forSo far the spreading-out has mostly happened through the cloud, not control rooms: the refrigerators stay in the providers’ data centres, and companies rent time over the internet. A handful of machines have been installed on customers’ own sites, but “quantum processors in every control room” is a prediction, not a trend yet.

20

Noise, errors and decoherence

The pond, the raindrops and the gust of wind

The chapter’s last part, “solving error correction”, starts with the enemy. Quantum systems are powerful but finely tuned instruments; the slightest interference throws them off. The lecture separates three things:

  • Quantum noise — unwanted disturbances from outside: heat vibrations, electromagnetic waves, even cosmic rays. Picture a still lake; every pebble thrown in makes ripples.
  • Quantum errors — missteps in the dance, during processing or readout. A bit flip turns 0 into 1 or 1 into 0; a phase flip shifts the qubit’s phase. Each may be small, but in a large circuit they add up.
  • Decoherence — “the real nemesis”: a quantum system losing its quantum behaviour and acting classically, like waking from a dream where anything was possible into ordinary reality. It’s caused by interaction with the environment; every stray photon or molecule the qubit meets takes away a little of its “quantumness”.

The lecture then puts all three on one pond. The still pond is the ideal qubit, and a dropped pebble makes the ripples you intend — the state you prepared. Raindrops are noise, each nudging the state a little and blurring it. A gust of wind is a direct error in how you control or read the qubits: it doesn’t just disturb the water, it changes the whole ripple pattern. Dealing with these, it says, is central to building large quantum computers — and it leads to error correction.

Beyond the lectureThe two error types are two gates from topic 17. A bit flip is an unwanted X; a phase flip is an unwanted Z, which turns |+⟩ into |−⟩. A phase flip is invisible if you measure 0-or-1 straight away, but it reverses what the next H does, so it wrecks interference, the engine of every speed-up (topic 17, move 4). Engineers measure the two kinds of decay separately: T1 is how long a 1 takes to relax to 0, and T2 is how long the phase survives. The cosmic rays are real too: in 2021 Google found that high-energy particle strikes cause sudden bursts of errors across a whole chip at once — something codes must also cope with.

21

Error-correcting codes

Shor’s nine qubits, the surface code, and the cat

With errors this easy to make, the lecture says, a safety net is essential: quantum error correction (QEC), which does for qubits what classical error correction does for the bits in an ordinary computer. It describes three schemes:

  1. The Shor code, designed by Peter Shor, the first quantum error-correcting code. Instead of one qubit it uses nine to hold one qubit’s worth of information, and it can find and fix an error on any one of them.
  2. The surface code, which lays qubits out on a two-dimensional grid so that errors can be spotted and fixed efficiently as they appear along the grid’s lines. It’s a favourite for being robust and for its relatively high error threshold.
  3. Cat codes, named after Schrödinger’s cat. They use superpositions of coherent states, which the lecture describes as states whose waves share the same phase; an error shifts the phase, and the special superposition makes the shift detectable so it can be corrected.

The picture: an orchestra where each musician is a qubit, and the codes are a conductor who hears the wrong notes and brings the players back into tune. Reliable error correction needs a lot of qubits and sophisticated algorithms, the lecture admits, but progress has been promising.

The lecture then begins its next section, “challenges in achieving high-fidelity qubit operations” — calling it one of the most formidable problems in the field — and ends there. The rest of that section will belong to the next set of notes.

Beyond the lectureThe lab is the repetition code, the idea inside all three schemes. Store a bit as three copies; if one flips, the two checks — “do copies 1 and 2 agree?”, “do 2 and 3 agree?” — point to the culprit without ever saying whether the bit is 0 or 1. That is exactly what the ancilla qubits in move 14 measure. With three copies, a bit is lost only if two or more flip, so a 1% flip rate becomes about 0.03%. The quantum version can’t literally copy (no-cloning), so it spreads the state instead: α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ becomes α|000⟩ + β|111⟩.

Shor’s 1995 code is this idea nested: three copies against bit flips, and the whole thing repeated three times against phase flips — 3 × 3 = 9 qubits. The surface code’s threshold is about 1%: if every physical operation fails less often than that, making the grid bigger makes the logical qubit better (topic 17, move 14). The lab’s slider stops at 50% because there the copies carry no information and no vote can help. Real quantum codes hit a similar wall much sooner — that wall is the threshold.

Two of the lecture’s schemes have now reached hardware. Amazon’s Ocelot chip (February 2025) combined five cat qubits with a simple phase-flip code, and the French start-up Alice & Bob reported cat qubits going 33 to 60 minutes between bit flips (2025, preliminary). Quantinuum reported running 48 error-corrected logical qubits on Helios in 2025.

Watch forThe lecture’s explanation of cat codes is muddled. A coherent state is the quantum state that behaves most like an ordinary classical wave in a resonator, and a cat code stores a qubit in a superposition of two such waves with opposite phases — the “alive” and “dead” of the cat. The point isn’t that errors become detectable but that bit flips become extremely rare: the bigger the cat, the harder it is for noise to turn one wave into the other. That leaves mostly phase flips, which a simple repetition code like the one in the lab can handle.
