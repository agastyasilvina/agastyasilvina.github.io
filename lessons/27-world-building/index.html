Arc A · Playing God

4 moves
1

All fiction is fantasy

World-building as the joy of playing God

In the lectureThe joy of world-building in fiction, Gaiman says, is honestly the joy of getting to play God: as the author, you build the world, though some authors do it more invisibly than others. And as far as Gaiman is concerned, all fiction is fantasy. It is all made up. You create people who didn’t exist, or didn’t exist like that, put them into situations they were never in, and make them say things they never said. It is an act of magical creation.

The second claim widens the lesson to everyone. World-building sounds like the business of fantasy writers drawing maps of dragon country, but a realist novel set in a real town builds a world too: it chooses which streets exist, who lives on them, what the weather is doing and what nobody mentions. The only difference is how visible the building is. A crime novel set in an ordinary city is world-building done invisibly; Middle-earth is world-building done in the open.

So everything that follows — where the details come from, which questions you need answered, what the rules are, how much to show — applies to a kitchen as much as to a kingdom.

Elsewhere in these notesThe first lesson, topic 03, made the same point from the other side: fiction tells the truth with things that never happened.

2

Not Tolkien’s world: yours

Start from the world outside your window, then change it

In the lectureStarting out, especially as a young writer who wants to explore the fantastic and create places that are not, you’ll feel an urge you should always push back against: to take places from fiction. It’s the fan-fiction urge, and fan fiction is great in its place. But if you want to create a world, you don’t want one borrowed from Tolkien. Look at the world outside your window. Get out onto the streets, look at places, think about the places you’ve been — and then change them: make them bigger, make them smaller. If you’ve only ever been to school and think school is all you can write about, what would that school be like if it covered an entire city? If it was an island? If it floated in the sky and people could only visit it dragged on the backs of enormous birds? How do you get into that school?

The method has two steps, and the order matters. First, a real place you know from the inside; then one deliberate change. The change makes it fantastic; the real place underneath keeps it honest, because you already know how it works. A school that covers a city still has a timetable, a staff room and a smell at lunchtime — and now all of those have to scale up, which raises questions only your world can answer. “How do you get into that school?” is where a story starts.

Borrowing Tolkien gives you the opposite: a fantastic surface with nobody’s experience underneath it, and readers who have seen it before.

Try it — make it strange

Pick a place you know from the inside and one change, the way Gaiman does with the school. Then answer the three questions for yourself. Shuffle for new ones. Your notes stay in this browser only.

    3

    Cabbage and lockers

    Every stolen detail makes the world real — first of all for you

    In the lectureThat gives you a place grounded in realism. The moment you start describing a school, if you know a school, you know the things that make schools weird and unusual: the smell of cooking cabbage, the smell of sports clothes left unwashed in lockers. You know what that place is and what kinds of people are there. Every little detail you can steal from the world and smuggle into your fiction makes your world more real for your reader — but, much more important, more real for you. You need to be the one who believes in your story, and in your places. If you are building a world, you have to care about it.

    Look at which details Gaiman reaches for: smells. Not the layout or the architecture, but the things you only know from having been there. A reader recognises those with the body, and they are hard to fake, which is why they carry so much weight.

    And the order of importance is deliberate: the reader comes second. A writer who doesn’t quite believe in a place writes it vaguely and hedges; a writer who has smelled the cabbage writes it with confidence, and the confidence is what the reader feels.

    Drill — five stolen details

    Pick a place you know from the inside — a school, a hospital ward, a kitchen, a bus depot. List five things only someone who had been there would know: a smell, a sound, an unwritten rule, a texture, a kind of person.

    Now move the place into your invented world — make it a city, an island, a ship in the sky — and keep all five. Notice how the fantastic version suddenly feels lived in.

    4

    Where do they go to the toilet?

    The questions you need answered, even if the book never asks them

    In the lectureSometimes you’ll have to stop and ask yourself weird questions. Even if they’re never answered in the text, it’s always good for you to know. Where do these people go to the toilet? Where do they get their food from? How much food does it take to feed a small city — how much farmland, where are the farms, where does the food come in? Suddenly you’re asking questions it’s good to know the answers to, even if you never want to turn your book into a treatise on medieval economics. If you’re writing something set in medieval times, do your homework.

    These are logistics questions, and logistics is where invented worlds most often give themselves away: a desert city with no water supply, a castle with hundreds of servants and no kitchens, a long march with no food carried. Readers rarely notice the answers, but they feel the absence.

    The point isn’t to put the answers on the page. It’s that knowing them changes what you write: the carts coming in at dawn, the smell downwind of the tannery, the market day, the fields that start where the walls end.

    Try it — feed the city

    Gaiman’s question, answered roughly. A medieval town eats grain; each field gives a harvest, part of which goes back into the ground as next year’s seed, and some fields rest each year. The starting values are typical of English wheat around 1300. Move the sliders and see how far the fields have to stretch.

    Grain eaten in a year: Land under crops: Arable land, counting the resting fields: Fields in a ring round the town, out to: Walk from the gate to the last field:

    Beyond the lectureWhere the starting values come from. On the Winchester estates in 1283–1349, wheat yielded about 515 kg a hectare, and wheat returned about four grains for each one sown, so a quarter of every harvest went back into the ground as seed (Jules Pretty, Agricultural History Review, 1990). Across England, wheat yields after seed fell from about 8.5 bushels an acre in the 1270s to 6.5 in the 1450s (Broadberry, Campbell and colleagues, 2010). A three-field system rested a third of the arable each year and a two-field system half (Feeding Medieval England); around 1300 about 36% of England’s arable lay fallow. For food, Broadberry and colleagues assume farms supplied about 1,500 calories a person a day, which comes to roughly 220 kg of grain a year by my reckoning; the FAO puts a modern cereal-based diet at 175 kg.

    So the lab gives a floor, not an estimate. It leaves out pasture for animals, woodland for fuel, barley for ale, oats for horses and the tithe, and in reality farming families ate most of what they grew. Real towns drew on far larger areas: Bruce Campbell and colleagues found medieval London, perhaps 60,000–100,000 people around 1300, regularly drawing grain from more than 4,000 square miles — over 10,000 km² — within 60 to 90 miles (summary). That was a shared trading area, not fields set aside for London.

    The crush at the gate

    An Arabian Nights trope, finally understood in old Jerusalem

    In the lectureGo and look at places. A moment comes up again and again in the Thousand and One Nights: people travelling together enter a city, and in the crush at the gates they are separated. It never made much sense to Gaiman — until, a little over a decade before the class, Gaiman was in old Jerusalem with guides, going out through one of the old city gates while thousands of people poured through in both directions. Gaiman was swept up and carried away, and when the crowd had thinned enough to look around, the guides were nowhere to be seen. That was the moment from the stories. Gaiman has never written it, but writing an Arabian Nights story now, Gaiman would know how it feels, could make it very believable — and could give it to the characters.

    A trope you have only read about is a convention; one you have lived through is an experience. The reading gives you the event — separated at the gate — but not the sensation: the press of bodies, the moment of being carried, the turning round to find no one there. That is the difference between a scene that is plausible and one that is true, and it’s why “do your homework” in this lesson means going places, not only reading about them.

    6

    A notebook in the worst moments

    The part of a writer that is always taking notes

    In the lectureThere’s always a tiny part of you, as a writer, that — metaphorically or really — is standing there with a notebook, taking notes. It can come out at the worst times, in the worst moments of your life. You can be in a hospital while a friend or someone you love is wheeled away, and find yourself asking: what does this place smell like? How are they behaving? How do I feel right now? How do these people feel? Because one day you’ll be writing fiction and you will need that moment — the way the lights of the cars glint off the broken glass and the blood on the asphalt — to make a story true.

    This is the most uncomfortable idea in the lesson, and Gaiman doesn’t soften it. Watching yourself while something terrible happens can feel like a betrayal of the moment.

    But the note-taking part doesn’t replace the feeling; it sits beside it, and it is what lets the feeling survive in a form a story can use. Nothing has to be written down at the time, and nothing ever has to be used. It is the same compost heap as in topic 05, fed with the hardest material there is.

    Elsewhere in these notes“Listen, and write it down” is topic 05, move 7; the grief class in topics 22–25 is about living through those hospital corridors, not mining them.

    7

    The same act, real or invented

    New York or a graveyard: start with a world, then fill it with people

    In the lectureSo the action of world-building is always the same, whether you’re building New York, Los Angeles or the Isle of Skye — places that really exist — or creating a graveyard, an enormous world underneath London, or a magical America that doesn’t quite exist, in a state of heightened reality. You start with a world, and you fill it with people. And you decide what’s important about the world, and what’s convincing about it.

    Real places get no exemption. A novel’s New York is a selection too: the writer chooses which streets, which season, which neighbours, which smells, and the reader meets that selection, not the city. So the two decisions Gaiman names — what is important, and what is convincing — are the whole job either way.

    Beyond the lectureThe list is a quiet tour of Gaiman’s own books. The enormous world under London is Neverwhere’s London Below, first a BBC Two series in 1996 and a novel the same year (overview). The magical America in a state of heightened reality is American Gods (2001), winner of both the Hugo and the Nebula. The graveyard is The Graveyard Book (move 11). And the Isle of Skye is the setting of the novelette The Truth Is a Cave in the Black Mountains, first published in the 2010 collection Stories (review).

    8

    Misty smudges

    One real mountain makes the reader believe in all the others

    In the lectureThe worlds we build in fiction are soap bubbles: they can pop really easily, but they seem really, really solid. Anything you can say that makes them feel real buttresses them and gives them credibility — and then the reader assumes you could tell them everything else. The world goes off in all directions, but one little moment of reality, one thing that seems absolutely true, gives credence to everything you don’t say: to all the bits that are really just misty smudges in the background. The reader assumes that if we moved in, the smudges would turn into craggy mountains, each one different. You know they won’t; they’re smudges. But that’s fine, because the one mountain we visited was real — informed by you and a mountain, or by you, a very big hill and an imagination.

    This is the economy of world-building. You can’t build everything, so build one thing completely and let it vouch for the rest: one fully realised street makes the whole city real. The reverse holds too. One false detail — an inn with no smell, a battle with no aftermath — can pop the bubble, because then the reader starts doubting everything you didn’t show.

    Beyond the lectureTolkien made the same argument in a 1939 lecture, “On Fairy-Stories”, published in 1947 (overview). The storyteller is a sub-creator who makes a Secondary World the reader’s mind can enter; inside it, what the story says is “true” because it agrees with that world’s laws, and the reader believes it while inside. Then the soap bubble: “The moment disbelief arises, the spell is broken.”

    That is why Tolkien rejected Samuel Taylor Coleridge’s famous phrase, the “willing suspension of disbelief” (Biographia Literaria, 1817): a reader who has to push doubt down has already been thrown out of the world. A good world never asks for the effort, and Gaiman’s one real mountain is how you stop disbelief arising in the first place.

    Drill — one real mountain

    Choose one place in your world and write it down to the smell, the light and the noise. Then mention three other places only by name, in passing.

    Read it back. The named places borrow their reality from the one you built — that’s the smudges at work.

    Arc D · The rules

    2 moves
    9

    Nobody hands you the rulebook

    Know your world’s rules, explicit and implicit

    In the lectureOne thing that is very important for you to know, but not necessarily important for you to tell, is what the rules are — especially if you’re creating anything in the world of the fantastic. The problem is that when we arrive in this world, nobody tells us the rules. Nobody hands a small child a rulebook explaining how gravity works, how fire works, how basic economics works, how people behave and what they’re expected to do. You figure it out as you go along. There are rules, some explicit and some implicit, and when you’re world-building you need to know both. And knowing them, treat them as you would in this world, where people find things out by making mistakes: fire is hot, you learn, by picking up something that’s been in the fire, or putting your hand in it — “Ow.” Given a precious thing made of ice, you learn to keep it cold, because put it in your pocket and soon you have a wet pocket and no precious thing. It’s always by trial, and always by error.

    Explicit rules are the stated ones: laws, prices, the magic system as a teacher would explain it. Implicit rules are the ones nobody says aloud: who sits where, which questions are rude, what everybody knows not to touch. Real places run mostly on the second kind, and invented worlds feel thin when they only have the first.

    Drill — a rules ledger

    Write three explicit rules of your world and three implicit ones. For each, note who breaks it first, by accident, and what it costs them.

    Any rule nobody ever breaks probably doesn’t need to be in the story — but keep it in the ledger. You still need to know it.

    10

    Let them bump into things

    Characters learn the rules the way we do — and you always know more than you tell

    In the lectureSo, when world-building, let your characters discover the rules of your world — which you know. They discover them by making mistakes, or by taking advantage of them. Let things happen to your characters: let them go out and bump into things, let them make mistakes — and always know more than you tell. It’s fine if readers say they don’t really understand the rules of a place. Nobody understands the rules of New York either: you find out some of them by making mistakes, and some by hanging around with people who seem to have figured out a few of them — and that’s okay.

    This turns explanation into story. A rule explained in a paragraph is information; a rule discovered with a burned hand is an event, with a cost and a consequence. And the second route Gaiman mentions — hanging around with people who know — gives you a classic shape: the newcomer and the guide. The reader learns alongside the newcomer, which is why so many fantasies begin with someone arriving. In Neverwhere, Richard Mayhew falls into London Below knowing none of its rules, and learns them the hard way.

    Beyond the lectureA useful counterweight to “know more than you tell” comes from the fantasy novelist Brandon Sanderson’s “First Law”: “An author’s ability to solve conflict with magic is directly proportional to how well the reader understands said magic.” The two fit together. Rules you keep hidden can create wonder, mystery and trouble; but if a rule is going to get your hero out of trouble, the reader needs to have seen it at work first — or the rescue feels like cheating.

    Elsewhere in these notesNeverwhere’s other kindness to the reader — making every character look and sound different — is the funny hats of topic 15, move 11.

    A tricycle among the gravestones

    The idea that was better than the writer, and twenty years of waiting

    In the lectureThe first inspiration for The Graveyard Book was a little country graveyard behind a church across the lane from the Gaiman family’s house. Gaiman would take a son of two or two and a half across the lane with a tricycle, and the little boy would pedal along the paths between the gravestones. One day, watching: “You look so comfortable here” — and then, wouldn’t it be interesting to tell a story about a kid who gets lost in a graveyard and is brought up by dead people? It would be like The Jungle Book, only in a graveyard: The Graveyard Book. Gaiman would have been about 25, went straight up to the office and wrote the first chapter — then looked at it and thought: “The idea is so much better than I am a writer.” Not abandoning it, but admitting “my chops are not there yet to do justice to this”, and planning to come back in five or ten years, when good enough. Gaiman came back almost twenty years later, on realising that far from getting any better, “I was as good as I was going to get” — so there was no option: it was time to write it.

    Two lessons in one story. The idea came from a real place watched closely — a small child at ease among the dead — not from a plot. And the decision to wait is unusual advice; most writing advice says start now. Gaiman’s version is to keep the idea alive and feed it, and to begin when waiting has stopped making you better.

    The joke at the end is serious too. You will never feel ready, so at some point readiness has to be declared.

    Beyond the lectureThe Graveyard Book came out in 2008, won the Newbery Medal in 2009 and the Carnegie Medal in 2010, and was the first book to win both — the leading American and British prizes for children’s fiction (report). In NPR’s report on the Newbery, Gaiman described the original thought in the same terms: a story about a kid without a family, adopted by dead people, like The Jungle Book (NPR, 2009).

    Watch forGaiman’s retellings differ in the small print. In interviews, the year is 1985, in a very tall, very thin terraced house in Sussex, Gaiman was 24 rather than 25, and what got written then was “a scene” rather than the first chapter (SciFiNow). When the writing really began, some twenty years later, the first chapter written was chapter four, “The Witch’s Headstone” (Publishers Weekly). The book came out about 23 years after the idea.

    Elsewhere in these notesThe book’s first chapter — the knife in the dark, the toddler climbing up to the graveyard — is the map of colliding wants in topic 11, move 1.

    12

    Collecting graveyards

    A chapel from one, a hill from another, and a graveyard built in the head

    In the lectureIn those twenty years, knowing the story would be set in a graveyard, Gaiman went to look at them. The world-building exercise was simply visiting graveyards: Abney Park in Stoke Newington, with its glorious ruined chapel in the middle — “Right, I’m going to have you. You’re in my story” — and the Glasgow Necropolis, all on a hill: “I’m taking your topology,” the hill and the place apart from the city. Gaiman built a graveyard in the head, played with it, saw what kind of people lived in it, worked out the details, and wherever Gaiman went, visited the local graveyard to see if there was some little thing to take and add. So twenty years later, starting to write, Gaiman knew the graveyard had to be the book’s jungle: big and interesting enough to set basically a whole novel in one location. By then Gaiman knew what a graveyard was, why it was there, its history — and that allowed it to be a real place.

    One invented graveyard, assembled from many real ones, each contributing a single feature: a chapel here, a hill there. That is move 3’s stolen details, practised over years. Notice the shape of each theft: not “a graveyard like Abney Park” but Abney Park’s chapel — one specific thing, taken whole.

    Beyond the lectureAbney Park opened in 1840, the smallest of London’s “Magnificent Seven” Victorian cemeteries, with its chapel at the heart of the grounds (London Museum). The ruin is gone now: gutted by fire and vandalism in the 1980s, the chapel was repaired in 2017 and brought back into use by a £5 million restoration finished in August 2024 (Hackney Council). The Glasgow Necropolis opened in 1833 on a hill above Glasgow Cathedral (Historic Environment Scotland). And another London cemetery gave the book a piece: from Highgate, Gaiman has said, came the Egyptian walk (NPR, 2011).

    Watch forThe Glasgow Necropolis isn’t outside Glasgow: it stands on the hill just above the cathedral, about a kilometre east of the city centre. In a 2011 interview Gaiman praised an unnamed graveyard “outside Glasgow”, so the hill in this story may belong to another cemetery.

    Drill — collect a place

    Next time you’re somewhere that reminds you of your setting — a market, a station, a church, a car park at night — pick out one thing and tell it, silently, “I’m going to have you.” Write down what it was and what it gives you: a shape, a sound, a history.

    13

    Homework and tiny notebooks

    Research, walks, voice notes and the compost heap

    In the lectureA lot of world-building is doing your homework — like going through the tunnels for Neverwhere and being shown around. Go on walks, see things, and take notes. Use the voice function on your phone to leave yourself little notes; use a tiny notebook. Gaiman always carries tiny notebooks, impossible to get out of a pocket, and jots down things and little ideas — maybe never going back to them, but once they’re written down, they’re rotting away, usefully, on the compost heap of the imagination, there if they’re needed.

    Two kinds of research meet here. One is deliberate: arranging to be shown the tunnels. The other is ambient: a notebook on every walk. The second is the cheap habit that makes the first pay off, because the ideas you jot down without a purpose are the ones that turn out to fit a world you haven’t built yet.

    Elsewhere in these notesThe compost heap is topic 05, move 1; how much research is enough — smash and grab, don’t fall into the vortex — is topic 15, move 9; and Neverwhere’s London Below began as a pun on a place name, in topic 10.

    You’d know you were there

    Middle-earth and Narnia, read as a child

    In the lectureAuthors you read when you are young affect you on a level that is very primal, and very different. The ones who built worlds stayed with Gaiman, who would look at what they had done, puzzle over it and think about it a lot. Tolkien, obviously, was a huge influence — though Gaiman has never written anything set somewhere resembling Middle-earth, except a little in Stardust, which is much more a world of fairy tale. What mattered was the feeling that this was a place Gaiman would recognise if seen: go there, and you’d know you were there. The same with Narnia: C. S. Lewis built an entire country, a kingdom, and in Narnia you would know you were in Narnia — the people, the way everything looked and felt, the descriptions of huge cliffs, and of looking down at clouds so far below that they looked like tiny sheep.

    “Would I know I was there?” is a test you can put to your own world. Recognisability doesn’t come from size or a long history, but from a handful of vivid, specific sensations that stand in for the whole — clouds so far below they look like sheep.

    Beyond the lectureThe sheep are in the first chapter of The Silver Chair (1953), at the top of an enormous cliff in Aslan’s country: far below are “little white things that might, at first glance, be mistaken for sheep”, which turn out to be clouds as big as mountains. And Stardust — first a four-part illustrated story with Charles Vess (1997–98), then a novel (1999) — follows a young man from the village of Wall, which borders Faerie (overview).

    15

    The world-building you don’t notice

    Mary Poppins’s London, and Lud-in-the-Mist

    In the lectureThen there were writers who built worlds in a way Gaiman wasn’t conscious of as a child, and only as an adult looked back on: “Whoa, it was a remarkable, fascinating act of world-building.” P. L. Travers’s Mary Poppins stories seemed to be set simply in London, and Gaiman didn’t examine how Travers built that universe — one where you could go to a zoo, or a circus of the stars, at night; where people appeared in the park in spring and painted the trees, taking everything out of its winter clothes and into its summer ones; where things happened between things; where Mary Poppins’s mere presence deformed reality and made everything hyper-real, a bridge between pure imagination, the way things should be, and the world we lived in, the way things were. And Hope Mirrlees’s Lud-in-the-Mist, which defies the whole show-don’t-tell paradigm by beginning almost as a travelogue and a history of an imaginary country — the country of Dorimare and its town of Lud-in-the-Mist — and gradually becomes a detective story, a ghost story, a story of generations, of one person’s struggle to be who they are and find happiness in an awkward world, and of the infinite struggle between fantasy and reality, fairy and the prosaic. All of it there in an act of world-building: of geography, and of history.

    Two opposite techniques. Travers hides the building inside a familiar city: London is ordinary until, suddenly, it isn’t. Mirrlees puts the building first, as a history lesson, breaking “show, don’t tell” — and gets away with it because the history is itself the subject of the story. Rules of craft are defaults, not laws.

    Beyond the lectureThe episodes Gaiman remembers are real chapters. The zoo at night is “Full Moon”, chapter 10 of Mary Poppins (1934), where the animals go free because Mary Poppins’s birthday falls on a full moon. The circus of the stars is “The Evening Out”, chapter 7 of Mary Poppins Comes Back (1935), with the Sun as ringmaster and the constellations performing. Spring comes in “Nellie-Rubina”, chapter 9 of the same book — though there, rather than painting the trees, a pair of wooden figures fix leaves to the bare branches.

    Lud-in-the-Mist (1926) really does open like a gazetteer — “The Free State of Dorimare was a very small country…” — and its second chapter sketches “the history of Dorimare and the beliefs and customs of its inhabitants” (Project Gutenberg). Gaiman has called it “one of the finest in the English language… a little golden miracle of a book” (Fantasy & Science Fiction, 1999).

    16

    Islands on paper

    Earthsea’s map, and the archipelagos drawn at eleven

    In the lectureAs a small boy, Gaiman sat drawing maps, influenced a lot by the maps in Ursula Le Guin’s Earthsea books — the map at the front, with its archipelagos. Loving the shapes of islands, Gaiman drew magical little archipelagos and islands, the shapes of cities and worlds, and imagined stories set on them. And even now, every so often, Gaiman will nip in and set a story in one of the places on the maps drawn at 11 or 12.

    The map came before the stories, and waited decades for them — the same patience as the graveyard. A drawn world is a store of places with no stories in them yet; given time, stories find their way in.

    Beyond the lectureA Wizard of Earthsea came out in 1968 with maps, and Earthsea’s archipelago was Le Guin’s own creation (ursulakleguin.com). Maps have started stories before. Robert Louis Stevenson began Treasure Island with one, drawn one afternoon: “I made the map of an island… the shape of it took my fancy beyond expression; it contained harbours that pleased me like sonnets.” And later: “the map was the most of the plot” (“My First Book”, in Essays in the Art of Writing).

    Drill — draw the map

    Spend ten minutes drawing an island or a city: coastline or walls first, then three places with names. Put it away. In a month, take it out and write one scene in one of the three places.

    To check

    • Names. “Hope Mirrlees”, “Dorimare” and “the Glasgow Necropolis” are the standard names for what the lesson mentions.
    • Gaiman’s age, and a chapter or a scene. The lesson says about 25 and a first chapter; interviews say 24 and a scene (move 11).
    • Dates. “A little over a decade ago” for Jerusalem and “almost twenty years later” for the book count from when the class was made; the lesson gives neither year.
    • Abney Park. No source outside the lesson connects Gaiman with Abney Park; Highgate is the cemetery Gaiman has credited elsewhere. The chapel is no longer a ruin.
    • The Glasgow hill. The name in the lesson is the Necropolis, which is in the city rather than outside it (move 12).
    • “Nellie-Rubina”. The wooden leaves come from a quotation of the chapter, not a reading of the book itself.
    • The feed-the-city lab is a floor: crops only, wheat-like yields, no animals, ale, horses, fuel or tithe. The London figures come from a summary of Campbell and colleagues’ study.
    • Gaiman’s introduction to Lud-in-the-Mist: which edition first carried it is unclear; the quoted praise is from 1999.
