The ninth lesson from this class, and the last: everything after the first draft. Gaiman’s whole method fits in two verbs, explode and fix, and most of this page is about the second. How to read your own work as a stranger, the one question that turns a first draft into a second, what to believe in other people’s notes and what to ignore, and why perfect is the enemy. Then the rules Gaiman borrowed from Robert Heinlein by way of Harlan Ellison, the two jobs a writer does at once, rejection, a writer’s responsibilities, and the class’s last advice: write, finish things, and go and live.

Arc A · Explode, then fix

3 moves
1

Two processes

Explode onto the page, then walk around the shrapnel

In the lectureTo be an artist, and particularly a writer, is “a two-fold process”: creating, then fixing, or editing. “The first thing you do as a writer is you explode. You explode like a bomb explodes. You explode onto the page.” The story is an explosion, and you get to the end of it. Once it’s done, you get to walk around it and look at the shrapnel and the damage it did, “and you get to see who died. And you get to see how it worked.” That’s the point where you get to think about it: what works, and what doesn’t.

The order matters more than the metaphor. Thinking about what works is the second job, and it needs something to work on: the explosion comes first, all the way to the end, without stopping to tidy the debris. Topic 32’s stuck writer and this lesson’s editor are the same person at different moments. Mid-explosion, the job is to keep going; afterwards, it’s to look hard.

2

Read it like a method actor

A week away, a printout, a pencil, and a reader’s notes

In the lectureOnce something is finished, short story, novel or script, Gaiman leaves it if possible: walks away, does something else, then maybe a week or ten days later prints it out, comes back to it and pretends, “like a method actor”: “Pretend like this is the most important thing in the world to me. Pretend that I’ve never read it before and I know nothing about it.” Then comes the reading, on paper, with a pen or a pencil, “not vicious, not cruel, but I’m a reader”, and a note in the margin for anything that doesn’t work for a reader. Some notes catch things that, as a writer, you thought you could get away with: “I don’t need to write the whole battle, do I?” And then, as a reader: “but I was expecting that battle. You’ve lit everything up to this battle.” So the note says: needs a battle. Try to pretend that you as a writer and you as a reader are two different people, because those notes are what really guide you: “the primary engine through a second draft”.

Elsewhere in these notesThe same pretend-you’ve-never-read-it trick rescues a story that has stalled in the middle (topic 32, move 3). Here it comes at the end of a whole draft, with a pencil, and its output is a list of notes rather than a single wrong turning.

Drill — the method actor’s read

Finish something, put it away for a week, then print it. Read it at the speed a reader would, with a pencil, as someone who has never seen it and badly wants it to be good.

Write only reader’s notes in the margin: bored here, lost, I was expecting the battle. No fixes yet. The fixing is the next move, and the notes are its engine.

3

What was that about?

The question that gets you to a second draft

In the lectureAt the end of the first draft, read it with new eyes and ask: “okay, what was that about?” It’s the most important question you can ask. The difference between a first and a second draft can be tiny, but the second is the most important draft, and the question gets you there. Then you buttress what the story is about, and eliminate the places where you’re writing stuff that isn’t: “a wonderful, easy yardstick for what stays in and what goes out.” What you thought it was about probably isn’t what it’s about, or is only part of it. It becomes obvious that the little bit of plot you loved has nothing to do with it, “and that’s good. And you can let it go”: cut it, save it in a file of things that may come in useful later, with never any heartache. And where you seemed to be talking about the theme without quite knowing it, go in and make that stronger: “shore up the sides”. “The process of doing your second draft is a process of making it look like you knew what you were doing all along.”

Try it — what was it about?

For a draft of yours: say in one line what it turned out to be about, list its scenes one to a line, and sort them. Keep, shore up, or cut to the file. Your notes stay in this browser only.

    Elsewhere in these notesGoing in, Gaiman wants some idea of what a story is about, without expecting really to find out until it’s over and reread (topic 10, move 7). This is the other half of that thought: the rereading, and what to do with the answer.

    Arc B · Notes, and perfection

    2 moves

    Right, and wrong

    Believe the problem; distrust the fix

    In the lecture“You always have to remember when people tell you that something doesn’t work for them, that they’re right. It doesn’t work for them. And that is incredibly important information.” But when they tell you what they think is wrong and how to fix it, “they’re almost always wrong”. Sometimes they’re right, and you say “whoa, thank you”. Most of the time, take the important part, that it didn’t work for them, and know that their fix probably isn’t one: normally the problem is earlier in the text. Sometimes something needs to go away; sometimes something needs to be written to set something up. Accept in faith that something doesn’t work for people, but never take it as more than that information, “because if you try and fix things their way, you’ll be writing their story and you have to write yours.”

    It’s a division of labour. Readers are the instruments: they register where the story fails, and they’re never wrong about their own experience. Diagnosis and cure are the writer’s job, because only the writer knows what the story is about, the answer from move 3, and so which repairs serve it.

    Elsewhere in these notes“The problem is always earlier” is the heart of Gaiman’s lesson on getting stuck: the wrong turning is a couple of streets back, or a town or two (topic 32, move 4). It applies to a reader’s complaint just as it does to your own stall.

    5

    Perfect doesn’t happen

    You can’t fix a blank piece of paper

    In the lectureLong ago, Gaiman wanted Sandman coloured by Steve Whitaker, “really good colorist”, who had coloured V for Vendetta, and now dead. The job was Whitaker’s: all it took was colouring a few black-and-white pages to show the editor. Whitaker would show Gaiman beautiful pages, Gaiman would say “great, send them in”, and the answer was always “not yet, they’re not quite right, they’re not perfect”. The pages never came in, and DC Comics hired another colourist. “Perfect does not happen in this universe.” Perfection is the goal, the mountain we’re heading towards, “the gleaming crystal city on the hill”, and anything we do will contain its share of errors and mistakes; we can’t be crippled by that, or afraid of it. “If you write something, it can be improved. The problem is you cannot fix a blank piece of paper.” You can fix a story that doesn’t work, dialogue that isn’t quite there, a beginning, “but you cannot fix nothingness.” So be brave, just start, let the process carry you through, and be willing for it sometimes to land you on the rocks. Even as a teacher, “I’m not sure I have much more of an idea of what I’m doing now than I did when I started 30 something years ago”: some things will be better than others, and “I won’t necessarily know why.” But Gaiman has faith in the process. A story that isn’t written can never be one of the good ones, the loved ones; and fretting over the ones that disappoint feels like closing the door on the ones that turn out to be surprises: “I had no idea you were going to be so beautiful.”

    Beyond the lectureSteve Whitaker (1955–2008) was a British colourist and artist, best known for colouring V for Vendetta for its DC edition, who worked for Marvel, 2000 AD and DC, among others (Wikipedia). The story of the Sandman pages is Gaiman’s.

    Drill — something to fix

    Write the bad version of the scene you’ve been putting off, today, fast, knowing it’s bad. The only rule is that it has to exist by tonight.

    Tomorrow, fix one thing in it. Then another. You’re no longer facing a blank page; you’re editing, which is the easier job.

    Arc C · The rules

    4 moves
    6

    Write, and finish

    Rules one and two

    In the lectureGaiman once read an essay by Harlan Ellison pointing to an essay by Robert Heinlein, with rules for writers, rules for how to get published: “I read them and I believed them and I have applied them and they have worked for me.” Rule one: you have to write. If you don’t write, nothing will happen. Rule two: you have to finish what you write. Some people start and abandon things, or start and never let them go: “I know people who just really want to make something perfect so they never finish, they never let it go. You have to finish.”

    Elsewhere in these notesRule two is the one Gaiman learned the hard way: an attic full of first pages, until, at about twenty, Gaiman started finishing things (topic 9, move 6). And Ellison, whose essay passed the rules on, was one of the short-story writers Gaiman loved as a boy (topic 11, move 5).

    7

    Send it out

    Rule three, and an underrated kind of bravery

    In the lectureRule three: having finished it, you have to send it out into the world to somebody who could publish it. The internet broadens the scope, with “so many websites that actually want fiction”, and there are publishers and agents: “you send it out and that bit is important.” Gaiman talked earlier in the class, with Coraline, about bravery. Some acts everyone knows are brave: standing up to an armed robber, dealing with a wild dog attack. “People don’t normally list sending a story out into the world as one of those acts of bravery up there with standing up to armed robbers or wild dog attacks, but really, they really are.”

    Elsewhere in these notesThe earlier talk about bravery is the yellow-jacket nest, and Gaiman’s line between what was brave and what wasn’t (topic 3, move 4).

    8

    Hands off, then out again

    Rewrite only on request; when it comes back, send it again

    In the lectureThe next rule, Heinlein’s, is to refrain from rewriting except to editorial request: having sent it out, don’t start it again, don’t keep writing the same story over and over. Ellison added a note: unless you feel what the editor has asked for would compromise the integrity of the story, in which case don’t touch it, and defend it, “also perfectly valid”. Then: when it comes back, “because it will probably come back”, you have to send it out again. Not “my heart is broken, I will never write it again, I will put it away”; send it somewhere else. “There is somebody so drunk, so desperate, so confused that they will buy it”, or perhaps somebody with good enough taste and smart enough: whoever it is, keep sending it out until you reach that person.

    Beyond the lectureHeinlein’s rules come from the 1947 essay “On the Writing of Speculative Fiction”: “1. You must write. 2. You must finish what you start. 3. You must refrain from rewriting except to editorial order. 4. You must put it on the market. 5. You must keep it on the market until sold.” (Open Culture). Note the order: in the original, the rule against rewriting comes before sending the story out. It’s aimed at endless polishing as much as at editors, which makes it Steve Whitaker’s problem from move 5.

    9

    Start the next thing

    Gaiman’s own rule

    In the lectureAnd the last rule, “which is mine, it wasn’t Heinlein’s, it wasn’t Harlan’s”: and then start the next thing. Through all of this, when you finish the first thing and start sending it out, begin writing the next. Apart from anything else, when a publisher says “we love this novel, but what’s the sequel like?”, you can say “yes, here is the sequel, I finished that too”. Don’t write something, send it out, “and then wait for fame and fortune to come knocking on your door”: keep writing, keep finishing, and you’ll have more things to send out. “Those were Heinlein’s rules, they were Harlan Ellison’s rules, and I’m afraid they’re mine too, they work for me.”

    Try it — keep it on the market

    The rules as a tracker for your own stories: write, finish, send out, send out again, and always a next thing. Everything stays in this browser only.

      Beyond the lectureGaiman isn’t the only writer to add it. Robert J. Sawyer’s essay on Heinlein’s rules gives them a sixth, “Start Working on Something Else”: “As soon as you’ve finished one piece, start on another” (“Heinlein’s Rules”).

      Arc D · Two jobs at once

      3 moves
      10

      Author and first reader

      Both are true, at the same time

      In the lectureAs a writer, two things go on at the same time. One: you are doing it. You are all of your characters, everything they say, everything that happens. The other: you’re the first reader. When the magic is happening, “when the wind is beneath your wings”, it can feel as though you simply are the first reader, the first person ever to be here, unable to get the words down fast enough, describing something that is being put in front of you, with almost no volition, or none at all. “And that’s true. And it’s also true that it’s you doing it. And you must never forget that it’s you doing it.”

      11

      The bricklayer

      Word by word, a hundred thousand times

      In the lectureThere will be bad days and good days, and the bad days won’t feel like being the magical first reader. They’ll feel like being “a very tired bricklayer who is building a wall across a very, very long field”, brick by brick, which is word by word: what is the next word? Inspect the possible words, find one that fits better, put it in, “it’ll do”, and again, and again, knowing that a novel means doing that 100,000 times. Some days you’re too close to it to feel grand about responsibility for your characters; you’re just going “can you just say something that’s interesting?”, or “oh, I’ve done another one of those scenes where it’s two people in a room telling each other things they know already”. That’s part of the writing process too, and the frustration, with yourself and with the material, is okay.

      12

      It all sounds like you

      The galleys can’t tell the good days from the bad

      In the lecture“The weird thing, the magical thing, the absolutely unpredictable and unexpected thing”: once it’s edited and polished and handed in, three months later the publisher sends the galleys to go through. You’ll remember that some of the book was written on days when every word was like pulling teeth, “like writing through a migraine”, and some on magical days “when words dripped like diamonds from your fingers”. But there’s no way to tell the difference: “it all sounds like you”. It’s an incredibly humbling moment, “because frankly, you think one of the two should be better, but it really isn’t, it’s just the book you wrote.”

      This is the practical consequence of move 10. If the bricklayer days and the diamond days read the same, then a bad day is still a working day, and waiting for the magic is a mistake. The feeling of inspiration is real, but it isn’t visible on the page. The reader only sees the words, and the words are yours either way.

      Arc E · Rejection

      2 moves
      13

      Humility and arrogance

      Know you don’t know; believe you’re brilliant

      In the lectureYou’re always going to be rejected as a writer, “and that’s basically healthy”. The great thing about being a young writer is that you need, on one hand, the humility to know that you don’t know yet, and on the other, an arrogance “normally only seen in sort of seven-year-old boys”: the conviction that you are brilliant, that this is the greatest idea anybody’s ever had, and that by writing it you will set the world on fire, “because that is the engine that is going to get you through the stuff that actually needs to be written. You need to think you’re brilliant, it’s okay that you’re not.” People ask how to cope with rejection, and there are only two ways. One is to get sad, put the thing away, stop writing and go and get a real job. The other is a “kind of crazed attitude”: that the most important thing now is to write something so brilliant, so powerful, so good, that nobody could ever reject it.

      Elsewhere in these notesGaiman’s own early stories went out and came back, out and back, and the response then was to treat not knowing as the problem: Gaiman became a journalist, with a licence to ask questions (topic 3, move 7).

      14

      So good nobody can reject it

      A phone call to Harlan Ellison

      In the lectureGaiman’s friend J. Michael Straczynski, as a young writer, came to Hollywood wanting to sell stories and scripts, and nobody bought anything. So Straczynski looked up Harlan Ellison in the Los Angeles phone book, “because back then we had phone books”, rang Ellison, and asked for advice. Ellison, “very, very blunt, as was always Harlan’s way”, said the writing was crap: write really good stuff and it will sell. Then the phone went down. “And what was lovely” is that Straczynski took it and thought: I’ve got to get better, so good that nobody can reject anything I write, and went on to a long career in television and film. “But rejection either way is healthy.” If you’re starting out, it’s okay to be rejected. “Not quite right for us” is a really good phrase, because it’s true. The trick is to go on: “write the next thing, write the next thing, write the next thing.”

      Beyond the lectureStraczynski told the story in 1996. By that account Ellison answered “YEAH WHAT?”, and then: “It’s crap. Stop writing crap and you’ll sell. Anything else?” Straczynski said thank you, hung up, and, by Straczynski’s own account, hid in the basement for seven days (the post). The two later became friends. Straczynski went on to create Babylon 5, writing 92 of its 110 episodes (Wikipedia).

      Drill — not quite right for us

      Send one finished thing out this week, to somewhere that could actually publish it. Note where and when.

      When it comes back, send it somewhere else within a day, before the sting can talk you out of it. Then open the next thing and write a page.

      Arc F · A writer’s responsibility

      2 moves
      15

      What am I responsible for?

      A death that wasn’t what it seemed

      In the lectureOver the years Gaiman has given an enormous amount of thought to the responsibilities of a person who creates art, though not to begin with. Then a man was found dead, believed to be a suicide, with a note saying that the Sandman did it, that the Sandman would bring perfect peace, and a copy of Sandman beside the body. Gaiman had to ask: did I do this? “I had a very, very bad weekend.” A few days later it turned out not to be suicide: the man had been murdered by a boyfriend, who had written the note and put the comic there to make it look like a Sandman-inspired suicide, and who then also died, by suicide. “I felt like someone had tried to frame me for murder.” But the question it raised, what is my responsibility?, was huge and important, and Gaiman’s answer was: “my responsibility is to tell good stories, tell honest stories, tell them as well as I can, tell them to as many people as I can.” To encapsulate, as much as possible, “the things I believe, and the values that I believe”; to have a point of view, but to allow other points of view in; and “to be able to allow characters who I might not even want to associate with into my fiction, and to do them justice.” That, for Gaiman, has to be there at the bottom of everything.

      16

      A farce you can stand behind

      Good Omens, and darkness for children

      In the lectureSo Gaiman needs to be able to point at Good Omens and say: yes, “it’s a silly, funny farce about the end of the world, but absolutely, it has a message I can stand behind.” Human beings have to be responsible; we have to fix the world; war is very rarely a good idea, and avoiding war, which rarely gets praised, is huge and important; and “the greatest triumphs and the greatest tragedies of the human race are nothing to do with people being basically good, or people being basically evil. They’re all to do with people being basically people.” And Coraline is filled with scary stuff for kids. To people who think children should be kept safe from all the darkness, “good luck to you”; but if you keep young people safe from the darkness, then when the darkness shows up, “you are denying them tools or weapons that they might have needed and could have had.” It’s a book about bravery, about carrying on. Asked how anyone can write horror for a seven-year-old, Gaiman’s answer is to do it as well as possible, “because there are kids out there who need to know that they’ll get through this.”

      Good Omens (1990) was written with Terry Pratchett. Both answers have the same shape: a book can be a farce, or frightening, and still be responsible, as long as its author can say what it stands for.

      Elsewhere in these notesCoraline’s epigraph, that fairy tales tell us “dragons can be defeated” (topic 3, move 3), and the readers who tell Gaiman, quietly, at signings, that the book got them through the worst of their teens (topic 3, move 5). That is the responsibility of move 15 in practice.

      Arc G · Go and live

      2 moves
      17

      Write. Finish things.

      The answer that sounds flippant, and isn’t

      In the lecture“So you’ve just finished the class. Let me give you some advice.” The question Gaiman gets asked all the time: “I am an aspiring writer. What should I do?” The answer is meant absolutely seriously, though some think it’s flippant: “You should write.” And when people say they already write, what now? “You should finish things.” Some think it’s a joke. Others say “ah, yes, you have a very good point”, and go off and carry on writing, but start to finish things.

      18

      Go and get your heart broken

      Read, write, and do a lot of living

      In the lectureSometimes people need other advice. Brian K. Vaughan, a wonderful writer of Y: The Last Man and much else, came to Gaiman when very young: “I want to be a writer, and it isn’t happening.” As they talked, it became clear that Vaughan could write and could finish things, but “just didn’t have anything to say”. So Gaiman’s advice was different. Normally it would be just to write and finish things; this time it was: “Go out there into the world. Go get a job, go get fired from a job, go get your heart broken. See as much of the world as you can, find things, bump into things, get hurt. Come back in a few years’ time, and you will have a lot of things to write about.” And Vaughan did. Coming back, Vaughan asked: do you tell that to everybody? “No. I tell it to people every now and again, when it seems like that’s what they need.” But experience is good. Reading is fantastic: “Read everything you can, write everything you can, but do a lot of living, because everything that happens in the living is going to wind up in the fiction.” You’ll need every human being you ever meet and everything you see, “because one day you’ll write a story and you’ll realize that actually you can write this story, because once on a mountain top in China, a little man tried to sell you a pot of Snow White Honey.”

      Elsewhere in these notesThe compost heap where everything you meet rots down into stories (topic 5, move 1), and the notebook a writer keeps open even in the worst moments (topic 27, move 6). This last lesson ends where the class began: with the world, as material.
