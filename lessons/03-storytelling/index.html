Tell the truth — with things that never happened.

One argument, made six or seven different ways: fiction is lies, lies are how truth travels, and the lies only work if you were honest when you made them. Arc A · What a story is for

3 moves
1

We are storytelling creatures

The assumption the whole class stands on

In the lectureHuman beings are storytelling creatures. We tell stories. Stories are vital. Stories are important. We can go back later to why they’re vital, later to how they’re important, later to how long they’ve been around. The important thing to understand is that stories are part of us, and we convey truth with stories — which is the most gloriously giant contradiction you can ever imagine.

Notice what he refuses to do. Three times in a row he says the argument can wait: why they are vital, how they are important, how long they have been around — later. He is not making an evolutionary case or a psychological one. He is laying down a working assumption so the class can get on, and the assumption is not “stories are nice” but stories are the format truth arrives in.

The contradiction he flags is the real content, and it is worth holding on to because every later move is a consequence of it. We tell each other true things by describing events that did not occur. Not as decoration, not as a sugar coating on an argument that could have been made plainly — as the delivery mechanism itself. The lie is not the cost of the truth. The lie is how it moves.

Where it comes fromThe claim is old and well populated. Kenneth Burke, “Literature as Equipment for Living” (1938) — stories as tools you keep for situations you have not met yet. Barbara Hardy, “Towards a Poetics of Fiction” (1968) — narrative as “a primary act of mind”, not an art form layered on top of thinking. Brian Boyd, On the Origin of Stories (2009) and Jonathan Gottschall, The Storytelling Animal (2012) for the modern versions. Gaiman cites none of them, which is the right call for a first lesson — but if you want the “why” he keeps postponing, it is in those.

Drill — the story you were handed

Write down a story you were told before you were ten. Not a favourite — the first one that arrives.

Underneath it, write the one sentence of instruction it smuggled in. Not the moral printed at the end; the thing you actually took. Then ask whether anyone could have simply told you that sentence instead, and whether it would have stuck for thirty years.

2

Memorable lies

The mechanism, demonstrated on Little Red Riding Hood

In the lectureWe are using lies. We’re using memorable lies. We are taking people who do not exist and things that did not happen to those people in places that aren’t, and we are using those things to communicate true things. Tell a child Little Red Riding Hood and one takeaway is always taken: there are people out there who may not mean you well. When they say “where are you going, what are you doing” — you may not want to tell them. They might get your grandmother eaten. Some wolves are hairy on the inside, and some wolves are hairy on the outside.

The definition is precise enough to work with: people who do not exist, things that did not happen to them, in places that aren’t. Three separate falsehoods stacked, and the result is a true statement about strangers that a four-year-old will still be carrying at forty.

Then he does something easy to miss. Having claimed the story transmits truth, he immediately takes it apart in front of you: Little Red Riding Hood would walk in, look at the bed and say that is a wolf. She would not stand there asking about the eyes. And wolves cannot talk. He demolishes the plausibility of his own example — and the point survives, because the truth does not arrive in spite of the impossibility, it arrives without anyone checking. The reader knows the lie is a lie the entire time and takes the true thing anyway. That is the whole trick, and it means realism is not the price of being believed.

The takeaway he picks is also worth noting for what it is not. It is not “obey your mother” or “stay on the path”, the morals the story is usually flattened into. It is a piece of operational advice about information: when someone asks where you are going, you may not want to answer. Specific, usable, and not something children are generally told out loud.

Some wolves are hairy on the inside, and some wolves are hairy on the outside.

And the outside ones are not the problem

Where it comes fromCharles Perrault, “Le Petit Chaperon Rouge” (1697) — the oldest printed version, and it ends with the wolf winning. Perrault attaches a moral that is almost exactly Gaiman’s reading: the dangerous wolves are the ones who are charming, quiet, polite and sweet, who follow young women home, and those gentle wolves are the most dangerous of all. Three hundred years early, and about people, not animals. The Grimms (1812) added the huntsman and the rescue. — On lies carrying truth: Picasso, “art is a lie that makes us realise truth” (1923); Camus, fiction as “the lie through which we tell the truth”; Stephen King, On Writing — “fiction is the truth inside the lie”.

Watch for“Hairy on the inside” is not Gaiman’s. It is Angela Carter — the grandmother’s warning in The Company of Wolves (Carter’s wolf stories in The Bloody Chamber, 1979, and the 1984 Neil Jordan film she co-wrote). He is almost certainly quoting knowingly, to an audience he expects to catch it, but the line gets repeated as his. Attribute it to Carter.

Drill — build one backwards

Write one true sentence you actually believe about how people behave. Keep it small and unfashionable — the kind of thing you would hesitate to say at a table.

Now invent the shortest possible sequence of events that would make a reader arrive at that sentence without you stating it. People who do not exist, in a place that isn’t. If you find yourself needing to write the sentence down at the end, the events are not doing their job yet.

3

Dragons can be beaten

The epigraph he made up and blamed on G.K. Chesterton

In the lectureI began Coraline with a quote that wasn’t from G.K. Chesterton, although I said it was. I said: fairy tales aren’t true, fairy tales are more than true — not because they tell us that dragons exist, but because they tell us that dragons can be defeated. That, for me, was the important thing about Coraline. I wanted to tell my kids something that had taken me thirty years of living to figure out.

Two things are going on here and only one of them is the joke about the fake quotation.

The first is the sentence itself, which reframes what a frightening story does. Children already know about the monster; nobody has to introduce the idea. What the story supplies is the part they do not have — evidence that the thing can lose. On that reading a scary book for children is not an indulgence to be justified, it is the only form in which the reassurance can be delivered, because reassurance without a credible monster reassures nobody.

The second is the working method, and it is the more useful of the two. He knew what the book was for before he knew what the book was. One sentence, put at the front, doing the job of a brief: it took him thirty years to learn, he wanted his children to have it sooner, and so a novel got written. That is a far more tractable starting point than a premise or a character, because you can check every chapter against it.

Where it comes fromThe real Chesterton is Tremendous Trifles (1909), in the essay “The Red Angel”: fairy tales do not give the child his first idea of a bogey — the child has known the dragon intimately ever since he had an imagination. What the fairy tale gives him is his first clear idea of the possible defeat of the bogey; it provides a St George to kill the dragon. Gaiman’s line is a compression of that, and a good one — which is presumably why it escaped and now circulates worldwide as a Chesterton quotation. He has owned up to it repeatedly, including here, and it keeps not sticking.

Watch forHe says “defeated”; the epigraph as printed in Coraline reads “beaten”. He is quoting himself from memory. If you are citing it, use the book.

Drill — write the brief

For the thing you are working on now, write the one sentence the book is for. One sentence, no semicolons, no “and also”. Something you had to live to learn rather than something you read.

Then take your last three scenes and, for each, answer in a word or two how it earns that sentence. Any scene that cannot answer is either the wrong scene or evidence the sentence is wrong. Both are useful findings.

The yellow jacket nest

How the second half of Coraline got written

In the lectureI’d written a third of Coraline, then we moved to America, everything got busier, there was no free time and nobody was waiting for it — a publisher had told me it was absolutely and utterly unpublishable. Then I was walking with my children in the woods and trod on a yellow jacket nest. I shouted at them to get away and stayed exactly where I was, because if I ran after them the cloud would follow me and get to them. I waited till they were at the top of the hill, getting stung the whole time, then ran. I put all three of us in the bath and counted: about 39 stings for me, one or two each for them. My glasses had gone flying. Which meant the next day I had to go back.

⅓of the book written
39stings, counted in the bath
1trip back, the next morning

The anecdote is good, but the craft is entirely in the distinction he draws afterwards, and he draws it hard:

Not brave

Standing still in a cloud of wasps while your children run, taking thirty-nine stings. “There was absolutely nothing brave about it.”

Brave

Walking back to the same patch of ground the next morning, on purpose, to look for a pair of glasses. “That was terrifying. That was brave.”

The first was not a choice. There was one available move and he made it; fear had nothing to decide. The second was entirely optional, entirely informed, and nothing was chasing him. Courage is not what you do when there is no alternative — it is what you do when there is one, and you know exactly what it costs.

Then the sentence the book had been waiting for: being brave doesn’t mean you’re not scared — it means you’re scared, and you do the right thing, and you do it anyway. He calls what it gave him a handle, which is exactly right. It is not a theme in the essay sense. It is a grip: something to hold the remaining two-thirds by.

And it converts straight into structure. Coraline’s first crossing into the other flat is curiosity — she does not know. The second, after her parents are taken, is deliberate, informed, and unforced. The plot had to make her go back. Which is to say the glasses in the grass are not an illustration of the theme, they are the blueprint for act two: the return trip is the one that counts, and it has to be frightening or it proves nothing.

Standing there, getting stung, while my kids ran away, was not scary. Going back the next day to find my glasses — that was brave.

The moment a half-finished book found out what it was about

Where it comes fromCoraline (Bloomsbury / HarperCollins, 2002) — won the Hugo, Nebula and Bram Stoker awards for best novella. The two-crossing structure is on the page: the door is opened more than once, and the difference between the openings is the book. — The definition of courage is standard but rarely stated this cleanly: Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics III, defines courage as requiring fear in the first place — a man who feels nothing is not brave but insensible. The much-quoted modern phrasing, “courage is not the absence of fear but the judgement that something else is more important”, is Ambrose Redmoon (1991), not Twain or Mandela whatever the internet says.

Drill — sort your own two columns

Write two short lists. In danger: times something bad was happening to you and you reacted. Afraid: times nothing was happening yet, you had a choice, and you took the harder one anyway.

The first list is where anecdotes come from. The second list is where themes come from. If the thing you are writing has a climax drawn from list one, you have written an incident; the book is still looking for its second crossing.

A giant lie that carried people

Twenty years of signing queues

In the lectureEvery event I do, every signing I do, there is a woman in her late teens, in her twenties, now even in her thirties, with a battered school edition of Coraline. They stop and they tell me very quietly that the book saved their lives in their teens — when they were being abused, when their parents’ marriage was falling apart, bad things happening at school. They’d tell themselves that if Coraline could get through what Coraline got through, they could get through that. But the best thing is: I told them a giant lie. A girl who doesn’t exist, a door that doesn’t exist, an other mother with buttons for eyes who definitely doesn’t exist. None of it is true. But it helps. It carries them through. It is true.

Read what those readers actually did with the book, because it is not what people usually mean by comfort. They did not find it soothing. They used the plot as evidence. If she got through that, I can get through this is an argument with a worked example in it — and a worked example is the one thing advice cannot supply. “You will survive this” is a claim. A hundred and sixty pages in which someone does is a demonstration.

Which retroactively justifies how frightening the book is, and settles the argument about scary children’s books in one move: the reassurance is only worth as much as the threat it is measured against. Make the other mother safe and the book proves nothing. The button eyes are load-bearing.

He lands on the contradiction from move 1, now with twenty years of evidence behind it: everything in there was made up by me, it’s not true. But it helps. It carries them through. It is true. Same sentence, both ways, no resolution offered — because there isn’t one, and that is the point.

One more thing, quietly in there. He does not say he wrote it for them. He wrote it for his own children, aimed at one sentence about dragons, from a wasp sting in a wood. The readers he is describing were not a target audience; they were a consequence of the thing being honestly made.

Where it comes fromBruno Bettelheim, The Uses of Enchantment (1976) is the famous case for exactly this — children need the frightening version of the tale, and sanitised versions leave them without equipment. Treat it carefully: Bettelheim’s clinical claims and his scholarship have both been seriously challenged (Jack Zipes, Breaking the Magic Spell, 1979, and the later questions about his credentials and his borrowings). The narrower claim — that a story works as a rehearsal you get to survive — stands without him.

Drill — write for one person on their worst day

Pick a specific reader. Not a demographic — a person, at an age, in a room, on a bad evening. Someone you could name.

Now find the page in your draft that would matter to them, that night. If there isn’t one, you are writing something to be admired rather than something to be used. Write that page next, and let it be as frightening as it needs to be — anything you soften, you also make useless.

As honest as you can make it

The only rule he states as a rule — and immediately qualifies

In the lectureAll fiction has to be as honest as you can make it. This, I believe. This may not be true for every other writer out there. It may not be true for any other writer out there, but it’s true for this writer. You have to make it as honest as you can, because that’s what people respond to. Any success I have achieved as a writer of fiction, I have achieved because I’m an honest writer of fiction — because my people are real people, because you care about them.

Watch the hedge, because it is deliberate and it is the difference between a teacher and a guru. He states the law, then removes its universality in two sentences: may not be true for every other writer, may not be true for any other writer, is true for this writer. He is handing you a thing that worked for one person and declining to claim more. Everything else on this page should be read at that setting.

The word doing the work is honest, and it needs pinning down because three obvious readings are all wrong. It does not mean accurate — the entire argument so far is that the events are invented. It does not mean confessional — that comes later, and is a means, not the thing. And it does not mean nice.

What it means is given by the sentence he attaches to it: my people are real people, because you care about them. Honesty is a property of the writing at the level of the individual choice — not making a character behave the way the plot needs them to, not resolving something because the chapter is running long, not giving someone a good line they would not have. Every dishonest sentence in fiction is a small convenience taken at a character’s expense, and the reader feels each one as a slight loss of care. That is why it is a commercial argument as much as an artistic one, and he frames it that way: that’s what people respond to.

Where it comes fromHemingway, A Moveable Feast — “all you have to do is write one true sentence. Write the truest sentence that you know.” Same instruction, and the same slippery word. John Gardner, The Art of Fiction (1983) supplies the mechanism: fiction works as a “vivid and continuous dream”, and what breaks the dream is not implausibility but the author becoming visible — showing off, taking shortcuts, arranging. Dishonesty is the author’s thumb on the scale, and the reader always sees the thumb.

Drill — the deletion test

Open your last finished scene and find the sentence you wrote to be admired. It is usually the best-sounding one. It is often a simile.

Delete it. Read the scene again. If nothing is lost, that sentence was you, visible in the frame. Repeat once per scene — there is almost always exactly one.

Then the harder half: find the moment a character did what was convenient rather than what they would have done. That one you cannot fix by deleting.

A licence to ask questions

What he did about the rejections

In the lectureThe hardest time for me was starting out as a very, very young writer. I wrote short stories and sent them to places that could conceivably publish them, and they all came back. Out and back, out and back. And I thought: okay, one of two things is true here. Either I’m not good enough, or I don’t understand the world — the stuff I don’t get, the stuff I need to know. And I thought: okay, as of today I am now a freelance journalist specialising in publishing and fantasy and science fiction. I decided I was a journalist because that gives me licence to ask questions. Licence to go out into that world, meet everybody, find out who everybody is, what they do, how they read. To learn.

The diagnosis is a fork with two branches and he notices that only one of them is actionable. Not good enough is either false or unfixable today. Don’t understand the world is a gap, and gaps can be walked into. He does not establish which one was true — he takes the branch you can do something about. That is a repeatable move well beyond writing.

Then the part worth stealing outright: he issued himself the credential. No commission, no masthead, no permission. “As of today I am now a freelance journalist” — and the reason given is not income or clips, it is licence to ask questions. A job title is a socially acceptable reason to phone a stranger and take an hour of their time, and the hour is the thing he was after. Every question a young writer would be embarrassed to ask becomes routine when the asking is the job.

Note also what he went to find out: who everybody is, what they do, how they read. Not who could help him. That is reconnaissance, not networking, and the difference shows up in what you come home with.

Where it comes fromIt worked, and the paper trail is public: through the 1980s Gaiman wrote journalism and interviews across British magazines, and the apprenticeship produced two commissioned books — a Duran Duran biography (1984) and Don’t Panic: The Official Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy Companion (1988) — before the fiction arrived. The interviewing also got him into the rooms where Sandman later became possible.

Drill — issue yourself the title

Write down the question you would most like answered by someone already doing the thing you want to do, and the reason you have not asked it.

Now invent the smallest true title that makes asking normal — a newsletter with eleven subscribers, a blog, a podcast with no episodes yet, “I’m writing something about…”. Make it true this week by doing the minimum that makes it true, then send three emails. Ask what they do and how they read. Do not ask for help.

He had been reading it wrong

Milford, with Gwyneth Jones, Diana Wynne Jones and Mary Gentle

In the lectureThen I got invited to my first writers’ workshop. I was sitting there with a bunch of fantastic writers — Gwyneth Jones, Diana Wynne Jones, Mary Gentle. And I realised very quickly that my reactions to stories were things like: I like this, I don’t like this, this is good. That wasn’t their reaction. They were responding on a much deeper level. They were reading different stories to what I was reading. I was reading as an audience. They were reading as craftspeople, as people who built these things. And I also realised I was wrong. I was wrong if I wanted to be a writer. And they were right. That experience, more than anything else, changed me.

“They were reading different stories to what I was reading” is the exact phrasing and it is better than the usual way of putting this. Not reading the same story more carefully — reading a different object. Where he saw events and people, they saw a machine: what is being withheld and for how long, whose scene this is, where the weight was put, what the writer tried and abandoned.

The second qualifier matters as much as the first. I was wrong if I wanted to be a writer. Audience-reading is not a defect; it is what books are for, and it is the response every writer is actually working to produce. It is simply the wrong instrument for the job of making one. Most people never acquire the second mode, and the ones who acquire it badly lose the first and spend their lives unable to enjoy anything.

The practical form of the upgrade is narrow and testable: can you say how a passage works without saying whether you liked it? “This is good” is a verdict and teaches nobody anything, including you. “She has withheld the name for four pages and the paragraph gets shorter every time she nearly says it” is a mechanism, and mechanisms can be borrowed.

They were reading different stories to what I was reading.

The same pages. A different object.

Where it comes fromMilford — the original writers’ conference was founded in Milford, Pennsylvania in 1956 by Damon Knight, Judith Merril and James Blish; Blish moved to England and the British Milford SF Writers’ Conference has run annually since 1972. Peer critique by working writers, no tutor at the front. It is the ancestor of Clarion and of most workshop culture since. — The skill itself has a standard text: Francine Prose, Reading Like a Writer (2006). Samuel Delany’s criticism (About Writing, The Jewel-Hinged Jaw) is the harder, better version. — Diana Wynne Jones became a lasting friend and influence; the debt turns up across Gaiman’s children’s books and in what he has written about her since her death in 2011.

Drill — ban the verdict

Take a chapter you love and reread it with a pen. Write twenty observations. Opinion words are forbidden — good, bad, beautiful, boring, loved, hated, clever.

Only mechanics: what is on the page, what is withheld, where the paragraph breaks fall, which sense is used, how long since we were told the weather, what a scene would lose if it started one line later. When the list stops being painful you have the second mode.

Do it once more with something you dislike. That one teaches faster, because the machinery is visible from outside.

Every voice, and nothing to say

The most exact self-diagnosis in the whole lesson

In the lectureUp until that point I had a facility with voices. I could do, essentially, impressions of other writers. I could write things that felt kind of like things that other writers would have written, and written well. But I didn’t have anything to say. And that wasn’t because I hadn’t lived. That was because I wasn’t really prepared to say anything true about who I was. I didn’t want to be judged. I didn’t want people reading any of my stories to know who I was, or what I thought, or to get in too close.

This is the sentence to keep. It separates two things that are almost always confused, and getting them the wrong way round costs people years.

The received explanation for having nothing to say is that you have not lived enough — go and have experiences, get older, travel, suffer a bit. He rules it out in seven words: that wasn’t because I hadn’t lived. The material was already there. The shortage was never of material. It was an unwillingness to spend it.

And pastiche is what fills the gap in the meantime. Note that he is not describing incompetence — the impressions were good, the sentences were well made. That is precisely what makes it a trap: it produces publishable-looking work indefinitely, it improves with practice, and from the inside it feels like a career developing. Facility is what competence looks like when it is being used as cover. If you can write convincingly in four borrowed voices, you have a technical gift and a hiding place, and only you can tell which one is currently in use.

The three things he did not want readers to know are worth listing separately, because they escalate: who I was, what I thought, to get in too close. The first is biography, the second is opinion, and the third is the real one — proximity. He did not want to be near anybody on the page.

Drill — the two pages

Page one: write a page in the voice you imitate best. Enjoy it; do it well. Notice how easy it is, and how quickly it arrives.

Page two: the same event, the same characters, with no voice. No cadence borrowed from anyone, no stylistic signature, nothing you would be complimented on. Only what you know to be true about how that event would actually go.

Page two will be worse and it will be yours. The question is not which is better today — it is which one could still be improving in five years.

Walking down the street naked

The price, and what actually happened when he paid it

In the lectureYou had to be willing to do the equivalent of walking down a street naked. You had to be able to show too much of yourself. You had to be just a little bit more honest than you were comfortable with. And if people judged you, if they felt they knew who you were, that was just something you were going to have to live with. What was strange is that once I started doing it, I was expecting to be judged, or shunned — and what I discovered was their opinions were: we really like this, we love this story, that’s a good story. It felt huge. It felt personal. And I realised that’s because I was being honest about me.

The useful part of this is not the metaphor, it is that he turns discomfort into an instrument. A little bit more honest than you were comfortable with is a reading you can take. You already know where the line is — the flinch is unmistakable — and the instruction is to go a short distance past it, deliberately, every time. Not far past. A little. The gauge is calibrated in exactly one unit and the unit is your own reluctance.

Now the honest accounting, because it is easy to read this as a promise and it is not one. He did not say the judgement would not come. He said: if people judged you, that was something you were going to have to live with. The agreement is made in advance and without terms. What came back happened to be warmth — we love this story — but that is the outcome he reports, not the deal he made. Anyone selling this as “be vulnerable and you will be loved” has dropped the clause that makes it work.

And the reason the warmth felt huge and personal is structural, not sentimental: praise for a borrowed voice lands on the voice. There is nobody home to receive it. Praise for something you actually meant reaches you, which is also exactly why it was frightening to send.

You had to be just a little bit more honest than you were comfortable with.

Discomfort as a measuring instrument

Where it comes fromHe has put this more sharply elsewhere — in the “Make Good Art” commencement address (University of the Arts, Philadelphia, 2012), it is stated as a rule of thumb: the moment you feel that, just possibly, you are walking down the street naked, exposing too much of your heart and your mind and what exists on the inside, showing too much of yourself — that is the moment you may be starting to get it right. Worth watching alongside this lesson; it is the same idea with the gauge made explicit.

Drill — the drawer paragraph

Write the paragraph you would not want one named person to read. Name them before you start — the whole point is that the reluctance is specific.

Put it in a drawer for a week without rereading it. Then read it cold and ask one question: is it embarrassing, or is it just true? Embarrassing usually means you were still performing, at a different angle. True goes in the book.

You do not have to publish it. You do have to be able to write it.

11

Specific is universal

Why strangers said you wrote my childhood

In the lectureSome things, when you get really specific, apply to so many of us. … Once it was published, over and over I had people coming up to me, writing to me, saying: you wrote my childhood. None of those things happened to me, my childhood wasn’t like that, but you wrote about my childhood, you wrote about me. And I did — because I was writing about me, because I was being honest, because I was being specific. If you’re specific, then what you say that’s true applies to other people too.

Backwards, and the technical centre of the whole lesson. The intuition says that to reach many readers you should write something broad enough to cover them; the report from the signing queue says the opposite, and says it in the reader’s own words: none of those things happened to me — you wrote my childhood.

The mechanism is straightforward once stated. A general sentence — “he had a difficult childhood” — gives the reader a label, and a label can only be agreed with. One particular lane, one particular drain, one particular book read under one particular blanket while a particular row went on downstairs has a shape, and a shape can be matched. The reader is not comparing facts; they are recognising a structure — the specific relief, the specific kind of watchfulness. They can only find that structure if the details are sharp enough for it to have one.

So: generality invites agreement, specificity invites recognition, and only one of those feels like being seen. Which also means vagueness is not a safe middle. It is the one choice guaranteed to reach nobody, while looking from the inside like reaching everybody.

Where it comes fromFlannery O’Connor, Mystery and Manners (1969) is the flintiest statement of it: fiction operates through the senses, begins in the concrete, and the writer’s problem is nearly always an unwillingness to be specific enough for long enough. Nabokov: caress the details. Chekhov’s moonlight on the broken bottle-neck. — The line often given as Tolstoy’s — “describe your village and you will be universal” — is unsourced and probably apocryphal; the idea is real, the attribution is not. Use O’Connor.

Drill — name everything

Take a page of your draft and underline every general noun: a car, a dog, a school, a song, a shop, some trees.

Replace each with one that could be identified — the make and the rust, the breed and what was wrong with it, the exact song and who was allowed to play it. Do not add sentences; swap words.

Then read it aloud. The test is not whether it sounds vivid. It is whether a stranger could now be wrong about a detail and still feel the page was about them.

The seven-year-old is already in your armoury

The Ocean at the End of the Lane — his most personal novel

In the lectureIt’s the one that really is about a seven-year-old me, and it was a very uncomfortable book to write, because it had to be too honest about who I was, about what I felt when I was that age. The great thing about being an adult writing from the perspective of a seven-year-old boy is that I was a seven-year-old boy. I have that in my armoury. I didn’t need to go out and interview seven-year-old boys. The family wasn’t really my family, but the house and the environment and the place were my house, my environment, my place. And the more I wrote remembering what it was like to be seven, the more being seven came back to me. Things I hadn’t thought about for forty years, for fifty years, came flooding back.

Two separate claims, and both are practical.

The writing is the retrieval

The usual order is: remember, then write. He describes the opposite — writing from the little he had brought back more, and the more he wrote the more arrived, things untouched for half a century. So “I don’t remember enough to write this” is not a verdict, it is a description of the state before starting. The memory is not a warehouse you visit beforehand; it opens as you work.

His analogy for the sensation is exact. Going back to a town you left thirty or forty years ago: at first everything is strange and you are sure you have lost it, and then — hang on, if you go down here, that’s that road — and the spaces start making sense to each other again. You didn’t even think you knew it, but it’s in there waiting for you. It comes back as connections between places, not as facts, which is also how to go looking for it: not “what happened” but “what was next to what”.

What he swapped and what he kept

The division is deliberate and it is a usable rule. The family was invented. The house, the environment, the place were real. He needed freedom in the part that had to carry the plot, and he needed unforgeable texture in the part that had to be believed. Invent where you need to move people around; keep the real where you need the reader to stop questioning.

And “I have that in my armoury” is doing quiet work against a common piece of advice. Research is one way in. Having been there is another, it is free, and it is generally better — on the condition set in move 10, which is that you are willing to be uncomfortable about it. He says twice that the book was hard to write. That is the same cost as the drawer paragraph, paid at novel length.

Where it comes fromThe Ocean at the End of the Lane (2013), which won Book of the Year at the British National Book Awards — a short novel set in the Sussex countryside of his own childhood, narrated by an adult recovering a version of himself at seven. Gaiman has said elsewhere that it began as a short story for his wife, and that he did not realise it was a novel until he was well inside it — which fits the retrieval account above exactly.

Drill — the floor plan

Draw, badly, the floor plan of a room you were in at seven. Doors, windows, where the light came from.

Then name ten objects in it. Not ten interesting objects — the first ten, including the boring ones. Where the plug was. What was on top of the wardrobe.

Only then write. Not about the room: about something that happened in it. The point of the plan is that you will find yourself knowing things you did not know you had, and they will arrive as adjacencies — this was next to that, which means she must have been standing there.

Truth as a condiment

The technique the whole lesson has been circling

In the lectureWriting yourself, going back to your own past and visiting your own past — as a writer, you’re not writing autobiography. What you’re doing is lying, but you’re using the truth in order to make your lies convincing and true. You’re using them as seasoning. You’re using the truth as a condiment to make an otherwise unconvincing narrative absolutely credible. It is vitally important that whatever engines you might have used to convince your teacher that your homework really was eaten by the dog now come out to convince the reader that your father really was having an affair with an extraterrestrial monster from another dimension who is about to destroy the earth.

The ratio is the instruction. Seasoning, not the meal. Autobiography with the names changed is not what he is describing and he says so first, to close it off. The true material is deployed in small quantities, at the points where credit is needed, and spent immediately on something impossible.

The mix, as he describes it
Invented — the narrative, the people, the eventsTrue

The small green part is what the reader tastes and cannot argue with. It pays for all the rest.

The homework line is the best thing in the lesson and it is not a joke. It says the skill is not new — you have had it since you were eight. Take apart what a child actually does when lying well and you get the working method: one detail too specific to be invented, a small amount of independently checkable truth wrapped around the unverifiable claim, complete confidence in delivery, and no over-explaining. That is the same apparatus, pointed at a reader instead of a teacher, and the claim being smuggled through is now an extraterrestrial monster.

Which is why the arc ends here rather than at “be honest”. The honesty from moves 6 to 11 is not a virtue the class is recommending. It is the supply of true material, and this move is what it is for.

Whatever engines you used to convince your teacher that your homework really was eaten by the dog now come out to convince the reader that your father really was having an affair with an extraterrestrial monster from another dimension.

You have had the skill since you were eight

Where it comes fromJ.R.R. Tolkien, “On Fairy-Stories” (lecture 1939, essay 1947) gives the theory: the reader is not suspending disbelief but granting secondary belief, which holds only while the invented world stays internally consistent — and breaks the moment the writer is caught being careless. — The technique is old and shameless: Defoe’s Journal of the Plague Year (1722) with its invented parish statistics, Swift’s exact latitudes, M.R. James burying one ghost in a page of dry antiquarian detail. Verifiable detail is the collateral; the impossible thing is the loan.

Drill — five true things

Write five true details from your own life. Sensory and checkable: a smell, a real name, the actual weather that week, a physical habit someone had, the thing on top of the wardrobe from move 12.

Now write a short scene in which nothing that happens is true — and place all five details inside it. Put at least one in the first three sentences, before the reader has decided whether to trust you.

Read it back and find the moment you stopped doubting it. That moment is the condiment working, and it will usually be one of the five.
