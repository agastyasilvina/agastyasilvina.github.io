The eighth lesson from this class, and the most practical: what to do when the writing stops. Gaiman doesn’t believe in writer’s block, at least not the kind you can do nothing about. Being stuck is another matter: it has causes, and the causes can be found. Walk away; come back as a stranger; look for the wrong turning, which is always further back than the place you stalled; and ask whether the stall is telling you to dig deeper. Then two stories from Gaiman’s own desk, a novel stopped for two months and a short story written in a day, and a last image for the long haul: driving through fog.

Arc A · Stuck, not blocked

3 moves
1

The fancy word

Writer’s block sounds real, and sounds hopeless

In the lectureStaring at a blank page or a blank screen, the biggest thing you can do is give in to despair, to blankness. People love to talk about writer’s block because “it sounds fancy, it sounds like a real thing, it also sounds like something that you can do nothing about”: I have writer’s block, I cannot write, and it is the will of the gods. Now I must alphabetize my spice rack. That isn’t true. Gaiman has had long emails from people with writer’s block, and thinks: “if you had real writer’s block, how can you be writing me a long email?” What they’re actually saying is: I’m stuck on the thing, I don’t know what’s happening, “it’s dead on the page”.

The difference matters because of what each word lets you do. A block is a condition, like weather, and you wait for it to lift. Being stuck is a situation, like a car in a ditch: something put you there, and something can get you out. The rest of the lesson is about finding the something.

Elsewhere in these notesGaiman’s teenage drafts died the same way: a hundred or two hundred first pages in the attic, each of which would “sputter and flame out and be dead” three pages in (topic 9, move 6). The cure there was to start finishing things; this lesson is about how.

2

Start one step away

Don’t stare at it: go and do something else

In the lectureSo, one: start one step away. If you’re actually stuck, “don’t just sit there staring at the page, staring at the screen, staring at your keyboard, being angry, go do something else.” Go for a walk, a run, a swim; go and garden, “go play with small children”, go feed the chickens: “go do whatever it is that you can do.”

Notice what the list has in common. None of it is writing, none of it is another screen, and almost all of it occupies the body while leaving the mind loose. It isn’t giving up on the story. It’s leaving the story alone long enough for something to move.

Beyond the lecturePsychologists call this incubation: setting a stuck problem aside for a while before coming back to it. A meta-analysis of the experiments, by Ut Na Sio and Thomas Ormerod, found a real effect, larger when people had worked on the problem for longer first, and smaller when the break was filled with demanding work. On one kind of problem, an undemanding activity during the break beat simple rest (“Does incubation enhance problem solving?”, Psychological Bulletin, 2009). A walk, a garden, chickens: undemanding.

3

Read it as a stranger

The old pretend-you’ve-never-read-it technique

In the lectureTwo: come back pretending you’ve never read it before, “the old pretend you’ve never read it before technique”. Start at the beginning and read it through. Very, very often, once you do that, “where the story should be becomes obvious. Where you went off the rails becomes obvious and you did go off the rails.”

Reading from the beginning matters as much as reading fresh. Stuck writers tend to stare at the last page, where the trouble showed up. Reading from the top lets you meet the story the way a reader will, with the momentum of everything before, and so feel where that momentum first slackens.

Drill — read it cold

Take something you stopped writing. Leave it at least a day. Then read it from the first line, at reading speed, without a pen, as if a stranger had handed it to you.

Make one mark only: the first place you caught yourself wanting to skim. That’s your best candidate for where it went off the rails, and it’s usually earlier than where you stopped.

Arc B · A town or two back

2 moves
4

The problem is always earlier

The car leaves the road here; the wrong turning was streets back

In the lecture“The problem is always earlier”: earlier than the place where the car goes off the road and you’re stuck. “You actually took a wrong turning a couple of streets back or a town or two back.” You can normally see it if you come to the story from the beginning, and suddenly you’re going: “well hang on, why are we with him anyway? She was much more interesting and we should be with her here.” So Gaiman abandons the half a chapter that had led down a dead end, and goes back. You can do that: “Nobody but you ever gets to read your first draft. Nobody but you ever needs to know that you got stuck.”

Try it — find the wrong turning

Three stalled stories, outlined beat by beat. Each stops where it says stuck. Read from the top as if you’d never seen it, then tap the beat where it took the wrong turning.

    The example in the lecture is worth stealing whole. The fix wasn’t a better sentence, a cleverer scene or a twist. It was a question about whose story this is, and the honest answer sent half a chapter to the bin. That’s the scale of repair a wrong turning usually needs: not patching where you stalled, but going back to where you chose the wrong road, and choosing again.

    5

    Stuck can mean go deeper

    Why have I stopped? Maybe because it’s darker in there

    In the lectureThere’s a whole bunch of things you can do when you’re stuck. “Sometimes it’s just you’re being shallow. Sometimes you actually need to be a bit better than you are. Go a bit deeper. Get a bit more honest.” Ask: why have I stopped writing this? Perhaps because if you have to go into it, it’s a deeper, darker place. Perhaps because if you wrote that scene, you would be writing a scene you don’t want to write. “Why don’t you want to write it? Why is it painful for you? What happens if you do write it?”

    Try it — the stuck kit

    The lesson’s three moves, in order, for a story of yours that has died on the page. Your notes stay in this browser only.

    1. Step one away.
    2. Come back as a stranger. Read from the very beginning. Where did you first stop believing it, and whose story was it there?
    3. Ask why you stopped.

    Elsewhere in these notesTwo neighbours of this idea. Gaiman’s complaint about young writers who swerve away from the place “where your two characters are meant to collide” (topic 10, move 10, “Don’t flinch”). And Margaret Atwood’s advice for a writer who can’t start: you’re afraid of something, so identify the fear and look it in the face (topic 13, move 10).

    Arc C · When the plot says kill

    2 moves
    6

    The most efficient thing

    Halfway through a comedy, the plot wants a murder

    In the lectureWith Anansi Boys, Gaiman was about halfway through, maybe a little more. One character was going to see another, the plot was running, everything was good. Then: “hang on, if she goes to see him at this point in the plot, he’s just going to kill her. That’s the most efficient thing for him to do.” But: “I’m writing a comedy. People can’t kill each other in comedies.” There’s no point in P. G. Wodehouse where Jeeves picks up an ashtray and smashes it on Bertie Wooster’s head: “You don’t do that. That’s wrong.” So Gaiman had to stop, and did.

    This is being stuck from the other side. Nothing has gone off the rails here: the logic of the characters is working perfectly, and it leads somewhere the genre seems to forbid. The villain has every reason to do the efficient thing. Pretending not to notice would make the villain stupid and the book a cheat.

    Beyond the lectureAnansi Boys (2005) is the comic novel about Fat Charlie Nancy, the son of Anansi, the trickster god of West African and Caribbean stories. In the finished book, a confrontation does end in murder: Grahame Coats, Charlie’s employer, kills Maeve Livingstone, whose late husband’s royalties Coats has been stealing (Wikipedia). And the book stays a comedy: Maeve carries on as a ghost, set on revenge. Whether that is the scene Gaiman means, the lesson doesn’t say.

    7

    What each character needs

    Two months off, and a new idea of comedy

    In the lectureGaiman stopped the book for a couple of months, thinking: this is going to happen, so what does it mean for the rest of the plot? Where does the plot need to go? How can something be a comedy? What is the difference between tragedy and comedy, and between horror and comedy? The answer Gaiman reached: “as long as each character gets what they need, at the end of the story, it’s fine.” That is what Gaiman tried to deliver in Anansi Boys: “Each of the characters gets the thing they need, even the appalling ones. Sometimes what they need is appalling, and sometimes what happens to them is appalling.” Gaiman had to be willing to follow that, to learn it, to stop and figure it out, and to “re-plot and re-calibrate”: “Once I felt that I got it and it was part of me, then I started again and wrote it all the way to the end.”

    Two things to take from this. First, the time: two months is long, and it was spent thinking about the story, not away from it, until the question was answered for the whole book and not just for one scene. Second, the answer was a rule about endings, not about the scene that caused the trouble. Being stuck in the middle was solved by deciding what the end owed every character.

    Elsewhere in these notesAsking what your characters want is Gaiman’s flashlight for plotting: when you’re stuck, it lights the road ahead (topic 10, move 12). Here the question becomes what they need, which isn’t always what they want, and sometimes is appalling.

    Arc D · A story in a day

    3 moves
    8

    Dying on the page

    An alien tourist’s diary, and 24 hours left

    In the lectureGaiman was asked for a short story for an anthology of science fiction for young people, and had an idea: an alien tourist, a girl from another planet, “somebody from almost a way of thinking that is beyond what we thought”, visiting Earth, and her diary. Gaiman wrote a few openings and a few bits, “and it kept dying on the page. It wasn’t a thing yet.” The alien’s voice and her weird world were interesting, but it didn’t feel like a story. So Gaiman phoned the editor, Jonathan Strahan: “Jonathan, I’m really sorry, but the story isn’t going to happen.” Strahan said that was a pity, and then: “You still have 24 hours to get the story in.” Another writer, whom Gaiman first declines to name and then names, Kelly Link, had just written a story for Strahan in, Gaiman believes, 12 hours.

    Notice how gently the editor does it. No argument and no pleading, just two facts: the deadline is still open, and someone else has done it in half the time. The rest of the decision is Gaiman’s.

    Beyond the lectureThe anthology was Strahan’s The Starry Rift. Strahan has written that Gaiman wrote the story for it, but that “time and circumstance” meant it went instead into Gaiman’s own collection, Fragile Things (2006), as its new story (“How to Talk to Girls at the Hugos”). It was nominated for the 2007 Hugo Award for best short story, and won that year’s Locus Award (Wikipedia).

    9

    The wrong point of view

    A boy who can’t talk to girls, at a party full of aliens

    In the lectureGaiman took the notebook with the fragments and thought: “I can’t tell that story… I think it doesn’t work because the point of view is wrong. I need a human point of view.” A character who’s basically Gaiman at 16, and a title that simply arrived, How to Talk to Girls at Parties, though “normally, titling stories drives me nuts”. It would be about being 15 going on 16, when girls have suddenly become aliens: five years ago “you all had scabby knees”, all one tribe of kids, and now they wear makeup and dress differently and seem to know amazing secrets, gone into adulthood ahead of you. All at a party, with a boy who hasn’t worked out how to talk to girls, or to other people, and definitely isn’t listening. If it worked, the girls would tell the boy all about coming to this planet as aliens, “and he is going to absolutely fail to understand anything he is being told.” It took a day: down to the bottom of the garden with a blank notebook, back that evening with the story written. And “nothing actually turned out to be abandoned”: the fragments of the alien diary went to the girls at the party.

    The material didn’t change; the camera moved. The alien’s inside view was interesting and inert, because an alien narrating her own strangeness has no one to misunderstand her. Put the same strangeness in front of a boy who can’t hear it, and the gap between what’s said and what’s understood does the work: the reader gets what the boy misses. The point of view made the story, and it’s funny, sad and true about being fifteen.

    Beyond the lectureThe story has since become a graphic novel, adapted by Gaiman with art by Fábio Moon and Gabriel Bá (2016), and a film directed by John Cameron Mitchell, with Elle Fanning and Alex Sharp, which premiered at Cannes in 2017 (Wikipedia).

    10

    Hanging concentrates the mind

    The deadline as a tool

    In the lectureIt was “a better story I felt actually than it deserved to be”, given where it was written from, the need to get it written, the crunch. “But on the other hand, it’s quite possible that that time crunch is really what focused me”: “the willingness at the end to just throw out what wasn’t working, grab what would work, and put it in.” They say “hanging concentrates the mind wonderfully”, and missing that deadline felt a little bit like a hanging. Gaiman knew it was time to concentrate, and to choose solutions that would work.

    A deadline does what the stuck writer can’t do alone: it takes away the option of waiting for a perfect solution. With a day left, “it doesn’t quite work” stops being a reason to stop and becomes a reason to change something, now: the point of view, in this case. A day isn’t long enough to be precious about fragments, so they get reused instead of mourned.

    Beyond the lectureThe hanging line is Samuel Johnson’s, as James Boswell recorded it in 1777: “Depend upon it, Sir, when a man knows he is to be hanged in a fortnight, it concentrates his mind wonderfully.” Its context is darker than its reputation. Johnson had secretly written pieces for William Dodd, a clergyman hanged for forgery, and the line was Johnson’s way of deflecting a friend’s suspicion that Dodd hadn’t written them (Quote Investigator).

    Drill — by tonight

    Take an idea that keeps dying on the page. Give yourself until this evening, and allow one change only: the point of view. Tell it through someone who doesn’t understand what’s going on.

    Use every fragment you already have, and give each one to a character to say or do. Nothing gets abandoned; it just changes hands.

    Arc E · Fog, and one headlight

    2 moves
    11

    What you don’t know

    A novel is bigger than what you know about it

    In the lectureWriting a novel, there are things you know and things you don’t, but “a novel is big enough that there are going to be a lot more things that you don’t know going into it than things that you do know.” Outline or no outline, you’ll still be moving from point to point, with a lot of things you don’t know happening on the way.

    That takes some pressure off the stuck novelist. Not knowing what happens next isn’t a sign that something has failed; it’s the normal condition of a novel in progress, for writers who plan and writers who don’t. The question isn’t how to see the whole road. It’s how to keep moving without seeing it.

    12

    One headlight

    Creep forward; now and then the mist clears

    In the lectureE. L. Doctorow said that “writing a novel is like driving through the fog with one headlight out”. You can’t see very far ahead most of the time, and you creep along fairly slowly. “But you could get from New York to Los Angeles like that. You just have to keep going.” So again, “it’s about forward motion”: from the things you know to the next things you know, slowly. Every now and again the mists clear and you get a wonderful view of the valley on the other side of the town you’re heading for, and you know what’s happening, and then the mist comes back in and you’re creeping along again. “But that’s how you write a novel. Well that’s how I write novels.”

    Watch forThe image has grown in the retelling. Doctorow’s own words, in a 1986 Paris Review interview, have no fog and no broken lamp: writing is like driving a car at night, “you never see further than your headlights, but you can make the whole trip that way” (PBS NewsHour, quoting the interview). The fog crept in through quote collections, and the missing headlight is Gaiman’s own.

    Elsewhere in these notesGaiman has a light for the road in another lesson too: what your characters want is “like a flashlight: it shines a light on the road ahead and lets you move forward” (topic 10, move 12). The headlight shows a few metres; the flashlight tells you which way to point it.
