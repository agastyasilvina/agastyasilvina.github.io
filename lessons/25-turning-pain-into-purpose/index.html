Margo’s Books

“Turn Pain Into Purpose” begins: a book for every first-grader

The last heading opens with Harry’s question: what does turning pain into purpose look like for O’Connor?

When O’Connor was thirteen, O’Connor’s mother, Margo, was diagnosed with stage 4 breast cancer that had already spread to other parts of the body. O’Connor didn’t know it then, but the doctors told O’Connor’s father, Bill, that Margo would live only through the year. O’Connor knew only that everything had changed. In fact Margo lived until O’Connor was 26 — thirteen more years, “a small miracle”.

Margo was a primary-school teacher who loved reading; it mattered enormously. So the family started a project, Margo’s Books: for about twenty years, every spring, Bill handed out a book to every first-grader in their tiny rural Montana town. After Bill died, O’Connor and O’Connor’s sister decided to keep it going, as Bill and Margo’s Books — and every spring the two of them fly to Montana to hand the books out, passing on what their parents would have wanted.

Everybody in the town knew Bill and Margo, so there they are still “very much alive”: the sisters hear all sorts of stories. It’s a way for the two of them to connect, to do something meaningful, to enrich people’s lives through reading — and to give thirteen-year-old Mary-Frances, who didn’t know how to handle what was happening, a purpose that carries on because of who Margo was.

The lesson draws a conclusion from it: if you’re looking for longevity and the pinnacle of health, start to serve your community. Look out for one another. That, Harry says, feels like the right answer.

Beyond the lectureThere is research behind “serve your community”, with a catch. In a meta-analysis of studies of adults over 55, Okun, Yeung and Brown (2013) found that organised volunteering went with a 47% lower risk of dying — but only 24% lower once people’s health and circumstances were taken into account. A 2013 systematic review of 40 papers found the same kind of link with lower mortality, and with less depression and more wellbeing, in long-term studies of volunteers — but the few experiments that assigned people to volunteer didn’t confirm the benefits, so cause and effect is still unproven.

Watch for“The pinnacle of health” goes beyond the evidence. Volunteering goes with living longer, but much of the raw link reflects healthier people being the ones who volunteer. Serve your community because it matters; count the longer life as a likely bonus, not a promise.

2

Energising, not adrenalising

What purpose gave Harry

Hearing Harry’s story, O’Connor is struck that Harry found the difference: purpose is energising; it isn’t adrenalising. (The adrenaline “superpower” was part 1, move 6.)

Absolutely, Harry says — the difference is clear on the timeline of Harry’s life. Before 28 it was head in the sand, fingers in the ears: “I don’t want this life. I don’t want any of the responsibilities. This system, this thing that I was part of, is what took my mum away.” Then came: “I can do something with this. I can do good. I have a platform.” Therapy started the process; purpose now gives a resilience — not only to grief but to everything else. There are still things that hurt and things that cause stress, plenty of them, but a resilience has been gained.

Have the skills and tools of a lifetime let Harry face loss differently than at twelve, O’Connor asks? For sure. Harry has met so many people who have been through something similar, in a job that means meeting people where they are, from all walks of life, all round the world. And when you share your own grief and your own story publicly, it gives other people the opportunity — the permission — to open up to you.

Harry’s real hope is that people of all ages, and especially young people, don’t make the mistakes Harry made for sixteen to twenty years, and don’t end up with regrets about how they handled their grief. It’s hard, “it sucks”, but lean on the people around you, and don’t be afraid to share your story, and how utterly devastated you are.

3

Say something

Advice for the person beside someone grieving

What advice is there for people on the receiving end of someone else’s grief, Harry asks — what can you say or do that gives comfort, if you don’t know what to say? O’Connor starts with honesty: O’Connor says the wrong thing all the time, while talking to literally thousands of grieving people. (Harry had already guessed.)

The key is that there is no right thing to say — which is exactly why O’Connor says the wrong thing. The important part is to say something at all. Then watch the reaction, and if it’s withdrawal, or shock and horror, recognise it and own it: “Look, I’ve totally said the wrong thing here. Let me start again.”

What matters is being present. If you believe your job, standing next to a grieving person, is to cheer them up, you are going to be disappointed, because that is not your job. Your job is just to be there, to support them in whatever they’re going through.

Drill — say something

Think of someone you know who is grieving. Write the two sentences you could actually say to them this week — and the one you’d use to own it if it lands badly: “I don’t think that came out right. Let me try again.”

4

Three worries

What grieving people fear, and what to say back

O’Connor recommends a book by Shelby Forsythia, Of Course I’m Here Right Now, which says grieving people worry about three things:

Worry 1My grief is not normal.SayOf course you feel this way.
Worry 2I am alone — without the one person I relied on.SayI’m here. (And mean it.)
Worry 3I’m going to feel this way forever.SayRight now, this is how you feel, but you won’t always feel this way. I don’t know what that will look like, and neither do you — but it will not feel like this forever.

That third one, Harry says, is one of the reasons Harry keeps sharing parts of the story whenever it’s asked for: it’s a constant signal that Harry is okay, and that it will be okay — especially to young children who have lost a parent.

Beyond the lectureThe book is Shelby Forsythia’s Of Course I’m Here Right Now: Three Actually Helpful Things to Say to Someone Grieving (Broadleaf Books, 2026). Forsythia describes three stories grieving people tell themselves — “I’m crazy” for feeling this way, “I’m alone”, and “it’s going to be like this forever” — and the three phrases of the title answer them in turn: of course, I’m here, right now (interview with Presbyterian News Service, 2026).

Accepting

“How to Accept”: an ongoing verb, not a destination

We hear a lot about acceptance in grief, but acceptance makes it sound like a land we arrive in where everything is resolved. A better word is accepting: an ongoing process in which, for right now, you are accepting something that is really hard or painful. You don’t have to do anything with it, fix it, or be angry about it. You’re just accepting that it’s true, right now.

Here’s how the practice works:

  1. Once a day, name one thing that is true and painful. Not to dwell on it or spiral — just say it out loud, or write it: This is real. This happened. I am still here.
  2. At the same time, give yourself compassion for having to go through it: Of course I feel this way; anyone would, in my situation.
  3. Simply practise it, and see what insights it leads to.

It’s brief — about thirty seconds; you could pause and try it now. But over time it builds a familiarity with the truth that makes integrating the loss possible, and eventually natural. (It’s the “incorporating rather than avoiding” of part 1, move 15, made into a daily habit.)

Try it — thirty seconds of accepting

Type — or just say to yourself — one thing that is true and painful right now, then press start and read the lines as they come. Nothing is kept unless you tick the box, and then only in this browser.

This is real.

This happened.

I am still here.

Of course I feel this way. Anyone would, in my situation.

    Beyond the lectureThe practice is close to the psychologist Kristin Neff’s “self-compassion break”. With a hard situation in mind, you say three things. This is a moment of suffering is the mindfulness step — the class’s “this is real, this happened”. Suffering is a part of life, or “other people feel this way”, is common humanity — the class’s “anyone would”. And may I be kind to myself, often with a hand on the heart, is self-kindness — closest to “of course I feel this way”. The class adds a line Neff’s version doesn’t have: I am still here.

    Invictus

    “We Heal in Community”: from a flight home to a movement

    In 2013, Harry says, Harry was pretty lost emotionally and pretty erratic; the panic attacks were still happening, and the missions had finished. Harry needed something to focus on — something to get teeth into.

    That became the Invictus Games, an international multi-sport event for wounded and disabled veterans and serving personnel — from 25 countries, Harry says — set up in London in 2014. It started with Harry’s time in Afghanistan: flying home on a plane with the coffin of a Danish soldier, and three British soldiers in induced comas, wrapped in plastic — one of them holding a test tube of the shrapnel taken out of that soldier’s own neck.

    Invictus was born from seeing the scars and sacrifices of war: making sure service personnel from all over the world get a platform to use sport as part of their rehabilitation — to rediscover their identity, or create new ones, and so help heal themselves, which ultimately helps heal their families too. It has grown into a movement because of the resilience and strength the competitors show on the field, on the court and in the pool, and the strength of its community is like nothing Harry has experienced anywhere else. Creating it, Harry never foresaw the impact it would have on Harry’s own life.

    Beyond the lectureThe flight Harry describes was in early 2008, at the end of Harry’s first tour of Afghanistan, and Harry has told it before — in the opening speech at the 2017 Games, for one (Forces News). The idea took shape after Harry visited the 2013 Warrior Games in Colorado Springs, a US event for wounded service members, and lit its cauldron that May (ABC). The first Invictus Games were held at London’s Queen Elizabeth Olympic Park from 10 to 14 September 2014, with 409 competitors from 13 nations. The most recent, in Vancouver and Whistler in February 2025, were the first with winter sports — 534 competitors from 23 nations — and the next are in Birmingham in July 2027. Harry’s “25 countries” was right until this summer: in July 2026 Uganda became the 26th member nation.

    Somewhere for the love to go

    Four ways people use what they feel

    The interesting thing about grieving, O’Connor says, is that we have to do something with all that love. Different people find different purposeful ways to use it:

    • New relationships — connecting with new people.
    • Something transcendent — a love that is about more than the one person who died: loving humanity, or the universe.
    • Love for yourself — often when the person who died had limitations and couldn’t give you love, for one reason or another; out of that, you discover you can give that love and care to yourself.
    • Community — realising you have love to give, and that it needn’t be for this one person: it can be for a whole community of people.

    That last one, O’Connor says, resonates with Invictus: all the effort it takes to build it — that purpose, that engine — is driven by the energy that comes from community.

    8

    Healing in company

    Decades of research, one conclusion

    After studying grief for decades — from the cardiovascular side, the immune system and the neurobiology — what really strikes O’Connor is that healing, integrating a loss into your life, happens in community. Community is the container that holds the grieving brain and the grieving body while we integrate the loss into our lives.

    The benefits of connecting with other people show up in our blood pressure, in which genes we switch on, even in our risk of dying. So mattering — being seen, being heard, being needed — is a hard finding, O’Connor says: a scientifically backed understanding of what we need.

    Beyond the lectureEach of O’Connor’s three claims has a study behind it. Mortality: a meta-analysis of 148 studies and over 300,000 people found that people with stronger social relationships had a 50% greater likelihood of survival over about seven and a half years — an effect the authors call comparable to established risk factors (Holt-Lunstad, Smith and Layton, 2010). Blood pressure: following 229 adults aged 50 to 68 for five years, Hawkley and colleagues (2010) found that loneliness predicted rising systolic blood pressure two to four years later — 14.4 mm Hg more over four years for the loneliest than for the least lonely, by the University of Chicago’s account. Genes: in a small early study, lonely people’s immune cells showed more activity in inflammation genes and less in genes that respond to the body’s own calming signals (Cole and colleagues, 2007) — a pattern later named the “conserved transcriptional response to adversity”, which one trial found that four weeks of acts of kindness could reduce.

    Mattering is a research idea too: the sense that others notice us, think we’re important and depend on us (Rosenberg and McCullough, 1981) — or, in a later definition, “the perception that, to some degree and in any of a variety of ways, we are a significant part of the world around us” (Elliott, Kao and Grant, 2004, quoted in a 2022 review).

    9

    The big project

    A tool from prolonged grief treatment

    The evidence-based psychotherapy for prolonged grief, O’Connor says, is prolonged grief treatment, developed by Katherine Shear at Columbia University. One part of it is what the class calls the big project: find something important to you, something that matters and pulls you forward. It has to be internally motivated.

    It might be a flower garden full of your mother’s favourite flowers; a fundraising walk in honour of a cousin who died; or a plaque on a bench on a hill, where you can sit and think about how your loved one would have looked out over that view. In the end, what you’re looking for is something that:

    • helps you feel connected to the person who’s gone;
    • brings them into your life now;
    • lets you talk about them with other people, if you want to;
    • and makes you feel you’re honouring their legacy.

    (Bill and Margo’s Books, in move 1, is exactly that.)

    Beyond the lectureThe treatment runs over sixteen sessions, through a set of milestones: understanding and accepting grief, managing grief emotions, seeing a promising future (the goals work), strengthening relationships, telling the story of the death, living with reminders, and connecting with memories (Prigerson, Shear and Reynolds, 2022). It has been tested in three large trials. In 2005, 51% responded against 28% with a standard therapy; in 2014, with older adults, 71% against 32%; and in 2016, with 395 people, about 83% responded to the treatment — and adding the antidepressant citalopram didn’t improve the grief response, though it eased depression.

    Watch forTwo details are loose. Katherine Shear first developed the treatment — then called complicated grief treatment — at the University of Pittsburgh, and later moved to Columbia, which now houses the Center for Prolonged Grief. And “the big project” isn’t the treatment’s own term: the nearest part is its work on personal, aspirational goals, which begins by asking what you would do if a magic wand meant grief no longer got in the way.

    Drill — sketch your project

    One line each: what it is; why it matters to you; how it connects you to the person; and who you might tell about them while you do it. If all four lines come easily, you may have found it.

    10

    Share it, and listen harder

    Harry’s advice, for both sides of the conversation

    To survive trauma and grief, Harry says, we have to share it. Sharing reduces the pain and the suffering, mostly because so many people feel that what they’re going through is unique to them, and feel very alone. The moment you share your story, you can almost guarantee that someone in the room, or somewhere in your orbit, will understand what you’re going through.

    And if you’re the one sitting there wondering what to say: first, be the ear, and listen. If you think you’ve heard it before, listen harder — what you’re about to be told may be the first time it has ever been told. Once people have opened up, many would like something in return. That doesn’t necessarily mean advice; but if they’re going to be vulnerable, the least you can do is be vulnerable as well. Harry has never found anyone who regretted it.

    Functioning isn’t healing

    The class’s last words

    If Harry could go back and have a moment with a younger self, it would be to give twelve-year-old Harry a hug and say that everything is going to be okay.

    Margo would never believe, O’Connor says, that O’Connor is here talking to you — as a scientist, as a professional woman. O’Connor still wears the pearls Margo wore on Margo’s wedding day, knowing how proud Margo would be; and Margo would want O’Connor’s experience of loss to be something that helps you, too.

    Harry wishes this class had existed at twelve, or twenty, or even thirty, and hopes it helps you find the resilience that eluded Harry for too long: “Functioning is not the same as healing, and now I know the difference.” Hopefully you know yourself better now, and have the tools to be more vulnerable, for yourself and for the people around you.

    O’Connor’s summing-up is a line from O’Connor’s husband: “Grief can’t be optimized. It can only be lived.” If you’re brave enough to re-examine a loss from long ago, it may open up a lot of feelings, thoughts and memories — and that’s okay. It is worth doing.

    And the class’s last line: this lives in each of us, and the real superpower is learning how to tap into it — a final turn on the false “superpower” of adrenaline that the class began with.
