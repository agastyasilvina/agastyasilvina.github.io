Study notes · Topic 04 · Waiting for the crash vs investing now

Call every bottom perfectly — and still finish behind.

The lecture runs two thought experiments. The first hands you godlike powers — you know the exact day the market bottoms, every time — and you still lose to somebody who buys blindly every month. The second asks how long you would wait to invest a million dollars, and then shrinks the answer until it applies to you. Here is the argument, the numbers checked, and the three places it does not reach.

The three crashes

The opening setup

In the lectureThe biggest crash was 1929 at −89.2%. For comparison: the dot-com bubble took the market down 78%, and the 2008 crisis 54.1%.

The lecture opens by establishing how bad it can get, because the whole argument is about whether it is worth waiting for one of these. Three drawdowns, worst first:

Watch forThese three are not the same index, and the lecture calls them all “the market”. −89% is the Dow, Sept 1929 to July 1932. −78% is the Nasdaq, which was where the dot-com damage was concentrated — the S&P 500 fell roughly half that over the same stretch. The 2008 figure is usually quoted nearer −57% for the S&P 500 on a closing basis; 54.1% is in the right neighbourhood but not the number most sources give. Compare like with like or the ranking is partly an artefact of which index you picked.

Worth adding what the chart above leaves out, because it matters more to the argument than depth does: how long each took to come back. Depth is what frightens people into waiting; recovery time is what actually costs them. A crash you buy into is only a bargain if there is a recovery on the other side within your horizon.

Arc B · Experiment one: perfect timing

4 moves
2

The rules of the game

The thought experiment, stated precisely

You are dropped into 1920 with forty years of investing ahead of you and $100 a month to put to work. Two strategies, and you must pick one for the whole forty years:

  • Dollar cost averaging (DCA). Invest the $100 every month, no matter what the market is doing. No judgement, no waiting.
  • Buy the dip. Hold the $100 in cash each month, let it pile up, and deploy the pile only when the market is in a dip — 1929, for instance.

Then the lecture stacks the deck. The dip-buyer is given godlike powers: they know, in advance, the exact month the market bottoms, every time. Not a good guess — the actual low. Every purchase they make is at the best price available.

That is the part worth pausing on, because it is what makes the result interesting. The experiment is not testing whether market timing beats DCA. It is testing whether the theoretical maximum of market timing — a level nobody has ever reached or will — beats buying blindly every month.

Where it comes fromThe experiment and both headline figures are Nick Maggiulli’s, from Just Keep Buying (2022) and the simulations he published on his blog Of Dollars And Data. The video is a summary of that book; the arithmetic underneath is his, run on US market history.

3

Perfect timing loses anyway

The first result

In the lectureRun the simulation and DCA outperforms buy-the-dip over 70% of the time — despite the dip-buyer knowing exactly when the market will bottom.

Logically it looks impossible. If you always buy at the lowest price, how do you lose? The answer is that price is only half of it, and time is the other half. While the dip-buyer sits on cash waiting for a bottom that may be four years away, the averager’s money is already in the market, compounding through everything that happens in the meantime.

Cash waiting for a crash is not neutral. It is a position, and it is losing to inflation while it waits. The lecture’s phrasing is that the market is likely to keep rising and leave you behind — and the longer the stretch without a crash, the further behind you get.

The shape of the result is as telling as the number. Buy-the-dip does well for start dates in the 1920s, because a once-in-a-century crash arrives shortly after you begin hoarding cash. After the 1930s it essentially stops working. That is the strategy’s entire case: it needs a catastrophe, early, while you are still holding cash rather than assets.

The dip-buyer has perfect knowledge of the future and loses anyway. The cost was never bad prices. It was the waiting.

The point of experiment one

Watch for“Over 70% of the time” is a count of starting months in the simulation, not a probability that applies to your particular decade. The 30% where dip-buying wins is not random noise either — it is concentrated in start dates that sit just before a severe crash. The honest reading: dip-buying is a bet on a specific and rare kind of history, and you have to be right about the timing of that history in advance.

4

Now miss the bottom by two months

The second result — the one that settles it

In the lectureNobody on the planet can actually call the bottom. Miss it by just two months and DCA’s win rate rises from 70% to 97%.

This is the move that turns an interesting result into a decisive one. Take away the godlike powers — not all of them, just enough to make the dip-buyer slightly human, off by two months in either direction — and the strategy loses almost always.

Which tells you where dip-buying’s edge actually lives: not in the general idea of buying cheaply, but in a precision that nobody has ever demonstrated. A strategy whose advantage evaporates under a two-month error is not a strategy, it is a wish with a spreadsheet attached.

And two months is generous. In the real version you do not know you are in a dip; you know the market has fallen and might fall further. The bottom is only visible in hindsight, which is precisely when it is no longer available.

Drill — check your own record

If you have ever waited for a better entry price, write down the date you decided to wait and the price you were waiting for. Then look up what the price actually did. Do it for every instance you can remember, not just the one that worked.

This is the only version of this argument that will convince you, because it is about you rather than about 1929.

5

And you wouldn’t have bought anyway

The behavioural objection

In the lectureAnother problem: you are very unlikely to actually invest when the market is crashing and everyone is panicking.

The simulation assumes a dip-buyer who deploys years of accumulated savings into a market that is down 40% and still falling, while the news is at its worst and the people around them are selling. The historical record of actual human beings doing this is thin.

The conditions that produce a bargain are the same conditions that make buying feel insane. That is not a coincidence — it is why the bargain exists. So the strategy requires two impossible things in sequence: know the bottom, then act against every instinct and every headline at exactly that moment.

Which is the quiet argument for automation. A monthly transfer that happens without your involvement keeps buying through the crash because nobody asked it how it felt. The advantage of a rule is that it does not have to be brave.

Watch forThis cuts both ways, and the lecture only takes one side of it. The same person who can’t bring themselves to buy during a crash may also not be able to stop themselves selling during one. Automation protects the buying; it does nothing about the selling. If you know that about yourself, the size of your position matters more than the schedule of your purchases.

6

The hundred-year question

A different question: not when, but how fast

In the lectureYou are gifted $1,000,000. Either invest it all now — lump sum — or invest 1% of it each year for the next hundred years. Waiting a century to get invested will not be kind to your purchasing power.

Experiment one killed off waiting for a crash. Experiment two asks the question that survives it: given money in hand today, should it all go in at once, or be fed in gradually?

The hundred-year version makes the answer obvious. Nobody would take a century to deploy a million dollars — the last instalment arrives long after you are dead, and the first ninety years of purchasing power are eaten by inflation while you wait. If you believe the assets rise over time, getting invested sooner is worth something.

Then comes the move that makes it bite: if a hundred years is absurd, so is a hundred months, and so is a hundred weeks. The absurdity does not appear at some threshold; it is the same cost at every scale, just smaller. Spreading money in has a price, and the price does not become zero because the schedule is short enough to feel prudent.

Watch forThis is a reductio, not a proof. It establishes the direction — spreading in costs something — not the magnitude at the horizons you actually care about. Twelve months is not a hundred years, and the cost over twelve months might be small enough to be worth paying for the reduction in regret. The reductio cannot tell you that; only the simulation in move 8 can, and even then only on past data.

7

Two shapes of $12,000

The mechanism, made visible

In the lectureYou have $12,000. Lump sum: invest all of it in month one. DCA: invest $1,000 in month one and hold the remaining $11,000 in cash, to be invested in equal payments over the next eleven months.

Same money, same destination, same twelve months. The only difference is where the money sits while the year happens — and that is the entire source of the performance gap:

Stated plainly: dollar cost averaging a lump you already hold means deliberately staying about half-invested for a year. That is not a subtle drag. It is the whole of the difference, and it explains the result before you run a single simulation.

8

Lump sum wins 68% of the time

The second result

In the lectureSimulated over 24-month periods from 1960 to 2018, the chart sits below the zero line most of the time. Extended to 1920–2018, lump sum beats DCA 68% of the time — and results are similar across other asset classes.

Two thirds. Not overwhelming, but consistent, across a century, and consistent across asset classes rather than an artefact of one market’s history. And it is exactly what the figure in move 7 predicts: if assets rise in most twelve-month windows, then being fully invested beats being half-invested in most twelve-month windows.

The lecture’s own summary of when lump sum loses is the honest part: it underperforms when you invest right before a crash — and over a long enough period, it recovers.

Independent corroborationThis one does not rest on a single author. Vanguard ran the same comparison in 2012 (Dollar-cost averaging just means taking risk later) across the US, UK and Australia, and found immediate investment beat averaging-in roughly two thirds of the time over twelve-month periods. Two different teams, different data windows, same answer — that is worth more than either result alone.

Watch for“68% of the time” is a frequency, not a magnitude. It tells you nothing about how much you win or lose in each case, and the 32% is not scattered evenly — it clusters in exactly the windows people fear, the ones that begin just before a crash. A strategy that wins small two thirds of the time and loses large one third of the time is not obviously better, and the win rate alone cannot tell you which this is.

The assumption underneath

Where the narrator says he was actually convinced

In the lectureThe numbers all made sense but weren’t quite convincing on their own. What settled it: buying a diversified global fund is investing in the future of humanity — and the twentieth century had two world wars, a cold war and multiple crashes, and humanity kept going.

Every result on this page sits on one load-bearing assumption, stated in the hundred-year question and never revisited: the assets go up over time. Remove it and the conclusions invert — if the market falls over your horizon, the person who sat in cash wins, and the dip-buyer was right all along.

So the lecture ends by arguing for the assumption rather than the arithmetic, and it is the most honest moment in it. A global index fund holding several thousand companies across every major market is not a bet on a chart; it is a bet that in ten or twenty years, collectively, people will have built more than they destroyed. The twentieth century is offered as evidence: it contained about as much destruction as a century can hold, and the line still went up.

Watch forTwo things to keep separate here. The evidence is one century of one species’ economic history, which is a sample of one and cannot be repeated for a control. The bet is reasonable anyway — it is roughly the same bet as owning a pension, a house, or a career. Just call it a bet rather than a law, because you will hold it better through a crash if you already know what you signed up for. Also: the fund’s holding count (4,115 companies as quoted) drifts constantly with index changes — read it off the current factsheet rather than repeating a number from a video.

What the lecture does not say

My additions — the gaps that matter most

“DCA” means two different things, and the difference is everything

This is the most misread point in the entire debate, and the lecture glides over it. In these studies, DCA means you already hold a lump of cash and choose to drip it in. That is the thing that loses 68% of the time.

Investing part of your salary every month is not that. Each paycheque is money that arrives and goes straight to work — a series of small lump sums, each invested immediately. It is already the strategy this research recommends, at the only speed available to you.

So if you invest monthly from income and this lecture made you anxious: you were never doing the losing strategy. The finding applies to windfalls — a bonus, an inheritance, a house sale, the cash pile you have been sitting on waiting for a better moment.

68% means it lost one time in three

Spreading the money in is not irrational; it is buying insurance, and insurance has a premium. The expected cost of that premium is the 68% figure. What you get for it is a smaller worst case and, more usefully, a plan you will actually execute. A worse strategy you follow beats a better one you abandon in March.

The backtest runs on the market that won

US equities, 1920–2018, are the single most successful major market of the century — which is precisely why they are the ones with the clean data everyone backtests on. The counter-case is Japan: the Nikkei peaked at the end of 1989 and did not regain that level until February 2024. Thirty-four years. An investor there, doing everything this lecture recommends, would have spent a working lifetime underwater.

A global fund is a real defence against picking the wrong country. It is not a defence against a bad era, and nothing is.

Not covered at all

  • The emergency fund comes first. None of this applies to money you might need soon.
  • Time horizon. Money needed within about five years does not belong in equities regardless of win rates.
  • Fees, taxes and wrappers. Over decades these can matter more than the lump-sum-versus-DCA decision ever will.
  • Currency. A global fund pays you in a basket of currencies while you spend one.
  • “The market recovers” is about diversified indices, not individual companies. Plenty of those never came back.

Watch forThese are study notes on somebody’s book summary, not financial advice, and nothing here knows your tax position, your debts, your horizon or your nerve. The useful output of this page is a question list to take to your own situation, not an instruction.

11

Decide the rule before you need it

Turning the finding into behaviour

Every result on this page points the same way: the winning behaviour is mechanical, and the losing behaviour is discretionary. The dip-buyer loses to a rule. The averager beats them by not having an opinion. So the practical work is not learning to predict markets — it is removing the moments where you get to decide.

Drill — write the rule down
  1. The rule. How much, on what date, into which fund, from which account. One sentence, specific enough that somebody else could execute it.
  2. Automate it so the decision is made once rather than monthly.
  3. The crash plan, written now while nothing is happening: what you do at −20%, and at −40%. If the answer is “keep buying”, say so in writing and sign it — that sentence is the whole point of writing it early.
  4. What would change my mind? Name in advance the evidence that would make you stop. Anything vaguer than that will be supplied by a headline at the worst moment. (Same test as topic 01, move 2 — if nothing would change your mind, it isn’t a position, it’s a loyalty.)
Drill — if you are sitting on cash you can’t deploy

Pick the window in advance — say three or six months — divide the pile by that number, automate it, and then stop reading about it. You are knowingly paying a small expected cost for a much better chance of actually finishing the job.

And separate the two sentences people use interchangeably: “I don’t have the money to invest” is a fact. “I’m waiting for a better price” is a forecast. Only the second one is what this lecture is about.

To check

What did not survive checking, and what I could not resolve from the video.

  • The 2008 figure. The lecture says −54.1%. The S&P 500’s peak-to-trough is usually given as about −57% on a closing basis (Oct 2007 – Mar 2009). Which index and which measure produce 54.1% is not stated.
  • Three different indices, one word. Dow for 1929, Nasdaq for dot-com, presumably S&P for 2008 — all called “the market”. The ranking is partly an artefact of that choice.
  • Simulation parameters. Neither figure’s underlying assumptions are stated on screen: window length, whether dividends are reinvested, real or nominal. Worth reading Maggiulli’s original posts before repeating the numbers.
  • 24 months or unspecified? The lecture describes 24-month windows for 1960–2018, then extends to 1920–2018 and gives 68% without restating the window.
  • The channel. Fetching the video page returned the title but no channel name or upload date. Attribution below is to the video and the book it summarises.
  • 4,115 companies. Quoted for the FTSE All-World. Index membership drifts constantly — check the current factsheet rather than the video.
