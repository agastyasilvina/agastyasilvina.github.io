Study notes · Topic 22 · Grief and resilience, part 1

“I’m fine” — the same grief, wearing different clothes.

Prince Harry was twelve when Princess Diana died, didn’t cry again for nearly two decades, and answered “I’m fine” almost every day for twenty years. In this MasterClass Harry tells that story alongside Mary-Frances O’Connor, a neuroscientist who studies what loss does to the brain and body. The first two lessons, covered here, explain what grief actually is — an emotion, a trajectory and a physical event — and the patterns it drives underneath: adrenaline that feels like a superpower, the crash when it runs out, and coping that is really shutdown. Followed in the class’s order, with the research behind its numbers boxed alongside.

Why this class

The trailer: resilience is what you build on the way back up

Everyone goes through some kind of loss. The class starts with the pandemic, when people lost loved ones, jobs and whole identities, with no roadmap for coming back. Its definition: resilience isn’t about not falling; it’s about what you build on the way back up.

Harry’s path there was long. Told at twelve that Diana had died, Harry didn’t cry — only at the burial, and then not again for nearly two decades — and was convinced that was strength. It wasn’t, Harry says now: it was “a 12-year-old boy with no permission to fall apart and no roadmap for how to heal”. In Harry’s twenties came two tours of war, partly an attempt to outrun grief, which didn’t work, because unprocessed grief doesn’t stay in the past; it comes with you. And if a prince, with the best help in the world, still had nowhere to put those feelings, the class argues, that shows how hard this is, and how universal.

Grief doesn’t always look like sadness. It can look like numbness, like distraction, or like the answer Harry gave nearly every day for twenty years to “how are you?”: I’m fine. The class’s claim is that these are all the same thing wearing different clothes — your strength looking for somewhere to go — and that resilience is learning to point it in the right direction, with intention.

Lived experience only goes so far, so Harry is joined by Mary-Frances O’Connor: neuroscientist, psychologist and professor, whose own mother died, and who then spent a career researching how grief affects us. O’Connor is “always amazed at how much life can hurt”, and just as amazed at how resilient people are — often both in the same person. And the research, O’Connor says, shows that resilience, not depression, is the most common outcome after loss. So the question isn’t whether you can come through it, but how.

The class is built in five parts:

  1. Understand what grief actually is — understanding alone is a kind of freedom.
  2. See your own patterns — not as flaws, but as information.
  3. Real tools, which put the power back in your hands.
  4. Rebuild — not back to who you were, but into who you’re becoming.
  5. Purpose — because what you’ve lived through is the foundation for what comes next.

It’s for anyone rebuilding — the widow starting over, the person whose future was upended by a job loss or a diagnosis — and for anyone who wants to support the people around them. What you carry never fully disappears, the trailer ends, but you can still build resilience, even for things you can’t see coming. (And yes: Harry asks whether swearing is allowed, is told it is, and starts the class accordingly.)

Beyond the lectureThe class is MasterClass’s “Redefining Grief: Building Resilience Through Loss”, released in October 2026; press reports say its proceeds go to two charities for bereaved children, Comfort Zone Camp and Scotty’s Little Soldiers. O’Connor is a professor of psychology at the University of Arizona, where O’Connor runs the Grief, Loss and Social Stress (GLASS) Lab, and is the author of The Grieving Brain (2022) and The Grieving Body (2025).

The resilience claim is well founded. George Bonanno and colleagues (2002) followed 205 older adults from before a spouse’s death to eighteen months after, and found that resilience was the largest single pattern, at 46%, against 16% with chronic grief. A 2018 review of 54 studies of loss and other potentially traumatic events put the average share of resilient people at about two-thirds.

Many faces, one pressure valve

Lesson 1 begins: what we were never taught

The first lesson starts from a diagnosis: most of us were never taught what grief actually is, and that misunderstanding is what keeps us stuck. Its goals are to show what grief is, how it differs from grieving, and why loss can keep affecting the brain and body years later. Harry and O’Connor repeat that they are not therapists, not here to fix you, and not here to take your grief away — only to pass on tools that may help build the resilience already inside you.

Grief comes up in all sorts of ways. Some people are simply angry; some are depressed; some are irritable; some reach for every distraction life offers.

Harry thinks society plays a huge part: the people around you, how you were brought up, the cultural expectations of your country about how loss should be handled. In particular, men are encouraged not to grieve. Boys are raised to believe that crying is weakness, unacceptable, not what men do — which Harry dismisses, with feeling, as rubbish. You have to be able to cry and to show emotion: “Take it from someone who suppressed emotions for 20 years. It doesn’t end well.”

Harry’s image is a pressure valve. Nobody can sustain the pressure that builds day to day, least of all in today’s world. The frustration, the anger, the sadness: you have to find a place to put it, and crying is one way of letting the pressure off.

3

Grief and grieving

A snapshot against a trajectory

One of the unexpected things O’Connor learned from research is that grief and grieving are different things:

Grief

  • The in-the-moment emotion.
  • It will show up, now and then, for the rest of your life.

Grieving

  • The trajectory: how grief changes over time.
  • As it becomes familiar, you learn new ways to interact with it, cope with it and fold it into daily life.

The distinction came out of brain imaging. O’Connor asked bereaved people to come in on a single day and lie in an MRI scanner — and then realised that each scan was a snapshot, an image of grief. But most of the questions people actually ask are about grieving: what changes in the brain at two months, six months, twenty months, six years? A trajectory is a different thing from a snapshot.

What follow-up research shows is that linear grief is not a thing. Track people over time and you see ups and downs. Everyone has the same feelings, but not everyone has the same experiences, and it’s messy: one day you think you’ve got this, the next you feel terrible.

O’Connor likes a stock-market analogy. The market can crash for a day, or have a really bad week, and still be up at the end of the year. Grief is like that — constantly changing, and there’s nothing wrong with that.

Beyond the lectureO’Connor has put the distinction in one line elsewhere: “Grief is a moment in time. Grieving, on the other hand, is a trajectory.” The “not linear” point is aimed at the best-known model, Elisabeth Kübler-Ross’s five stages (1969) — which were drawn from people facing their own deaths, not from the bereaved. Grief researchers find no scientific foundation for grief stages (Stroebe, Schut and Boerner, 2017); in the Yale Bereavement Study (2007), acceptance was the most common response from the first month, and yearning, not disbelief, was the dominant painful feeling.

What researchers use instead is the Dual Process Model (Stroebe and Schut, 1999): healthy grieving swings back and forth between facing the loss and rebuilding a life, with breaks from both — which is exactly the up-and-down chart of the stock-market analogy.

Drill — chart it honestly

If you’re grieving something, rate each day for two weeks from 1 to 10 and plot it. Don’t look for a straight line; look for the trend across the whole chart, the way you’d read a year of a stock price rather than one bad week.

4

Grief in the body

A spider web, and the person who was your pacemaker

Grief doesn’t only look like sadness, or even numbness, Harry says. It can be anything: being unable to sleep, stress, panic attacks, “the most bizarre things”. It’s as if grief sits at the centre and a spider web of different effects spreads out from it.

O’Connor explains part of why. Many people know the feeling of coming home at night, getting a hug from their partner, and feeling a little calmer. That’s a real physiological change: heart rate falls, blood pressure goes down a little. Lisa Feldman Barrett calls the underlying idea the body budget. When you’re bonded with someone, they are regulating you. They act like an external pacemaker: your cardiovascular system comes to expect their input, so they calm you down when you’re wound up and help get you ready when you have to give a presentation.

When a loved one dies, that pacemaker is ripped away all at once — so the loss has physiological consequences, not just emotional ones.

Beyond the lectureThe “external pacemaker” has a long research history. The psychiatrist Myron Hofer argued in 1984 that a close relationship regulates the survivor’s biology, so a death removes many regulators at once. In a well-known 2006 experiment, Coan, Schaefer and Davidson threatened married women with a mild shock inside a brain scanner: holding their husband’s hand calmed the brain’s threat response more than holding a stranger’s, and most of all in happier marriages. Sbarra and Hazan (2008) described loss as the breakdown of this mutual regulation, and recovery as learning to regulate alone. “Body budget” is Lisa Feldman Barrett’s plain name for allostasis, the brain’s running budget of the body’s energy.

5

Coping isn’t grieving

Stopping the most natural process

The way we’re told to cope with grief, Harry argues, doesn’t match how the body is meant to deal with it. Coping is a mechanism, and it may be needed in the early stages — but coping for years on end “is no way to live”.

Harry calls grieving the most natural human process there is. Stop it because you’re worried what other people will think — because it’s “not the done thing”, not manly, weakness — and you’re stopping your body and your nervous system from doing what they’re supposed to do.

(The lesson closes on a lighter note, with a fly in the room that has had to witness all of this and is, Harry suggests, now going through a huge amount of grief of its own.)

The adrenaline superpower

Lesson 2: energy your body can’t sustain

The second lesson is about hidden patterns that quietly shape behaviour without our noticing — how to spot them, interrupt them, and redirect the energy forward.

Most of us have felt a burst of adrenaline: the rush of a deadline, a crisis, a sleepless stretch with a newborn. That extra drive can feel like a superpower. But it’s really your body spending energy it can’t sustain, and recognising that in yourself, and working through it, is where resilience starts.

Harry’s version: most of us are very good at hiding pain. Everybody knew what had happened, so everybody watched to see how a twelve-year-old who had lost a mother was coping — which was all the more reason to give the impression of being fine. For fifteen or twenty years, asked “are you okay?”, Harry said “I’m fine”. It was numbness, emotional shutdown.

And without knowing it, Harry was being driven by adrenaline. It was the force that took Harry into the army at twenty — “the perfect distraction”, because the task at hand left room to think about little else. In that state Harry could do things never thought possible. The adrenalised state really did feel like a superpower, and gave Harry skills in several areas that wouldn’t otherwise have come.

7

The crash

When there was no other mission

Coming back from a second tour of Afghanistan in 2013, Harry had no other mission, and everything caught up. The post-traumatic stress wasn’t tied to Afghanistan: what Harry saw and experienced there seemed to act as a trigger for other traumas stored up inside.

The signs of depletion were physical. Putting on a dress shirt, the tightening of the collar felt like suffocating. There were panic attacks in the car on the way to work. That was the point at which Harry realised something bigger needed looking into.

At the end of 2019 and into 2020, Harry, Harry’s wife and their son Archie moved to Canada to get space away from everything, and from an environment in which Harry’s body had presumably been pumped full of adrenaline. Within two or three days came an adrenaline crash like nothing before: it was as if stress was needed to make the brain work at all. Harry became useless around the house, slipped into depression for about two weeks, and felt unable to function without adrenaline. So Harry asks O’Connor: is that weird, or a normal response?

8

Gas, brake and battery

O’Connor’s car model of the stress systems

O’Connor answers with a car:

Part of the carIn the bodyWhat it does
Gas pedalSympathetic nervous systemSpeeds you up — adrenaline, fight or flight.
BrakeParasympathetic nervous systemSlows you down — and easing off the brake also lets you move forward.
BatteryCortisolA separate system: a hormone that helps decide how to use your energy stores day to day.

Drive with a lead foot on the gas all the time, pushing all that fuel through your body, and it isn’t sustainable. Adrenaline is there for the thing that happens in the moment — not again and again and again. And research shows, O’Connor says, that in people under chronic stress cortisol eventually becomes depleted: you’re foggy, you don’t perform well, and you feel exhausted, even just getting up in the morning.

Harry recognises the brain fog, and the advice received over the last five or six years: find your gears, one to five or one to six, and don’t get stuck in one. Stuck in one gear isn’t sustainable for the car — which is your body.

Beyond the lectureThe car maps onto standard physiology: the two branches of the autonomic nervous system, and the stress-hormone system that releases cortisol, which mobilises energy and follows a daily rhythm — high on waking, low at night. O’Connor’s own work fits the lesson: in a 2012 study, bereaved women with complicated grief had flatter daily cortisol curves than other bereaved women, and across 80 studies a flatter curve goes with worse health on most measures.

Watch for“Cortisol eventually becomes depleted” is a simplification. Research on chronic stress finds a disrupted rhythm — a high, flat daily pattern after uncontrollable or traumatic stress — rather than a supply that runs out (Miller, Chen and Zhou, 2007). The popular idea of “adrenal fatigue”, glands worn out by stress, isn’t a recognised condition: the Endocrine Society says no scientific proof supports it. The experience — the fog, the exhaustion, the crash — is real; the empty tank is a metaphor.

A broken heart is real

The numbers behind the adrenaline

How dangerous can these adrenaline spikes be? Dying of a broken heart, O’Connor says, is a real phenomenon in the research, visible in large population studies:

21×heart-attack risk in the first 24 hours after a loved one’s death
~2×men’s risk of dying of a heart attack in the 3 months after a wife’s death
1.5×the same for women
30–40%more fatal accidents among the bereaved in the first year

So the adrenaline is “no joke”. It isn’t just feeling wound up; it’s a physiological change with medical consequences for the heart and circulation. Grief is physiological as well as emotional.

Beyond the lectureChecking the class’s numbers against the studies they come from:

The class saysWhat the research shows
21 times the heart-attack risk in the first 24 hoursMatches. Mostofsky and colleagues (2012) found heart attacks 21 times as frequent in the day after a significant person died, falling day by day. It’s a relative risk — the chance on any one day stays small — and it counts heart attacks, not deaths.
Men almost twice as likely to die of a heart attack in the first three months; women 1.5 timesThe figures (1.87 for men, 1.47 for women) are from Moon and colleagues (2014), but they count deaths from any cause, and the women’s figure wasn’t statistically significant. For the heart specifically, Carey and colleagues (2014) found heart attacks or strokes 2.2 times as often in the 30 days after a partner’s death, in men and women alike.
Fatal accidents 30–40% more likely in the first yearNo source found for this exact figure. A study of 1.58 million Finns found deaths from accidents, violence and alcohol together 50–150% higher after a spouse’s death, highest in the first six months; a US study of 373,189 couples found accidental deaths over 20% higher.
Higher risk of dying of flu, pneumonia, even sepsisSupported. In Elwert and Christakis’s US study (2008), deaths from infections and sepsis rose by over 20% in widowed men and 10–20% in widowed women, and deaths from flu and pneumonia by 10–20% in women.

The overall pattern — the “widowhood effect” — is solid: a 2011 meta-analysis found death rates about 12% higher after losing a spouse, rising to about 41% in the first six months. And “broken heart syndrome” is a real diagnosis: takotsubo, first described in Japan in 1990, is a sudden weakening of the heart triggered by intense stress such as a death in the family. Nearly 90% of patients are women, average age about 67; the heart usually recovers within weeks, but in hospital it can be as dangerous as a typical heart attack.

10

Protest and despair

Searching, then stopping the search

Research finds very common responses to losing someone important, and O’Connor names two of them:

  • Protest. The easiest way to feel it: you’re in a supermarket, you look down, and your toddler isn’t at your legs. That utter panic is protest.
  • Despair. A very different state. When you realise “I don’t think my mum is coming back”, there’s withdrawal, conserving, a fuzziness. You can feel the difference — and it shows up in the body as inflammation.

The surprise is that despair resolves protest. As awful as it feels, despair has a function: to stop searching for someone who isn’t coming back. Searching — all that adrenaline, all that cortisol — is metabolically incredibly expensive, and keeping it up causes enormous wear and tear on the body.

When the supercharged adrenaline becomes chronic, the cost can also show up in the immune system. People who are bereaved, O’Connor says, are at higher risk of dying of flu, of pneumonia, even of sepsis.

Beyond the lectureProtest and despair come from John Bowlby’s attachment research: Bowlby and James Robertson described toddlers separated from their parents moving through protest, despair and then detachment, and Bowlby later described adult mourning in four overlapping phases — numbing; yearning and searching; disorganisation and despair; reorganisation (1980). The inflammation finding is from O’Connor’s own field. In a 2009 study, bereaved women’s inflammatory markers tracked activity in brain regions that respond to reminders of the person who died, and a 2019 study of 99 widowed people found higher levels of inflammatory proteins in those whose grief was most severe.

11

The fear of healing

Would losing the superpower mean losing Diana?

Once Harry understood where the superpower came from, a fear followed: if I heal from this — if I go looking for the fix — do I lose the superpower? And does that mean losing the connection to my mum?

Part of the long numbness, Harry explains, came from feeling disloyal and guilty at the possibility of being happy without Diana: “I felt like I had to be sad and I had to be emotionally numb in order to prove to her that I missed her.” What Harry discovered instead was that Diana “just wanted me to be happy” — a huge mental shift that brought freedom and a different outlook on life.

The key to it all, Harry says, is knowing yourself: how your nervous system and your whole body react to particular situations. Harry can now recognise an adrenaline surge — and also knows which things don’t require adrenaline at all.

12

Two tools: muscles and breath

Pulling back from the inevitable crash

What can someone in an elevated state do to pull back before the crash? O’Connor’s answer comes from O’Connor’s own lab, from a study run with psychologist Lindsey Knowles in which bereaved people were taught progressive muscle relaxation (PMR): systematically tensing and then relaxing the muscles of the body — feet, calves, thighs, abdomen, shoulders, arms, even the face. The point is to really feel the difference between tension and relaxation.

O’Connor demonstrates with one hand. Grip it as tight as you can, and feel what gripping is like. Now relax it completely — and, importantly, feel what those dead-weight fingers are like. Practise this every day through the body’s systems, and then at a red light you can think, “I’m going to do one of those right now.” When you’re grieving the mind is doing so many things, and PMR is a way to skirt around the thoughts and find relaxation from the bottom up, through the body.

Harry’s most useful tool is breath and breathing — still a work in progress, because shallow breathing and a tight chest still play havoc, and a nervous system kept hypervigilant for fifteen or twenty years is easily triggered. But finding the time and place to take a moment, breathe and reconnect is, Harry says, a game changer.

Try it — progressive muscle relaxation

A guided round of the class’s exercise. Tense each area firmly but never to the point of pain for five seconds, then let it go completely for ten and notice the difference. Skip anything injured or sore.

Ready when you are.

Try it — a breathing pacer

Follow the circle: it grows as you breathe in and shrinks as you breathe out. If you feel light-headed, stop and breathe normally.

Pick a pattern.

Beyond the lectureThe study O’Connor describes is Knowles and colleagues (2021): 95 widowed adults were given six weeks of mindfulness training or of progressive muscle relaxation, or put on a waiting list. Only the muscle relaxation reduced grief and yearning more than waiting did — and it also did better than mindfulness. (The lead author, Lindsey Knowles, was then a PhD student in O’Connor’s Arizona lab and is now at the University of Washington.) PMR goes back to the physician Edmund Jacobson’s Progressive Relaxation (1929), and reviews find relaxation training has a moderate effect on anxiety.

On breathing: slow breathing near six breaths a minute produces the largest rhythmic swings in heart rate, a sign of the body’s brake at work (Lehrer and Gevirtz, 2014). And in a 2023 Stanford study, five minutes a day of “cyclic sighing” — two breaths in, then a long breath out, the pacer’s second pattern — lifted mood more than mindfulness meditation over a month; anxiety fell in every group.

Emotional shutdown

Turning the volume down on everything

Sometimes what feels like coping is actually emotional shutdown. The system turns down the volume on pain — but that also mutes joy, connection and responsiveness. For Harry it was like flat-lining through life, reaching for anything that could distract or take some of the struggle away.

It began within five days of Diana’s death. Thousands of people were laying flowers outside Kensington Palace, and the two young princes went out to greet them. Looking back, Harry feels they were intermediaries for those people’s grief towards their mother — the crowd was showing more emotion than the boys could. It felt very strange that the hundreds of hands they shook were soaking wet with tears wiped from people’s own faces, and Harry almost felt guilty at being unable to show the same emotion as people crying for someone they had never met.

Then came the burial, with just family, away from the cameras and the expectations and judgements of the world. There Harry finally cracked and cried — and remembers that it actually felt really good to cry.

But for years afterwards Harry was looking for proof. Harry didn’t believe Diana was gone, believing instead that Diana had engineered an exit from everything that was destroying that life, and would come back in disguise to whisk the two boys away. That protective belief lasted a decade. The mind’s and body’s defence was to shut down the emotions and the memories — not just the bad ones but the good ones too. Harry forgot what Diana sounded like, forgot the laugh, and couldn’t picture the face without a photograph.

So Harry asks: what happens in the brain when someone who has lost a person keeps half-believing they’re alive, or thinks they’ve seen them walking down the street? Is there research on that?

14

Gone, but also everlasting

Two beliefs the brain holds at once

“Absolutely,” O’Connor answers. “This is surprisingly normal.” You can’t understand grief and loss, O’Connor says, without understanding how love and bonding work in the brain. We think of the mind as one stream of information, but the brain can hold different beliefs at the same time.

O’Connor calls this the “gone but also everlasting” theory:

Gone — the memory system

  • We know they have died. We were at the bedside, or at the memorial.
  • We know the reality. We are not deluded.

Everlasting — attachment

  • Bonding — falling in love with a partner, or with your baby — is encoded as a “we”: “I will always be here for you, and you will always be here for me.”
  • They don’t have to be present for you to know they’re out there for you.

So a death isn’t, to the attachment system, evidence that they are gone for good. Part of us still believes they’re out there, just lost — that we need to find them. We yearn for them because we still expect them to walk through the door. Some people describe trying to text the person who died, and then thinking “what was I thinking? I must be crazy.” You’re not crazy, O’Connor says: it’s an important part of the brain, the part that holds bonds together by believing the person is out there.

Harry sums it up: in grief the body and mind are in conflict. Can you prevent the conflict, or prepare for loss? O’Connor’s honest answer is that grief is just going to hurt. You are in a conflict — you know they’re gone, and you really believe they’re still there — and every time you realise both can’t be true, a wave of grief comes. You can think of grieving as a form of learning: learning how to bring what “everlasting” means into a today in which your person isn’t walking around, and you can’t hug them any more — how to carry them forward without a physical body.

Beyond the lecture“Gone but also everlasting” is the central idea of O’Connor’s book The Grieving Brain (2022): one system knows the person died, while the attachment system goes on predicting they’re out there — a theory O’Connor has said is still being worked out. Grief researchers have long agreed that keeping a bond with the person who died is normal and often healthy — the idea of “continuing bonds” (Klass, Silverman and Nickman, 1996) — rather than something that must be cut for recovery to happen. Harry’s own account fits: in the memoir Spare (2023), Harry wrote of crying only once, at the burial, and of believing for years that Diana had planned a disappearance — a belief that ended only with a visit to Paris at twenty-three.

Drill — notice the waves

Next time a wave of grief arrives out of nowhere, note what set it off: a song, a place, a habit like reaching for the phone. Each one is a moment when “gone” and “everlasting” collided. Seeing the trigger doesn’t stop the wave, but it makes it make sense.

15

Incorporate or avoid?

The question to ask yourself

For Harry, the key is awareness: you have to let grief come to the surface and actually grieve, rather than suppress it, push it to one side, or ignore the reality.

O’Connor offers a practical test:

Probably fineYou can let difficult thoughts come to mind and think about your loved one — and you also have times in your day full of joy and creativity. Both negative and positive emotions.
BrittleYou feel okay because you’re actively trying not to think about the loss: avoiding places, conversations, memories. When it does come up, you fall apart.

So it’s worth asking yourself, honestly: is this something I’m incorporating into my life, or something I’m avoiding?

The recording ends here, at the close of the second lesson. The class’s remaining parts — tools, rebuilding and purpose — will be the next set of notes.

Beyond the lectureWhen to get help. Most grief, however painful, doesn’t need treatment. But a minority of people develop prolonged grief disorder, recognised in the American Psychiatric Association’s DSM-5-TR (2022) and the WHO’s ICD-11: intense yearning for, or preoccupation with, the person who died nearly every day, with other symptoms such as feeling that part of yourself has died, intense emotional pain or numbness, or being unable to re-engage with life — lasting at least a year after the death (six months in ICD-11) and getting in the way of daily life. It affects roughly one in ten bereaved adults, and it’s treatable: in a 2005 trial, 51% responded to Katherine Shear’s targeted grief treatment, against 28% for a standard therapy.

If your grief has stalled like that, talk to a doctor or a grief counsellor. If you ever have thoughts of ending your life, contact a local emergency number or crisis line straight away.
