Voice is the thing a reader responds to more than anything else, and it’s the thing a beginner most wants and least controls. The lesson’s answer is unglamorous: you cannot decide what you sound like, you can only write until it shows up — and then, separately, you can choose what a particular book sounds like, which is a craft question with actual technique behind it. Arcs A and B are the first half. Arc C is the second.

Arc A · What voice is

3 moves
1

Style is the stuff you get wrong

The thesis — and a quotation that may not exist

In the lectureYears ago he ran into a line from Jerry Garcia: style is the stuff that you get wrong. If you were playing the guitar perfectly, making music perfectly, there would be no style. He thought it was a great quote and remembered it — and years later went to find it on the internet, and the only place he could find it was himself saying it in interviews. “So maybe he never said it at all.”

Take the idea first, because it is the spine of the whole lesson. Perfect execution is invisible and identical. If every guitarist played a passage exactly as written, you could not tell them apart — what identifies a player is the set of deviations they can’t or don’t remove. Style is the residue of imperfection, made consistent by repetition.

Which means the thing beginners chase deliberately is, by definition, the thing that arrives by accident. You cannot select your deviations. You can only produce enough work for them to become visible — to a reader first, and to you much later.

Watch forThis is the second quotation in this class that traces back to Gaiman himself — topic 03 has the Chesterton line he effectively invented and which is now attributed to Chesterton everywhere. The pattern is worth knowing, and so is the fact that he volunteers it both times; he is the one who went looking and reported the result. Practical rule: the idea is excellent, and if you use it, attribute it to Gaiman rather than Garcia. There is a certain justice in a line about getting things wrong being a thing he got wrong.

2

First you sound like everybody

Imitation as the mechanism, not the embarrassment

In the lectureThe problem young writers have is that they don’t sound like anybody yet. “I didn’t really sound like anybody. What I did was sounded like everybody else.” That’s what you do starting out — you imitate. You find voices you like, you think this person’s doing something great, I’ll try and do that. His own list: R. A. Lafferty, Harlan Ellison, Roger Zelazny, Ursula Le Guin.

The sentence to hold on to is “I sounded like everybody else” — not like somebody, like everybody. That’s the difference between plagiarism and apprenticeship, and it is also the escape route: imitate one writer and you produce a weak copy, imitate thirty and the blend stops resembling any of them. The composite is where you appear.

Which is topic 05’s compost heap seen from the output end. There, the instruction was to throw everything on and let it rot down. Here is what rotting down looks like from the writer’s side: you stop being able to point at which bit came from where, and at that moment it is yours.

3

A page and a half

The manuscript in the attic

In the lectureAt 22 he wrote a children’s book — the first thing he ever wrote, still unpublished, existing only in manuscript in his attic. After Coraline came out he wondered whether it was any good, fetched it down and read it to his daughter, then six or seven. It went back up to the attic, where it will stay “until the crack of doom”. But about a page and a half, near the end, read like him. The rest read like Noel Langley and Roald Dahl and every children’s author he had ever read, all coming back out again.

There are two readings of this and you need both.

The encouraging one, which is the one he gives: the voice was already there at 22. It existed. He just had to do a great deal more writing for it to take over.

The exacting one, which is the one that makes it useful: it was a page and a half in a whole book. Call it two per cent. That is the actual ratio at the start, and knowing it changes what you expect from your own early work. You are not waiting to acquire a voice you don’t have. You already have it, at about two per cent, and the work is raising the percentage.

Drill — find your two per cent

Go back to something you wrote years ago and read it looking for one thing: the paragraph that sounds like you now. There will be one, and it will probably be somewhere late, after you stopped concentrating on doing it properly.

Mark it. That’s the earliest appearance of your voice, and everything around it is what you were borrowing at the time. Both halves are information — the borrowed parts tell you who you were imitating, which you may have forgotten.

Arc B · How you get there

4 moves
4

A million bad drawings

Chuck Jones, applied to sentences

In the lectureChuck Jones said you have a million bad drawings in you, and your job as an artist is to get them out so the good ones can follow. As a writer, and especially a young one, your job is to get the bad words out, the bad sentences out, the stories that aren’t any good yet.

The reframing is the whole value. Bad work is not evidence that you shouldn’t be doing this, and it is not even the price of good work — it is the route to it. The bad drawings are not wasted material standing between you and the good ones; they are the mechanism by which the good ones become possible, and there is no path that skips them.

Which also means the only genuinely wasted year is the one spent not producing them. Reading about craft, planning, waiting to be ready: none of it draws down the inventory.

Watch forThe number moves around in retellings — the version usually told about Chuck Jones has his teacher putting it at a hundred thousand bad drawings, not a million. The figure is rhetorical either way and nothing turns on it, but if you quote it, quote it loosely (“Chuck Jones used to say you have a great many bad drawings in you”) rather than committing to a number the source may not support.

5

Not on purpose

The catch in the previous move

In the lectureYou never get them out by going “I am going to write a really bad story now, I just have to get this out.” You think it is a great story. You think it is a great idea. You think it is at least good — and it may be. But the important thing is that you get it out.

This is the part that stops move 4 from being a licence to be careless, and it is subtler than it looks. The bad work has to be attempted sincerely. You cannot clear the inventory by writing deliberately badly, because deliberate badness never finds the edge of your ability — you only discover where your reach ends by reaching as far as you actually can and watching where it stops.

The uncomfortable corollary, stated plainly: at the time, every one of those felt like the good one. Which means the feeling “this is working” carries almost no information early on. That sounds bleak and isn’t — it means you can stop using the feeling as a gate, and simply finish the thing and find out.

His version of the arithmetic is bracing: if you want to write an award-winning television episode, you are going to write episodes the critics don’t like. Award-winning film, films they don’t like. Award-winning short stories, stories nobody reads and that don’t really work. That’s okay. The bad ones aren’t a detour from the career; they are the early part of it.

6

Finish things

The single highest-leverage instruction in the lesson

In the lectureIn his attic: a hundred or two hundred first pages — first two pages, maybe three — written between sixteen and twenty. A setup, some characters, wanting to see what would happen, and then three pages in they’d sputter and flame out and be dead. It wasn’t until he was twenty or twenty-one that he realised he had to start finishing them. At that point the improvement became, in a word Terry Pratchett loved, quantum.

“You learn more from finishing a failure than you do from writing a success — and you definitely learn more from finishing a failure than you ever do from beginning something that is fantastic but stops.”

Neil Gaiman

Why finishing does so much work is worth spelling out, because “finish things” sounds like discipline advice and is actually a claim about what gets exercised. A beginning only trains the skills of beginning — premise, atmosphere, a hook, a voice for two pages. Every hard thing lives after page three: structure, consequence, the middle where nothing has momentum yet, an ending that pays for the setup, and the discovery that your lovely premise cannot support a story.

None of that can be learned from a first page, however good the first page is. So a hundred brilliant openings teach you one skill a hundred times, and one finished disaster teaches you six things once. That is the “quantum” jump — not working harder, working on the parts that were never being touched.

Drill — finish the worst one

Take the project you are least excited about — the one you already know isn’t working — and finish it anyway, at whatever length gets it to an ending. Badly is fine. Short is fine.

Then write half a page on what broke and where. That half page is the thing you were never going to get from starting something new, and it’s worth more than the draft.

7

What stays the same

Voice, and the thing underneath voice

In the lectureTake a dozen of his short stories, or half a dozen novels: they use absolutely different language, they have a different feel, they could almost have been written by different people — except that they’re obviously all written by him. There is a uniformity, and it isn’t word choice. It isn’t even voice: each story has its own voice. It’s the attitude, the soul, the thing you take away.

This quietly splits a word the lesson has been using for two different things, and the split is the most clarifying idea in the arc:

  • Voice, at the project level — diction, syntax, register, person, distance. Chosen per book. Craft. That is the whole of arc C.
  • Attitude, at the author level — what you find funny, what you think is at stake, what you will and won’t do to a character, what you evidently believe about people. Not chosen, and not fakeable.

The second one is the stuff you can’t help doing from move 1 — and it survives every deliberate change of style, which is why the books can sound like different people and still be unmistakable. It is also why imitating someone’s prose gets you nowhere near them: you can copy the voice and you cannot copy the attitude, because it isn’t on the page, it is the thing that decided what went on the page.

Practical consequence, and it’s a relief: you do not have to protect your voice. Write a hard-boiled detective story, a space opera, a doctor-nurse romance — the attitude comes anyway. His own line: your voice will be there because it’s the stuff that is going to be there whatever you do.

Arc C · A project’s voice

5 moves
8

Ask before you write

Three questions, and the third is the sharp one

In the lectureIf you’re trying to find the voice of a project, the most important thing is to take a stab at it. Sit down and think, before you start writing: what are the most important things about this project? Does it feel like first person or third? Who is telling you the story — and does it matter who is telling you the story?

The first two are the standard questions. The third is the one that does the work, because it has a real answer either way and the answer determines the technique.

If it doesn’t matter who’s telling it, the narrator is a delivery mechanism and the craft goes into making it disappear — move 9. If it does matter, the narrator becomes a character you have to build before you can write a sentence — moves 10 and 11. Those are opposite disciplines, and choosing wrong costs you a chapter at best, as the next move shows.

Note also “take a stab at it”. The answer is not arrived at by thinking; it is arrived at by writing a chapter and reading it back. Thinking narrows the options. Drafting settles them.

9

American transparent

When the narrator should vanish — and a diagnosis worth copying

In the lectureHe wrote the first chapter of American Gods in first person, told by Shadow. Got to the end of it and thought: that doesn’t work. Then stepped back and asked why it didn’t work — and the answer was that you are inside his head, yet he is emotionally closed off from the reader.

The design required the closure. Shadow starts in prison, shut down; he is told his wife is dead and everything stops; he is numb, dead inside, and the book is the slow business of waking up, going all the way down into death and coming back. So the technique and the character were fighting each other. First person promises interiority; a numb narrator has none to give. The reader gets locked in a room with someone who won’t speak.

What’s worth stealing is the move rather than the conclusion: he didn’t simply try something else until one felt better. He asked why the first attempt failed, and the answer named a structural conflict — which then told him what to do instead. “This isn’t working” is a feeling. “This isn’t working because X fights Y” is a solution.

The replacement he calls American transparent: the author is invisible, the words and characters are there, and you do nothing to draw attention to yourself. His examples are Elmore Leonard, who did it beautifully, and Stephen King when he flies. And the line that rescues it from sounding like an absence of style — a lot of the craft of the narrative voice comes in disappearing into the text. Invisible prose is not prose with the craft removed. It is craft spent entirely on not being noticed.

10

Invent the narrator

How Stardust got its voice

In the lectureStardust has a narrative voice, and the writer is a character — a completely different kind of writer from him. In his head, the narrator of Stardust is writing it long before there is a fantasy genre: just an adult novelist writing a fairy tale for adults, as people did in the teens, twenties and thirties. He liked the idea that this writer was possibly a woman. So the voice is very formal, slightly stilted, lusher, a little antique — completely different from Neverwhere, though both are third person.

This is the most directly usable technique in the lesson. Instead of asking the unanswerable question — what should this book sound like? — he invents a person who is writing it, gives them a period, a sensibility and a gender, and then writes as them. An impossible aesthetic decision becomes an easy casting decision, and it stays consistent for three hundred pages because you can keep asking “would she write that?”

Note what “before there was a fantasy genre” buys him: a narrator who does not know they are writing fantasy cannot wink, cannot nod to the conventions, and cannot be ironic about the material — which is exactly the register a fairy tale needs and which a modern narrator finds almost impossible to hold.

And “slightly stilted” is deliberate. He chose a flaw and applied it consistently, which is move 1 turned inside out: if style is the stuff you get wrong, a project’s style can be the stuff you get wrong on purpose, in one consistent direction.

11

A child’s height

The Ocean at the End of the Lane, and a graduated point of view

In the lectureA first-person narrative, so the voice of the narrator becomes the important thing. He wanted it told by a man in his mid-forties, remembering what happened when he was seven — the story coming back to him. Told from an adult perspective, but the deeper he goes into memory, the more it has to be from a kid’s height and a kid’s eye. Not a particularly writerly style: the voice of somebody holding your hand, looking you in the eye, saying let me tell you what happened to me.

Two techniques, both concrete.

“A kid’s height” is a literal instruction about what is in frame. Tabletops seen from underneath, adults as legs and voices above you, a garden that is enormous, distances that take a long time. Not childish diction — childish vantage. It is a camera position, and it does more for the effect than any amount of simplified vocabulary.

The shift is graduated, not binary. Adult at the surface, child deeper in. That is a structural device rather than a mood: the prose can start composed and retrospective and lose its adult framing as the memory takes over, which is also, conveniently, exactly how remembering feels.

And “not a particularly writerly style” is the constraint that makes the confiding voice work. Fine writing announces a writer. This voice has to sound like a person talking to you, because the next move depends on you believing it.

12

Then let go

The trick he calls dangerous

In the lectureHe has used this confiding first-person voice for years — in the graphic novel Violent Cases at the start of his career, in Mr. Punch, in a number of short stories. “It’s incredibly dangerous, if done well, in that it’s convincing.” You really are saying: I’m not going to lie to you. I’m going to tell you true things. Just hold my hand. We’re going to walk together, you and I, into a dark place, but I’m here. It’s all safe. And then you walk them into the dark place, let go of their hand, and run away.

The mechanism is worth taking apart, because it is an unusually clean example of technique producing emotion.

The intimacy is real; the safety is not. A confiding first-person voice reads like testimony rather than fiction, so it buys trust very cheaply — the reader relaxes the guard they hold up against a story. That relaxed guard is the whole apparatus. The horror lands because the contract is broken from inside a form that promised it wouldn’t be.

Which also tells you the conditions. It only works if the promise was kept sincerely first — a narrator who is shifty from page one has nothing to betray, and you get a twist instead of a wound. And it works roughly once per reader per book, since the reader who has been dropped will not take your hand the same way again.

This is topic 03’s argument arriving as a technique. There, fiction was “telling the truth with things that never happened” and honesty was the working method. Here is what that looks like at sentence level: the voice has to be actually trustworthy for long enough that letting go costs the reader something.

He signs off by saying that if somebody wanted to imitate him, that would be a fun style to go in and imitate — which, after arc A, is an invitation rather than a warning. Imitate it. It goes on the heap with everything else.

Drill — cast the narrator

For whatever you’re working on, answer in writing before the next session: who is telling this, and does it matter? If it matters, cast them — age, period, what they think of the events, why they’re telling you. A paragraph is enough.

Then write the same page twice: once transparent, once in their voice. You will know within a page which book you are writing, and that is a cheaper way to find out than Gaiman’s chapter of American Gods.
