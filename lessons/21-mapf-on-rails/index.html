Study notes · Topic 21 · Multi-agent path finding on rails

Many trains, one track — plan the order, not the clock.

How the solver that won the 2020 Flatland Challenge moves thousands of trains through a rail network without a collision or a deadlock. It plans every path before the first train leaves — one train at a time, then repaired a few trains at a time — and while the trains run, it enforces the order in which they pass each piece of track rather than the times, so a breakdown can delay trains but never trap them. Followed from two trains on one track to fifty with breakdowns, with labs that replay a working Python port of the solver.

The 2020 Flatland Challenge asked how to run dense traffic on a railway: up to thousands of trains, each from a start station to a target, with no two ever colliding, while trains break down at random. The winning entry — by Jiaoyang Li and colleagues, team An_Old_Driver — beat every other entry, reinforcement-learning ones included, with classic multi-agent path finding. This page rebuilds their solver one idea at a time, from two trains on one track to fifty trains with breakdowns. Every lab replays output from a Python port of the solver, written while studying it, so what you see is what the algorithm does. The usual boxes apply: Beyond the paper for related and newer work, Watch for where it’s easy to go wrong. For the whole map first, start at move 10.

Arc A · The problem

3 moves
1

Two trains, one track

Multi-agent path finding, and why rails make it hard

Multi-agent path finding (MAPF) asks for one path per agent, from its start to its goal, such that no two agents collide. Time moves in steps; in each step an agent moves to a neighbouring cell or waits where it is. Two kinds of collision are forbidden: two agents in the same place at the same step — a vertex conflict — and two agents swapping places in the same step — a swap conflict, which on a railway means meeting head-on. (The paper calls the second an edge collision.)

Flatland, run in 2020 as a NeurIPS competition by AIcrowd with Swiss Federal Railways, Deutsche Bahn and SNCF, is MAPF on a railway with four twists, which the paper lists. Trains can’t move backwards, so the way a train faces matters. Trains enter the map over time and leave it when they arrive. The aim is to get as many trains home as possible before a deadline, not necessarily all of them. And trains break down at random, for a random time.

The first twist is the one that bites. Two trains that meet head-on on a single track can never get past each other, because neither can reverse. Planning each train on its own is easy — give each its shortest path — and the lab shows what happens if you stop there.

Try it — two trains, one track

A single track with a passing siding. Train 0 starts at the west end facing east, train 1 at the east end facing west; the squares are their goals.

Beyond the paperThe standard vocabulary is set out in Stern and colleagues’ 2019 survey of MAPF definitions and benchmarks: the kinds of conflict, and objectives such as the sum of costs (total travel time) and the makespan (when the last agent arrives). Finding an optimal plan is NP-hard in general (Yu and LaValle, 2013), which is why instances with thousands of agents are solved with fast methods that find good plans rather than the best one — the kind this page is about.

Flatland scores each instance between 0 and 1 as 1 + δ/Tmax − ΣTi/(m·Tmax). Tmax is the step limit, m the number of trains, Ti train i’s arrival step (Tmax if it never arrives), and δ is 1 only if every train arrives. A lower sum of arrival times scores higher, and getting every train home adds a small bonus of 1/Tmax.

Watch forIn textbook MAPF, an agent that reaches its goal stays there and can still be hit; in Flatland it leaves the map. And textbook MAPF wants every agent home, while Flatland scores how early trains arrive and tolerates some that never do. Results quoted from MAPF papers don’t carry over one-to-one.

Drill — the head-on meeting

Play Each train alone. At which step do the trains stop, and on which cells? Then play Planned together: what did avoiding the deadlock cost, in steps, and which train paid?

Answer

They stop at step 7, train 0 on (2,7) facing east and train 1 on (2,8) facing west; neither can ever move again. Planned together, train 1 takes the siding while train 0 passes on the main line, and arrives at step 15 instead of 13 — two steps for the longer route. Train 0 isn’t delayed at all.

2

Where can a train go?

The search graph is a cell and a heading

Flatland’s map is a grid in which every cell stores 16 bits, in four groups of four. Each group belongs to one direction a train can be facing — north, east, south, west — and its four bits say in which directions a train facing that way may leave. Straight track allows one exit for each way it can be faced; a switch gives one facing two exits; a dead end allows a single move, back the way the train came. Dead ends are the only places a train can turn round.

So a train’s position is a cell and a heading, and the planner searches over (cell, heading) pairs. The same cell faced two ways is two different states, with different futures.

Try it — read a cell

Select any rail cell, then a heading. The highlighted group of four bits is the one the simulator reads for a train facing that way; the arrows are the moves it allows.


Beyond the paperThe paper links this to MAPF for motion planning, where an agent’s state includes its orientation (Cohen and colleagues, 2019). Four headings per cell means up to four times as many states as cells, but a search only ever visits the headings a cell actually carries: two on plain track, one at a dead end, more at crossings.

Watch forThe heading is the direction the train is facing as it moves, not the side of the cell it came in from: a train that entered from the west is facing east. Mixing the two up turns every transition round by 180 degrees — an easy bug when writing a decoder.

Drill — read the switch

Select the switch at (2,4). Where can a train go if it faces east? If it faces west? Why does a cell need sixteen bits rather than four?

Answer

Facing east it may turn north into the siding at (1,4) or carry on east to (2,5); facing west it can only continue west to (2,3). This switch splits eastbound traffic and merges westbound traffic. A cell needs sixteen bits because its exits depend on which way the train faces — four exit bits for each of the four headings. This one stores 3089: 0000 1100 0001 0001.

3

Distance depends on direction

One backward search per goal

A* search needs an estimate of how far each state is from the goal. On an open grid the straight-line or Manhattan distance is the usual guess, but on rails it can be badly wrong: a train facing away from its goal may first have to run to a dead end, turn round and come all the way back.

So the solver computes the true distance instead, from every (cell, heading) state, with one breadth-first search that starts at the goal and follows the transitions backwards. Trains that share a goal share the table. This is the heuristic every later search uses — and on an empty network it isn’t an estimate at all, but the exact number of moves.

Try it — distances from the goal

Each number is the number of moves to the shaded goal for a train facing the arrow’s way. Run the search reveals them in the order the backward search finds them.

Beyond the paperPrecomputing exact distances is affordable because the map never changes and goals repeat: many trains head for the same station. One table holds four numbers per cell — for the paper’s 229 × 229 example map, 209,764 per goal — which is cheap next to the search effort it saves every time a train is planned.

Watch forThe distance is exact only on an empty network. Once other trains are planned, a train may have to wait or take a detour, so the table becomes a lower bound. That still suits A* and SIPP, which need an estimate that never overstates, but it’s no longer a promise.

Drill — the twenty

With Goal of train 0 selected, cell (2,7) shows 6 for a train facing east and 20 for one facing west. Where does the 20 come from?

Answer

Facing west, the train runs 7 moves to the dead end at (2,0), turns round there, and needs 13 more moves east to reach (2,13): 7 + 13 = 20. The switch at (2,4) is no help, because facing west it only allows straight on.

A plan is a line in time

Timetables and the reservation table

A plan says where every train is at every step. Railway planners have long drawn exactly this as a graphical timetable: position along the line across the page, time down it. A moving train is a slanted line; a waiting train is a vertical one.

Where two lines meet on the same track at the same time, the trains collide. The solver keeps every planned path in a reservation table that answers one question quickly: who is in cell x at step t? It’s the same picture, stored as a grid.

Try it — the timetable and the table

The track drawn above the chart is the x-axis; time runs down. Hover the chart, or focus it and use the arrow keys, to read where each train is.

train 0train 1in the sidingconflict

Beyond the paperSwap conflicts never show up as a shared cell: two trains trading places are never in the same cell at the same step. So the table also checks moves: entering cell b from cell a at step t is blocked if whoever was in b at step t − 1 is in a at step t. In the released C++ code both checks live in one function, ConstraintTable::is_constrained.

Watch forThe table treats a train as a point that fills one cell at a time. Real trains are long and occupy several track sections at once, which real timetabling has to model; Flatland abstracts that away, and so does everything on this page.

Drill — find the clash

With Each train alone selected, switch to the reservation table. Which cell and step do both trains reserve first? Where is that point in the timetable?

Answer

(2,7) at step 7 — a vertex conflict, the point where the two slanted lines cross. In the simulator the trains stop one cell short of it, nose to nose, as in move 1.

5

Plan around the others

Safe intervals instead of single steps

To plan one train among others already planned, search over (cell, heading, time) and skip every state the reservation table blocks. That is space-time A*. Its weak spot is waiting: a train that waits 30 steps in one cell costs 30 search nodes, and the search can spend most of its effort exploring different ways to wait.

Safe interval path planning (SIPP) groups the free steps of each cell into safe intervals: the longest runs of steps during which nobody else is there. A search node becomes (cell, heading, interval), and waiting inside an interval costs nothing extra. SIPP still finds the earliest arrival, because within one interval, arriving sooner is never worse than arriving later.

One Flatland touch in the code: a train that hasn’t left its station yet departs as late as it can without arriving any later, so it doesn’t sit on the network in other trains’ way.

Try it — safe intervals

One cell of the three-train example from move 6, over 30 steps. Coloured steps are reserved by trains already planned. Select any step to add or remove a reservation of your own (×) and watch the safe intervals underneath.

Planning one more trainSpace-time A*SIPP

Beyond the paperSIPP is from Phillips and Likhachev (2011), who designed it for robots moving among dynamic obstacles. In the port, for the last of 50 trains on a 60 × 60 map, it expanded 1,778 nodes against space-time A*’s 13,184 — about 7.4 times fewer — and ran about 8.8 times faster, arriving at the same step. The paper reports the same direction: with SIPP in place of A*, the entry finished three more instances within the 8-hour budget, and its score rose from 282.56 to 285.359.

Watch forSIPP’s arrival is the best for this train, given the trains already planned. That says nothing about the best plan for all of them together; the order in which they’re planned decides that (move 6).

Drill — intervals by hand

A cell is reserved at steps 3, 4 and 9 and free otherwise. List its safe intervals. A train has to wait in that cell from step 10 to step 40: how many search nodes does that wait cost space-time A*, and how many does it cost SIPP?

Answer

Three intervals: 0–2, 5–8, and 10 onwards. Space-time A* makes a node for every step of the wait — 31 of them, from 10 to 40 — while SIPP needs one, for the interval that starts at 10. You can build this cell in the lab by selecting steps until only 3, 4 and 9 are reserved.

Who goes first?

Prioritised planning, one train at a time

With many trains, the solver plans them one at a time in a priority order: prioritised planning (Silver, 2005). Each train gets a SIPP path that avoids the reservations of every train before it, then adds its own path to the table. Later trains must work around earlier ones; earlier trains never notice later ones.

It’s fast — the winning entry’s largest fully solved instance had 3,256 trains — but the order changes the result, and the solver can only guess a good one. The released code sorts the fastest trains first and, among equally fast trains, those nearest their goal.

The lab plans all six orders of three trains on a line with two sidings: trains 0 and 1 start in the west and head east, train 2 starts in the east and heads west.

Beyond the paperPrioritised planning is incomplete: an unlucky order can leave a later train with no path at all, even when a plan for everyone exists. Priority-based search (Ma and colleagues, 2019) searches over orders instead of fixing one. In Flatland, a train left without a path waits off the map and, unless a later replanning finds it one, counts as arriving at Tmax.

Watch forThree of the six orders tie here, so the default isn’t uniquely right — on this instance it simply avoids the bad ones. Orders that look fair can be costly: planning the lone westbound train first gets it home on time but costs the eastbound pair 16 steps between them.

Drill — the cost of going first

Which order is best for train 2, and what does it cost the other two? Which orders are worst overall, and how does the default avoid them?

Answer

Train 2 first: it arrives at step 19, as fast as it could alone, but the eastbound trains lose 7 and 9 steps (or 8 and 8), so the total rises from 59 to 70 — the two worst orders of the six. With the default, 1 → 0 → 2, train 2 alone gives way: it leaves its station 3 steps late and takes the southern siding while the others pass, 5 steps in all. One train giving way costs less than two.

7

Repair, don’t restart

Large neighbourhood search, and a small bandit

Prioritised planning gives a first plan quickly, but rarely a good one. Large neighbourhood search (LNS) improves it by repairs: pick a handful of trains — a neighbourhood — delete their paths, replan just those trains with prioritised planning in a random order, and keep the new paths only if the total travel time goes down. Otherwise, put the old paths back. Then repeat, for as long as the time budget allows.

The released code has three ways to pick a neighbourhood: a random walk from a badly delayed train that collects the trains in its way; trains that start at the same station; and trains that pass through the same switch. It chooses between them adaptively: a method that just improved the plan gains weight, and one that keeps failing slowly loses it.

That weighting rule is a small multi-armed bandit: several options, uncertain payoffs, and a running estimate of which pays best. It’s the place in this solver where learning fits most naturally.

    Beyond the paperLNS goes back to Shaw (1998), for vehicle routing, and choosing adaptively between neighbourhoods to Ropke and Pisinger (2006). Li and colleagues brought both to MAPF as MAPF-LNS (2021), the version this entry follows, and later MAPF-LNS2 (2022), which uses the same repair loop to remove collisions from an infeasible plan rather than to shorten a feasible one. Huang and colleagues (2022) replaced the hand-made choice of neighbourhood with a learned model that ranks the candidates. In the competition, LNS raised the entry’s score from 285.359 to 289.102, with four searches running in parallel on the four CPUs provided.

    Watch forThe paper also says the entry used simulated annealing “to determine the iteration limit for each instance”. That isn’t how repairs are accepted — they’re kept only when they help — and the released run script just holds a fixed table of iteration limits per difficulty level, “learnt from local instances”: none on the easiest and hardest levels, up to 1,230 in between. And the lab’s 37% gain is one small instance; on three other maps the port gained 8 to 15%.

    Drill — read the bandit

    Use the summary and the table under the charts. How many of the 400 iterations improved the plan, and which neighbourhood found most of the gain? Why might same switch have ended with so little weight?

    Answer

    109 iterations improved it, taking the total from 11,504 to 7,238 steps, 37% less. The random walk found 73 of the improvements and 3,085 of the 4,266 steps saved; the same start station found 33 (1,173 steps); the same switch, tried only 8 times, found 3 (8 steps). Its weight sank early, so it was rarely tried again — the classic bandit risk: an option that’s unlucky early may never get a fair second chance.

    When a train breaks down

    Keep the planned order, drop the clock

    Trains break down at random, so the timetable is wrong from the first breakdown on. Following the planned paths anyway is dangerous: a delayed train and an on-time one can end up nose to nose on the same track.

    Minimum communication policies (MCP; Ma, Kumar and Koenig, 2017) keep the plan’s order and drop its timing. For every cell the solver records the order in which trains will visit it, and a train may enter a cell only when it’s first in that cell’s queue. A delay can postpone a train but never reorder trains, so a collision-free plan runs without collisions or deadlocks however late anyone is.

    MCP can make trains wait for no good reason, though: everything queued behind a broken train waits too. So after a breakdown the solver also replans partially: it collects the switches and crossings the broken train has still to pass, finds the trains due through them after it, and replans those trains one at a time, within a 10-second limit.

    Try it — a breakdown

    Train 0 breaks down at step 3 for 12 steps (red ring). The dashed square is the switch at (2,4).

      Beyond the paperThe same idea — record who goes before whom at every shared place, and enforce only that — underlies the action dependency graph used to run fleets of warehouse robots (Hönig and colleagues, 2019). In the competition, partial replanning raised the entry’s score from 289.102 to 291.873.

      Watch forMCP’s guarantee rests on the plan: a planned collision would be carried out just as faithfully. And MCP keeps the order even when breaking it would be harmless — say, when the train ahead in a queue is broken down far away and the waiting train could slip past long before it recovers. That waste is what partial replanning is for.

      Drill — follow, or keep the order

      Play both modes. Following the paths, where do the trains lock, and why? With MCP, how late does each train arrive compared with the plan without a breakdown — step 13 for train 0, step 15 for train 1?

      Answer

      Following the paths, train 1 comes out of the siding onto the switch at (2,4) while train 0 is still broken down on (2,3), right in front of it. When train 0 recovers, the two face each other and neither can ever move. With MCP, train 1 waits at the end of the siding until train 0 has passed the switch. Train 0 arrives at step 25, 12 late — exactly its breakdown — and train 1 at step 20, 5 late: the price of waiting its turn.

      One train at a time

      Where the plan meets the simulator

      The planner assumes all trains move at once. Flatland’s simulator doesn’t: in each step it handles the trains one after another, in index order, and a train may only enter a cell that is empty at the moment it’s handled.

      So a train can follow another into a cell in the same step only if the leader is handled first. Otherwise it loses a step, which MCP absorbs. But a ring of trains, each wanting the cell the next one is leaving, can never move at all — although the planner’s conflict model, vertex and swap conflicts only, allows it.

      This one comes from the Python port, not the paper. On a dense test map, 12 trains locked in a ring and only 151 of 200 arrived. The port now refuses any planned move that would close such a ring; on that map, all 200 then arrived.

      Try it — handle the trains in order

      Every train wants to move one cell along its arrow in this step. The numbers are the order in which the simulator handles them.

      Beyond the paperIn the survey’s vocabulary, the planner allowed following and cycle conflicts, two kinds that MAPF variants often forbid because real robots — and real simulators — can’t always carry them out. The general lesson: the planner’s model of a conflict has to match how plans are actually executed. A first fix in the port, which forbade every move into a cell just left by a higher-numbered train, also ended the deadlocks but cost up to 6% of the score, because convoys could no longer leave stations nose to tail. Fix the precise failure, then measure the side effects.

      Watch forNot checked against the original: the ring was found in the port’s re-implementation of Flatland 2.2’s step rules. The port copies the rule of handling agents in index order, but the deadlock hasn’t been reproduced with the original C++ entry in the official environment.

      Drill — orders that work

      Four trains in a chain each want the cell ahead. Of the 24 possible handling orders, how many move the whole chain in one step? In the worst order, how many trains move? And a ring of four?

      Answer

      Exactly one: front to back, so every leader has moved before its follower is handled. In the worst order — back to front — only the front train moves; every other train finds its cell still occupied. The chain still advances over the next steps, with gaps. A ring of four moves in no order at all.

      The whole loop

      Plan once, then repair as you go

      Put together, the solver runs in two phases. Before departure it computes the distance tables, plans every train with prioritised planning and SIPP, improves the plan with LNS, and turns it into a visiting order for every cell. Then, at every step, it reads where the trains are, replans after a breakdown, and lets MCP decide which trains may move.

      One more idea completes the winning entry: lazy planning. On the largest instances it plans only some of the trains before departure and the rest during the episode. That keeps the network from being flooded all at once, and lets the later plans take account of breakdowns that have already happened.

      Beyond the paper — where learning standsFlatland was built as a reinforcement-learning benchmark (Mohanty and colleagues, 2020), yet in 2020 the best RL entry scored 214.15, a score this entry passed within its first 15 minutes; the competition report (Laurent and colleagues, 2021) describes the top RL approaches. So far learning has done better inside planners than instead of them — choosing LNS neighbourhoods, as in move 7 — or in decentralised settings where each agent sees only its surroundings, as in PRIMAL’s mix of reinforcement and imitation learning (Sartoretti and colleagues, 2019).

      Watch forThe code’s entry point is called PythonCBS, after Conflict-Based Search, a well-known optimal MAPF algorithm (Sharon and colleagues, 2015). Despite the name, none of its modes runs CBS: they are LNS, Parallel-LNS and CPR, a simpler scheme that reserves the direction of each track section on a train’s route.

      Drill — what was speed worth?

      From the table: which addition gained the most? What was the average reward per instance for the basic version, and for the final one?

      Answer

      Lazy planning, +5.63 of the 14.95 points gained in all. The average reward barely moved: 282.56 ÷ 349 ≈ 0.81 per instance for the basic version, 297.507 ÷ 362 ≈ 0.82 for the final one. Most of the gain came from finishing 13 more instances inside the 8 hours — in a time-boxed competition, speed is score.

      Eight lines to keep

      1

      Rails

      A state is a cell and a heading; trains can’t reverse, so a head-on meeting is permanent.

      2

      Distances

      One backward search per goal gives exact distances on an empty network.

      3

      Reservations

      Every planned path goes in a table of who is where, when; a collision check is a lookup.

      4

      SIPP

      Search over safe intervals, not single steps: the same earliest arrival, far fewer nodes.

      5

      Priorities

      Plan one train at a time; the order matters, and it’s only a guess.

      6

      LNS

      Repair a few trains at a time, keep only improvements, and let a bandit choose which trains.

      7

      MCP

      Run the plan by its order, not its clock; replan the trains a breakdown holds up.

      8

      Execution

      The planner’s idea of a conflict has to match how the simulator really moves trains.

      To check

      • The paper read for these notes is the three-page short version in the SoCS 2021 proceedings; the full ICAPS 2021 paper wasn’t read. Where a detail isn’t in the short version — the 16-bit cells, the three neighbourhoods and their weights, the default priority order, late departures, the per-cell queues — it comes from the released code.
      • The labs replay a Python port on small generated maps, not the original C++ on competition instances: the node counts, the 37% LNS gain and the 50-train episode’s 0.878 are the port’s. The port was run against its own re-implementation of the Flatland 2.2 simulator, not the official one.
      • The ring deadlock in move 9 comes from the port and hasn’t been reproduced with the original entry in the official environment.
      • Simulated annealing: the paper says it set each instance’s iteration limit; the released code has only a fixed table “learnt from local instances”. How the table was learnt isn’t stated.
      • Competition figures — more than 700 participants from 51 countries, more than 2,000 submissions, the 10-minute and 10-second limits, the scores and the 3,256-train instance — are as the authors report them; the AIcrowd leaderboard itself wasn’t checked.
      • Instance counts for the LNS and partial-replanning rows of the scoreboard aren’t given in the short paper, so the 13 extra instances can’t be split between the later additions.
      • NP-hardness (Yu and LaValle, 2013) is a result about optimal MAPF on general graphs, not a statement about Flatland’s rail variant.
      • The related work in the Beyond the paper boxes — Ropke and Pisinger, MAPF-LNS2, Huang and colleagues, Hönig and colleagues, PRIMAL — is summarised from memory, not re-read for these notes. Check the details before quoting them.
