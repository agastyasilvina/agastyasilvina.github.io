Topic 10 ended on a rule: characters want things, the wants collide, and characters get what they need rather than what they want. This lesson starts by showing that rule running inside a real opening chapter — then spends the rest of its time on the form where the rule has the least room to hide: the short story. Much of it is him reading his own work aloud; this page describes those passages rather than reproducing them.

Arc A · Wants in motion — The Graveyard Book, chapter one

3 moves
1

A hand in the darkness

The villain’s want, established in a paragraph

In the lectureWhat characters want and what they need always drive every story — how a character behaves, what’s going to happen, how they interact with everyone else. He picks up The Graveyard Book, which he hasn’t talked about before, and reads the opening: there was a hand in the darkness, and it held a knife. Very quickly we know we are dealing with a man, called the man Jack, walking around a house with a knife.

He reads on for two short paragraphs, and by the end of them the reader knows two things, in this order: what the man Jack has done — killed the family in the house, the mother, the father, the older child — and what he wants: one more. The baby.

Notice the economy. Topic 10’s orientation questions — who is this, what are they doing, why should I care? — are answered in about a hundred words, and the book is already into stakes. There is no mystery about the villain’s intent. The suspense is entirely about whether the want will be satisfied, which is a far stronger engine than hiding it would be.

And note what comes first: the deed, then the want. By the time you learn what he wants, you already know exactly what he’s capable of, so the want arrives carrying all the fear it needs.

2

The baby who wants out of the crib

The want that foils the villain without knowing he exists

In the lectureThe problem is, the baby isn’t going to be there — because when we meet the baby, what the baby wants is to get out of his crib. He has always been a wanderer and a climber. Woken by a crash downstairs, he gets bored and decides he can scale the side. He isn’t trying to escape. He just heads off — bumps down the stairs on his bottom, out of the door the man Jack left open when he crept in, and up the hill.

This is the most instructive move in the chapter, and it’s easy to read past. The baby’s want is completely innocent and completely unrelated to the villain’s. He doesn’t know there is a killer in the house; the crash that wakes him is the killing, and to him it is just a noise. Two wants that don’t know about each other collide, and the smaller one wins.

And look at where the escape route comes from: the door the man Jack himself left open. The rescue isn’t luck imposed from outside the story — it’s the consequence of what two characters wanted and did. That is precisely why it doesn’t feel like a cheat, which was the second half of topic 10’s definition of a story. A baby saved by coincidence would feel arranged. A baby saved by being exactly the sort of baby the book has just described feels inevitable.

3

Everybody wants something different

A whole first chapter built from colliding wants

In the lectureIn the graveyard, two ghosts, Mr and Mrs Owens, find the baby. “Now we have two ghosts in a graveyard and a baby, and we know that one of them wants to get involved and one of them doesn’t.” The man Jack arrives; they hesitate, about to leave the child — until the mother’s ghost appears, begging them to protect her son. Then a stranger called Silas deals with the man Jack and sends him away. The dead of the whole graveyard meet to argue about what to do. And the chapter ends with a child adopted by the inhabitants of a graveyard.

He walks through it as a list of wants, and laid out as one it is a small masterclass in topic 10’s recipe:

One pattern worth pulling out: the central deadlock — Mrs Owens against the whole graveyard — is resolved by two characters who weren’t part of it. Death arrives with a principle; Silas arrives with an offer. Neither side of the argument wins by argument. That is a very usable shape for any stalemate you’ve written yourself into.

“Every time one character wants something and another character wants something mutually exclusive, and they collide — every time that happens, you have a story.”

Neil Gaiman

And at the end of chapter one, the what’s-going-to-happen game from topic 10 is fully loaded: who is this child, and what is he going to be when he grows up? That question is the whole book, and it has been built entirely out of wants.

Arc B · What a short story is

3 moves
4

Close-up magic

The governing image for the rest of the lesson

In the lectureA good short story is a magic trick — close-up magic. Not the grand illusion where five people go into a box, there are fireworks and they’re gone. It’s the one where somebody shows you their hands are empty, covers them, and when they reveal them again there’s a rose. How did they do that? Was the rose always there? The rose must have been there.

The last three sentences are the ones that matter, because they describe what a good ending does to a reader. “Was the rose always there?” is the moment the reader replays the story in their head and realises the answer was planted all along. That is topic 10’s “not cheated”, scaled down to a form where there’s no room to hide anything: the reveal has to have been present, or the trick is a fraud.

“Close-up” is doing work too. A stage illusionist has distance, lighting and a hundred people to misdirect. A close-up magician is a foot from your face. In a short story every word is under that kind of scrutiny — there’s no spectacle to cover a clumsy move, which is the whole difficulty and the whole appeal.

5

Stories that stay

His introduction to M Is for Magic, described

He reads the whole introduction to M Is for Magic, a collection of his short stories for younger readers. Rather than reproduce it, here is what it says, in order:

  • Short stories fit the time a child actually has — a break, after lunch, a train. They set up, take you somewhere new, and deliver you back to school or home in half an hour.
  • Stories read at the right age never quite leave. You forget the title, the author, even what happened, and they still haunt the rarely-visited rooms of your mind.
  • Horror stays hardest. A story he read at nine ended with a room full of snails — probably man-eating — crawling slowly towards someone. He still gets the same creeps.
  • Fantasy gets into your bones. There’s a view of a village, hills and distant mountains that he can’t pass without The Lord of the Rings surfacing.
  • Science fiction puts you inside an alien head, which reminds you how little divides one person from another.
  • So short stories are tiny windows into other minds — journeys to the far side of the universe, and back in time for dinner.

Then the part that ties straight into this series. He’d been writing them for almost twenty-five years, and at the start they were how he learned the craft, because the hardest thing for a young writer is to finish something — and a short story is something you can finish. That is topic 09’s attic full of first pages, answered in an introduction written years before this class.

His favourite short-story writers as a boy he calls close-up conjurers: Saki, Harlan Ellison, John Collier, Shirley Jackson, Ray Bradbury — people who could make you laugh and break your heart in a handful of pages with just twenty-six letters and some punctuation. And a small consolation specific to the form: in a book of short stories, you don’t have to like them all. Another one will be along shortly.

The title is a tribute. Bradbury had gathered stories he thought younger readers would like into R Is for Rocket and S Is for Space; doing the same thing, Gaiman asked Bradbury whether he’d mind M Is for Magic. He didn’t.

Watch forIn a list of mid-century writers he read as a boy — Saki, Collier, Bradbury — the Jackson is Shirley Jackson, of “The Lottery” and The Haunting of Hill House. Not Shelley Jackson, a real but much later writer whose name is easy to confuse with hers.

6

The last chapter of a novel you didn’t write

Roger Zelazny’s advice, and why it works

In the lectureHe loved Roger Zelazny’s stories from nine or ten — the angst-ridden or super-competent people at the end of their tether, the poet’s precision with the right word in the right place. He didn’t really get to talk to him until he was thirty, at the World Fantasy Convention in Tucson, and asked him to talk about writing short stories, since he did it better than anyone. Zelazny thought, and said:

“All my best short stories are the last chapter of a novel I didn’t write.”

Roger Zelazny, as Gaiman remembers it

Gaiman’s gloss is the useful part. In short fiction you want the reader to feel — true or not — that these characters didn’t begin to exist when the story did. Things have happened to them. Things have brought them here. And now we’re at the point of heightened emotion, where the plot finally kicks in: the unravelling, the denouement, the last chapter.

The technique is implied history. You start at the climax and let the reader infer everything before it, which does two jobs at once: it saves words, and it makes characters feel real, because real people arrive in any scene carrying a past. His own reason for loving it is candid: “I am fundamentally lazy.” Write the eight thousand words; skip the hundred and thirty thousand.

A small satisfying connection: Zelazny is on the list of writers Gaiman imitated when he “sounded like everybody” in topic 09. Here, the same influence turns up as the source of the best technical advice in the lesson.

Drill — the novel underneath

Before writing a short story, list five things that happened to these characters before page one — a betrayal, a debt, an old promise, a death, a habit. Specific, not vague.

Then write the story without stating any of the five. They should leak into what people say, what they avoid, what they do without explanation. If a reader could guess at least two of them, you’ve written the last chapter of a novel.

Arc C · How to write one

4 moves
7

You’re paying by the word

The first principle he starts every short story from

In the lectureFor any writer writing a short story, the first place he starts is the idea that instead of being paid by the word, you are paying by the word. The fewer words you can use to tell the story, the better. Compression, economy — all of these are good.

It’s a small reframing that changes every decision. Paid by the word, every extra sentence is income; paying by the word, every sentence has to be worth what it costs. The question stops being “is this sentence good?” and becomes “would I pay for this one?” — which rules out the pleasant, harmless, decorative ones that a good sentence test lets through.

It’s topic 05’s Lou Reed lesson made into an editing rule: in a song there are so few words that each one carries weight, and the constraint is what teaches you. The short story is the prose form where you can impose that constraint on yourself.

Drill — the invoice

Take a finished page and imagine each sentence costs you money. Strike every sentence you wouldn’t pay for — not the bad ones, the unnecessary ones. Then read what remains aloud.

Most people find they can cut a fifth of a page without losing anything the reader would miss, and that the page moves faster for it.

8

Where does the penny drop?

A complete story in exactly one hundred words

In the lectureHe has written stories as short as a hundred words — technically one of exactly a hundred words, Nicholas Was…, collected in Smoke and Mirrors. He reads it. It’s very short, but he learned from it: about compression, and how to make the penny drop. Part of the fun is working out where in the story you realise that Nicholas is Santa Claus — where you learn that this is the punishment of Father Christmas.

You can read it in Smoke and Mirrors; it isn’t reproduced here. What matters for study is how it’s built, and the mechanism is clean enough to describe without quoting it.

It takes the most familiar figure in the Western world and describes him entirely from an unfamiliar angle — as a man in torment, living among beings he can’t talk to, forced out once a year against his will to perform a task at every child’s bedside. Nothing in it is false to the legend. Every detail is simply reframed from the prisoner’s side. Even the title runs straight into the first sentence, so it starts working before you’re aware you’re reading.

The result is that the penny drops at a different word for every reader. Some get it early and read the rest with dread; some get it only at the last line, which is “Ho, ho, ho”. Either way, the reader assembled the identity themselves — which is the rose from move 4. The trick works because the answer was in plain sight the whole time, described in words you’d never used for it.

Drill — a hundred words, one penny

Pick something everyone knows — a fairy tale, a famous figure, a household object — and describe it in exactly one hundred words, from an angle nobody uses, without ever naming it.

Give it to someone and ask them to mark the word where they got it. If it’s the first line, you’ve given it away; if it’s never, you’ve hidden it too well. The target is somewhere in the last third.

9

One thing

The discipline that makes compression possible

In the lectureThe glory of a short story is that just one thing has to happen. In novels, lots of things happen — a novel has to be about lots and lots of different things. A short story really only has to be about one thing. You can say that one thing, and then you can stop, and get on to something else.

This is what makes move 7 achievable rather than merely admirable. You can’t compress a story that’s trying to do four things; every one of them needs its own setup. A story that does one thing can spend every word on it.

It also gives you a diagnostic for a short story that won’t come right. If you find it needs two things to happen, it is almost always either two stories or the start of a novel — and the fix is to decide which, not to write faster.

“Then you can stop” is the permission to take seriously. A short story is allowed to end the moment its one thing has happened. Anything after is a novel clearing its throat.

10

Take the boat out

Why short fiction is where you learn the craft

In the lectureA lot of writers setting out assume they should go huge — a series of ten books, a mega-plot, a cast of thousands. That takes a great many skills, and even writers who’ve done it had to learn book by book. What’s lovely is short journeys: taking your boat out, going around the headland, and coming back in time for tea. Two thousand words, five thousand. A day, a weekend, a week. You can succeed, you can fail, you can learn — and the important thing is that you took the boat out.

The argument is really about the economics of learning. A novel is one experiment that takes a year to report back. A short story is an experiment that reports back in a weekend. Over the same year you run fifty tests instead of one, and each one ends — which, after topic 09, is the thing that actually teaches.

He lists what it’s good for testing: an idea (“can I make this happen?”), a tone of voice (“do I want to do that?”), something slightly mad. And the permission to start anywhere — from the setup, from the end, from something that happens in the middle: go and meet that middle thing and follow it and see where it goes. That’s topic 10’s “is it enough to start with?” with the stakes removed. In a novel, starting from the wrong fragment costs you months. In a short story, the worst case is his closing line:

“If it fails, it fails. It’s okay. You’ve lost three days. You’ve lost a week. No big deal.”

Neil Gaiman
Drill — around the headland

This weekend: one short story, under three thousand words, finished. Start from whatever fragment you have — beginning, end or middle — and don’t fix the other two in advance.

On Monday, write three lines: what you were testing, whether it worked, and what you learned. Do it four weekends running and read the twelve lines together. That page is your syllabus.
