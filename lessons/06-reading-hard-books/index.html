The claim underneath this lesson is that the interesting number is not how many books you read but how many changed you — and that reading the kind of book which can change you is a learnable procedure rather than a talent. What follows is that procedure, restaged into the three stages Adler actually uses, because the flat list of nine rules hides the shape.

Arc A · The stance

3 moves
1

The fork in the road

Why the narrator went looking for a method

In the lectureLong obsessed with stories, but after college the reading dropped to maybe one or two books a year — and not ones that challenged him or grew him in any meaningful way. Having reorganised his life around reading again, he hit a fork: would he have the stamina and the courage to read hard books, or keep opting for pure entertainment?

Two separate problems sit here, and most reading advice conflates them. Reading more is a scheduling problem — solvable with a habit, a chair and a phone left in another room. Reading harder is not. You can fix the first completely and still spend every one of those hours on books that ask nothing of you.

The word he reaches for is courage, and it is the right one. The barrier to a difficult book is not time; it is the specific discomfort of reading a page and knowing you did not understand it. That feeling is unpleasant enough that people reliably reorganise their reading to avoid it, and then describe the result as taste.

Drill — the honest count

List every book you finished in the last twelve months. Beside each, write one sentence: what did it change? An opinion, a habit, a piece of vocabulary, a way of seeing something.

Books with a blank beside them weren’t wasted — entertainment is a legitimate use of an evening. But the count you are actually interested in is the number of non-blank lines, and for most people it is startlingly close to zero.

2

Equals and unequals

Adler’s distinction between passive and active reading

In the lecturePassive reading is solely for information or entertainment. Every piece of writing is a conversation between equals or unequals — a magazine article written for a general audience is a back-and-forth between equals. Active reading is the way to overcome the superiority of an author who, at the beginning, knows more than we do. It’s our job to understand what the book is trying to teach, and we do it by discovery: research, investigation, reflection.

The distinction is not about genre, and that is the part worth holding on to. It is about the gap between you and the writer. A text pitched at your level can only inform you — it can rearrange facts you were already equipped to receive. Only a text above your level can teach you, because teaching requires someone who knows something you don’t.

Which produces an uncomfortable corollary. Difficulty is the signal that you are in the right book, not the wrong one. The instinct to close a book because it is over your head is exactly inverted — being over your head is the precondition for the thing you wanted.

And “learning by discovery” is doing real work in that sentence: with no instructor in the room, the labour that a teacher would normally supply has to come from you. That labour is what the nine rules are.

Watch forA test that falls out of this, and it is unforgiving. If you finished a book and struggled with nothing and disagreed with nothing, it was entertainment — whatever its subject, however serious the cover. That is not a criticism of the book. It is information about the gap, and therefore about what it was able to do for you.

3

The four levels

The map for the rest of the lesson

In the lectureAdler says active reading has four components: elementary, inspectional, analytical and syntopical. We’re going to skip elementary and jump straight into inspectional.

LevelThe question it answersWhat you actually do
ElementaryWhat does the sentence say?Decoding. Assumed of any adult reader, which is why the video skips it.
InspectionalWhat is this book about, and is it for me?Skim with purpose, under time pressure. Decide whether to commit.
AnalyticalWhat exactly is it saying, and is it true?The nine rules. Unlimited time, one book, chewed thoroughly.
SyntopicalWhat does the literature say about my question?Many books at once, compared on terms you set.

Watch forThe video calls them “components”, which loses something. In Adler they are levels, and they are cumulative — each one contains the ones beneath it. Analytical reading doesn’t replace inspectional; it begins with it. Syntopical reading uses both. So this is not a menu of four techniques to pick from, it is a ladder, and skipping a rung shows.

Arc B · Inspectional reading

1 move
4

Skim on purpose

Triage, before you spend the hours

In the lectureInspectional reading is light, almost superficial — but done with a purpose: to understand quickly what a book is about and its overall vibe, before spending hours on a book that might not be a fit. Unless a word is absolutely blocking comprehension, keep skimming and let the text wash over you. You have different gears, and you get better at modulating them.

The permission buried in this section is the most liberating thing in the lesson. If you skip words you didn’t understand and come away with half the book, Adler holds that this beats understanding nothing because you quit the moment the text turned difficult. Half of a hard book is not a failed reading. It is a successful first pass.

The gears idea is worth practising deliberately rather than waiting for it to arrive. Most people read everything at one speed — the speed at which they could, in principle, follow every clause — and then treat any book that resists that speed as impossible. Reading fast over the parts you don’t need is what buys you the time to read slowly over the parts you do.

Watch forAdler splits this level in two, and the video merges them. Systematic skimming is the pre-read: title page, preface, table of contents, index, the publisher’s blurb, then dipping into the chapters that look pivotal — this is the part that actually answers “should I commit to this?”. Superficial reading is the other half: go through the whole thing once, start to finish, never stopping for what you don’t understand. The video’s advice is the second half. The first half is the one that saves you the wasted hours, and it takes about fifteen minutes.

Drill — the fifteen-minute triage
  1. Title page and preface. Authors say what they are doing here, plainly, because they are still nervous.
  2. Table of contents, slowly. It is the author’s own outline — the thing rule 3 will later ask you to reconstruct.
  3. Index. Look up the terms you already care about; the page counts tell you where the weight sits.
  4. Dip into two or three pivotal chapters, plus the last few pages of the argument — writers usually summarise themselves at the end.
  5. Decide: commit, shelve, or abandon. Write the decision down with one line of why. That line is worth having when you meet the book again in three years.

Arc C · Analytical reading — the nine rules

6 moves

Analytical reading is what he calls chewing and digesting a book — and the phrase is older than Adler: Francis Bacon’s Of Studies has some books to be tasted, others swallowed, “and some few to be chewed and digested”. Fantasy, romance and thrillers rarely need it. Hard philosophy, science, some history and the classics do, and the nine rules are the structure that lets you extract the learning without a professor in the room.

The video gives the nine as a flat list. They aren’t one: Adler groups them into three stages, each answering a different question, and the grouping is most of the value.

5

Rule 1 — what kind of book is it?

Stage one, structural: what is this book about as a whole?

In the lectureIdentify the kind of book you’re reading. Adler breaks them into two: theoretical — what it is — and practical — what it ought to be. Then write it on the inside cover or in your commonplace book.

His examples: Penrose’s The Emperor’s New Mind and Hoffman’s The Case Against Reality are theoretical — they make a claim about the way the world is. How to Read a Book itself is practical, as is How to Take Smart Notes. And a nice stretch: Seneca’s letters count as practical under Adler’s definition, because their aim is to tell us how to act.

Then the honest wrinkle. Sapolsky’s Behave is a science book describing how the world is — theoretical — but it also wants to inspire specific actions, which makes it practical. His ruling: pick one, or both, it doesn’t matter. The value is not the label. It is that you have started sorting books in your head instead of receiving them all the same way.

Why it earns its place as rule one: the category tells you what would count as the book being wrong. A theoretical book fails if it is false. A practical book fails if the advice doesn’t work, or if the end it aims at turns out not to be worth wanting — a completely different kind of objection, which you cannot raise if you never noticed which sort of book you were holding.

6

Rules 2–4 — unity, parts, problems

The rest of stage one

2

State the unity of the book in one to three sentences

A general idea isn’t enough — you have to be able to say it. If you catch yourself saying “I know what it’s about, I just can’t say it exactly”, that is a good sign you did not understand it.

3

Outline the parts that make up that unity

The author chose a structure because they believed it was needed. That doesn’t absolve you from stating the parts in your own words — and your outline may not match the book’s chapters, which is fine. It only has to support the unity you stated.

4

Work out what problems the author is trying to solve

Books begin with a question. Sometimes the author flags them in headings; often not, and pulling them out is your job. Theoretical questions: does it exist, what kind of thing is it, why, what caused it, what is it for? Practical: what ends should be sought, by what means, and under these conditions what is the right thing to do?

His worked example of a unity, from a novel he had just finished: a young, amoral man dreams of making it big as a photographer, puts his ambition above everything, and ends up making a Faustian bargain. Twenty-four words, and you could argue with it — which is the test. A summary nobody could disagree with is usually a summary that says nothing.

These three are a closed loop, and that is how you use them. The unity, the outline and the problems have to agree with each other. If your outline doesn’t obviously support your one-sentence unity, one of the two is wrong — and noticing the mismatch is cheaper than reading the book twice.

Drill — the inside-cover card

For the next book you finish, write four things on the inside cover or in your notes, in this order: the kind (theoretical or practical), the unity in under three sentences, your outline in five or six lines, and the questions it set out to answer.

Do it from memory first, then check. Whatever you couldn’t produce from memory is the part you read passively.

7

Rule 5 — coming to terms

Stage two, interpretive — and the single highest-leverage rule

In the lectureUnderstand the major words and phrases the author uses — Adler calls it coming to terms. The difficult words are usually the ones you need to crack to unlock the whole argument. He recommends a two-column page: terms on one side, meanings on the other. If you want to read books outside your current level, this tip alone is probably the highest-leverage thing you can do.

The narrator is right to single it out, and it is worth being precise about what a term is, because it is not the same as a word. A term is a word used in one unambiguous sense. One word carrying two senses is two terms; two different words carrying the same sense are one term. Coming to terms means locating the sense this author is using — which is why a dictionary is so often useless here. The dictionary gives you the range. The book gives you the choice within it.

And this is the hinge of the whole system. Rules 1–4 work on the book from the outside — its kind, its shape, its questions. Rule 5 is where you go inside the argument, and every rule after it depends on having done it. An argument you cannot follow is, nine times out of ten, an argument built from a term you quietly translated into your own meaning.

Drill — the term sheet

Keep one page, two columns, at the front of the book. Every time a word stops you — or worse, nearly stops you and you carried on — write it on the left. Leave the right blank until you can fill it from the book itself rather than from a dictionary.

The blanks are the map of what you don’t understand yet, and they usually number about six. Six words is a much smaller problem than “this book is too hard”.

8

Rules 6–7 — propositions and arguments

Stage two continued

6

Mark the important sentences, and find the propositions in them

Propositions are the author’s judgements — what they actually assert. If the author hasn’t made the key sentences obvious, finding them is your job, not theirs.

7

Locate or construct the basic arguments

An argument is propositions arranged to convince you of something. When they’re spread across pages, you have to assemble them — on a separate sheet, or just by numbering the propositions in the margins in the order the argument needs them.

Two ideas in this section are worth more than the rules themselves.

“An essential part of reading is to be perplexed — and to know it.” If you never stop to question a passage, you cannot expect insights you did not already have. Perplexity isn’t the interruption of reading; it is the part where reading is happening. The failure mode is not confusion, it is unnoticed confusion.

The sin of verbalism. Adler’s name for being able to repeat an author’s words while being unable to make their argument in your own. It is the exact failure that rule 2 catches at the level of the whole book, appearing again at the level of a single argument — and it is the natural end state of highlighting.

The margin-numbering trick is the cheap version of rule 7 and it works because an argument is not a set of claims, it is a set of claims in an order. Numbering makes the order visible, and a gap in the numbers is usually a premise the author assumed you would supply.

Drill — the verbalism test

Close the book. Say the argument out loud in your own words, using none of the author’s distinctive phrases. If you can only produce their sentences, you have the words and not the thought — go back and number the propositions.

9

Rule 8 — did they answer it?

The end of stage two

In the lectureOnce you have a grasp of the arguments, go back to the questions from rule 4 and check: which did the author answer, which did they dodge, and what new questions came up along the way? Did they do the job well enough?

Short rule, and it is the reason rule 4 had to be written down. Judged from memory, you will silently grade the book on the questions it did answer — the ones it handled well are the ones you remember it raising. The dodged question leaves no trace unless you recorded it before the author had a chance to distract you from it.

Three outcomes worth logging separately: answered, dodged, and newly raised. The third is not a failure — a book that opens a question it cannot close has still done something for you, and that question is often the seed of the next book you should read.

10

Rule 9 — understand before you judge

Stage three, critical

In the lectureBefore you can agree or disagree, you must first understand. By the end you either understood it or you didn’t — there’s no middle ground. Understood and agree? Your work is done. Understood and disagree? Write down why, in your commonplace book or your outline. That is the crux of critical reading: to show where the author was uninformed, misinformed or illogical.

This is the rule people skip, and skipping it is what most book opinions are made of. “I didn’t like it” delivered before “I understood it” is not criticism, it is a report on the reader.

Note what the three grounds have in common: every one of them is a claim you have to be able to support. Uninformed — they lack a fact that bears on the case, and you can name it. Misinformed — they assert something false, and you can say what is true instead. Illogical — the conclusion doesn’t follow from their own premises, and you can point at the step. None of them is “I disagree”.

Watch forThe video gives three grounds; Adler gives four. The missing one is “your analysis is incomplete” — the author hasn’t solved all the problems they set out to solve, or hasn’t seen the implications of their own position. It is the most useful of the four in practice, because it is the most often true. Adler also attaches two conditions the video skips: don’t disagree for the sake of disagreeing, and give reasons — treat a disagreement as something that could in principle be resolved, and leave room for the possibility that the one who is wrong is you.

Worth connecting to topic 01: the obligation to state the author’s case before objecting to it is the steelman, arriving from a different direction. Adler’s test and Socrates’ are the same test — you have not earned a disagreement until you can make the other side’s argument yourself.

Drill — earn the disagreement

Next time a book annoys you, write two paragraphs before you write anything else: their argument, at its strongest, in your words — then your objection, labelled with which of the four grounds it stands on.

A surprising number of objections fail to survive the first paragraph. Those are the ones worth catching before you repeat them at a dinner party.

Arc D · Syntopical reading

1 move
11

Make the books come to terms with you

The level above analytical — reading on a mission

In the lectureSyntopical reading is for when you’re on a mission — two or more books on the same topic, because you have a specific question you want answered. You’re assembling your own bibliography. Start with inspectional reading, to find out fast whether a book will answer your question before you sink hours into one that won’t.

Then the inversion that gives the level its whole character: you make the books come to terms with you, rather than coming to terms with each of them in turn. You fix your own vocabulary first. Where an author uses a different word for something you have already named, you bucket it under yours.

His example is a good one because it is genuinely hard. Reading Spinoza’s Ethics, you meet mode and substance — words Aristotle and Descartes also use, and which Spinoza is deliberately redefining while writing in response to them. Taken at face value the result is chaos: three thinkers, the same words, three meanings. Anchored to your own term, it becomes a comparison — here is my concept, here is what each of them says about it.

That inversion is the difference between reading a lot and doing research. Come to terms with each author in sequence and you end up serially adopting five incompatible vocabularies, unable to put any two of them in the same sentence. The books can only be compared on ground that belongs to you.

The rest of the level, in his order: get the questions clear, define the issues — the places where the authors genuinely disagree — and then analyse the discussion. That last step has a name worth keeping: dialectical objectivity, which means laying out every side without taking one.

Which is topic 01’s steelman again, scaled up from an argument to a literature: state each position at its strongest, including the ones you came in wanting to beat. Adler’s version is stricter, though — in a syntopical reading you are not supposed to resolve it at all. The output is a map of the disagreement, not a verdict.

Drill — a five-book question
  1. Write one question you actually want answered. Narrow enough that a book could fail to address it.
  2. Assemble five candidates and give each the fifteen-minute triage from move 4. Keep the ones that speak to the question; shelve the rest without guilt.
  3. Write your terms down first — three or four words you will use throughout, defined in your own sentences.
  4. For each book, record what it says about your terms, not what it says about its own.
  5. List the points where they genuinely disagree. That list is the answer you were looking for — more useful than any single book’s verdict.

revision & checking

His own closing note is the right one to keep: at first the rules feel like too much, and you overthink every step — then it becomes like riding a bicycle and you stop counting the steps. And the last instruction, which is not about reading at all: resist the temptation to keep scrolling. Close the browser, turn off the wifi, and go and get the difficult book you have been putting off.
