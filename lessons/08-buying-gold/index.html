The numbers on screen

The stack, and whether the arithmetic holds

In the lecture123.2 troy ounces — 91 one-ounce bars from Argor-Heraeus and 10 hundred-gram bars from Valcambi. About 3.8 kg, or 8.4 pounds. At the $3,401 per ounce quoted while filming, worth $419,003, against roughly $267,284 paid. Up about 56% in under two years. “I got pretty lucky with the timing.”

House rule on this page and the others: recompute, don’t repeat. So:

Five of six check out exactly — the weights, the ounce conversion and the valuation are all internally consistent, which is more than most finance videos manage. The stated gain is $160 light against his own two figures. Trivial in itself; worth noticing because it tells you the numbers were typed rather than derived.

And note the sentence most people skip past: “I got pretty lucky with the timing.” He is not claiming skill for the 56%. Keep that in mind when arc C gets to what the percentage does and doesn’t prove.

2

Why it went up — his three reasons

The explanation offered for the run

  1. Central bank buying. His framing: the institutions that control entire countries’ currencies — the people who literally print the money — have been buying gold heavily.
  2. Geopolitics. Wars, trade fights, political tension. When people are nervous about the future they buy gold, and have for centuries.
  3. Inflation. Offered without elaboration, on the grounds that you experience it yourself.

The first is the strongest of the three, and it is the one with an actual mechanism: a central bank shifting reserves into bullion is a large, price-insensitive buyer, and unlike a retail investor it is not going to panic-sell in a drawdown. The second and third are real forces, but they are also the explanations that get offered for every gold move, in both directions.

Watch forAll three are narratives about why gold went up, produced after it went up. That is not a criticism of their truth — it is a warning about what they can be used for. The question that matters for a buyer today isn’t whether these forces are real, it’s whether they are already in the price, and that question isn’t asked. Everyone reading the same headlines has already bought.

3

Money, not an investment

The actual thesis, and where it came from

In the lectureHe started buying to keep savings somewhere safe until the right opportunity came. Reading the history of money, one pattern kept recurring: thousands of paper currencies, and every one eventually went to zero — the dollar itself having lost about 98% of its value since 1913. Then a Ray Dalio interview: gold is a currency; we have dollars, euros, yen and gold, and if you own a currency it makes sense to own some gold. The click: gold isn’t an investment, it’s a more durable form of money to store savings in.

This is the load-bearing idea of the whole video, and it is worth separating from the price chart. He is not claiming gold produces returns. He is claiming it is a place to keep money that doesn’t erode — and that the only reason gold stopped being money is that it can’t be printed.

The origin story is the human part, and it is more instructive than the thesis. In 2018 he found $1,300 for an ounce of gold intimidating, so he bought a one-ounce silver coin for about $20 instead. Then another. He carried them around, and describes the appeal plainly: solid, cold, heavy — real wealth, not numbers on a screen. That’s what eventually got him into gold.

Watch forTwo claims here need handling. “Every single paper currency went to zero” is too strong: most historical currencies were replaced — redenominated, or folded into a successor like the euro — rather than inflated into worthlessness, and holders were generally converted rather than wiped out. And “the dollar lost 98% since 1913” is roughly right as a statement about cash, and says nothing about savings, because almost nobody held banknotes for a century. That distinction is the whole of move 9.

Worth adding that the Dalio line is an argument for a small allocation — “at least a small portion” is doing work in that quote, and it is a materially different claim from the video’s own 3.8 kilos.

4

Speculation, admitted

The most honest thirty seconds in the video

In the lecture“A real investment is something you’ll hold for 20 or 30 years. If you’re buying in the hope it rises over the next two or three, that’s speculation.” And then: yes, he was speculating that gold would hold its value better than paper currencies — and so far that speculation has paid off.

Give him this one. A video whose entire premise is a 56% gain, stopping to say what I did was speculate, and it happened to work, is doing something most of the genre never does. It also quietly undercuts the thumbnail: if the position is a speculation, the gain is evidence about a bet that came in, not about a method you can copy.

He also volunteers that he hasn’t checked what the same money would have done in stocks, and that it might have done better. That is an unusual thing to leave in.

The distinction itself is the same one topic 04 runs on — a long horizon changes what counts as evidence. A two-year result tells you about the two years. It does not tell you the strategy was right, and he says so.

5

Is it still a good time?

His four questions

In the lectureNobody can predict the perfect time, but here is how he thinks about it. Are we still printing money? Are central banks still accumulating gold? Do major currencies still lose purchasing power to inflation? Are there still tensions between major countries? If most of the answers are yes — and right now they are — there’s still uncertainty in the system, and it makes sense to hold some gold.

The framework is clear and testable, which is more than most, and it has one structural problem worth naming: the answers have been “yes” almost continuously for fifty years. Money has been printed, currencies have lost purchasing power and countries have been in tension through periods when gold tripled and through periods when it fell by two thirds and stayed there.

A test that is always on cannot distinguish a good time from a bad one. What it actually supports is a different and more defensible position: hold a permanent allocation, because the conditions are permanent — which is a sensible thing to believe, and which removes timing from the question entirely. Sold as a timing signal it promises something it can’t deliver.

Drill — make the test falsifiable

Take his four questions and add the one that makes them a test rather than a mood: what would have to be true for the answer to be no? Write down, now, what would make you stop holding gold — a price, a real interest rate, a change in central bank behaviour.

If nothing would, you don’t have a thesis, you have an allegiance — the same trap as topic 01, move 2, and topic 04, move 11.

6

Bars and stamps

The practical part, and it’s the best part of the video

In the lectureStick with small bars from well-known refiners — Argor-Heraeus, Valcambi, PAMP Suisse, Perth Mint, Royal Canadian Mint. A legitimate bar has four things stamped on it: the weight, the purity, the refiner’s name or logo, and a unique serial number. “That’s like the bar’s passport.”

Those four stamps are the single most transferable thing in the video — a checklist you can run in a shop, in thirty seconds, without knowing anything about metals. Recognised refiners matter for the same reason: a bar from a name the whole market knows is one a buyer will take back without assay, and resale is where obscure product hurts you.

His preference for one-ounce bars over one large one is a liquidity argument, and the analogy is good: twenty-dollar bills rather than hundreds. If you need to sell some, you sell some — rather than breaking up a single large holding you can only sell whole.

He is also straight about the cost of that: smaller bars carry higher premiums — the amount over spot that covers the dealer’s costs — and he judges the flexibility worth it. That’s a real trade-off, fairly stated. Move 11 puts numbers on it.

7

Where to buy

Choosing a dealer

In the lectureLook for established dealers with many positive reviews, transparent pricing and clear buyback policies. Don’t shop on price alone — compare delivery times, customer service, and whether they educate you or just push high-margin products. For a first purchase, buy from two different dealers so you can compare. And don’t ignore online dealers: lower overhead often beats local coin shops even after delivery, and you can lock in the market price immediately.

The two-dealer tip is genuinely clever and costs nothing but a bit of admin — you learn more about a dealer from one transaction than from a hundred reviews, and doing two in parallel gives you a baseline.

Of his four criteria, one matters far more than the others and it is the one people skim: a clear buyback policy. Everything about buying is visible and competitive; the exit is where the cost hides. A dealer who will quote you their buy price today, in public, before you have bought anything, is telling you what the round trip actually costs.

“Whether they educate you or push high-margin products” is worth keeping too — the high-margin end of this market is collectible and commemorative coins, where you pay a large premium for a mintage story that the next buyer may not value at all.

8

The treasure map

Storage — and the one idea here worth stealing whatever you own

In the lectureTwo priorities when storing: accessibility and security. A small amount close to home for emergencies; the majority in high-security insured storage he can reach 24/7. The golden rule: never keep it all in one place — spread the risk, and make sure every location is both secure and reachable when you need it.

Then the part that deserves to outlive the video. He keeps what he calls a treasure map: a document with the complete access details, with copies given to two trusted family members, so that if something happens to him they know exactly how to retrieve everything.

Self-custody has a failure mode nobody plans for, and it isn’t theft — it is the owner being hit by a bus. Metal in an undisclosed location, a safe nobody can open, a vault account nobody knows exists: all of it is simply gone, and the more careful you were, the more thoroughly it is gone. This generalises well past gold, to crypto keys, safe deposit boxes and any account that isn’t in a will.

He also mentions a vaulted option for people who don’t want to deal with storage — a Swiss dealer offering free insured storage up to a threshold, instant sell-back at market price, which he says he has used for three years and which he discloses is linked in the description but not sponsored.

Watch forTwo things about “free insured storage”. Free storage is a customer-acquisition cost — it’s paid for by the buy/sell spread, so it isn’t free, it’s bundled. And storing metal with the dealer who sold it to you puts your holding back inside somebody else’s balance sheet, which is precisely the exposure the physical thesis exists to avoid. That isn’t a reason not to do it — it’s convenient and the firm may be excellent — but it means you are no longer making the argument from move 3. Which is exactly the issue in your bank question, at move 12.

Drill — write the treasure map tonight

Whatever you own that only you can reach — accounts, keys, a safe, a vault login — write the retrieval instructions as though for someone who knows none of it. Where it is, how to get in, who to contact, what proof they’ll need.

Then give it to two people, separately. One is a single point of failure; two is a system. This takes twenty minutes and is the highest-value thing on this page for most people, gold or no gold.

9

The wrong benchmark

The biggest gap, and it’s a logical one rather than a factual one

“The dollar has lost 98% of its value since 1913” is approximately true and almost entirely beside the point, because it measures banknotes in a drawer. Nobody held cash for a century. The comparison that would settle anything is gold against the alternatives people actually used to store savings — bonds, property, a share in productive businesses — and over that same window the equity comparison is not close.

So the argument proves less than it appears to. Currency debasement is an argument against holding currency. It is not, by itself, an argument for gold; it is equally an argument for a house, a bond ladder, or an index fund. To get from “cash decays” to “therefore gold” you need a second premise — that gold beats those other options, or does something they can’t — and that premise is never stated, let alone defended.

There is a good version of the second premise, and it is worth having because it is the honest case: gold does something an index fund can’t, which is exist outside the financial system. It has no issuer, no counterparty and no default. That is a real property, it is the one he is actually buying, and it is worth paying something for. It is just not the same claim as “gold preserves wealth better”, and the video slides between the two.

Cash decays → therefore gold. The arrow is doing work that nobody has shown it can do.

10

The 1980 problem

What “store of value” looks like on a bad entry

“Gold holds its value over 5,000 years” and “gold will hold my savings over 30 years” are different claims, and only the first one is safe. The counter-example is specific: gold peaked in January 1980 around $850 an ounce and did not return to that level in nominal terms until 2008 — roughly 28 years. Adjusted for the inflation it was bought to protect against, the wait was far longer still.

Which is the exact failure the purchase was meant to prevent. Someone who bought at that top, for precisely the reasons given in this video, spent most of a working life underwater in real terms. It is the same shape as the Japan case in topic 04: a long-run average that is perfectly true and perfectly useless if your thirty years happen to be the wrong thirty.

And the structural point underneath it: gold pays nothing. No dividend, no coupon, no rent. The entire return is the price going up, so there is no income to carry you through a flat decade — and with physical metal the carry is actually negative, because storage and insurance cost money every year you hold it. A share that falls 20% and pays a dividend and a bar that falls 20% and charges you rent are not the same position.

Watch forNone of this says gold is bad. It says the entry price matters enormously for an asset with no income, which sits awkwardly beside a video whose thesis is that timing doesn’t matter because the conditions are permanent. Both can’t be fully true. If timing doesn’t matter, the 1980 buyer should have been fine.

11

The round trip

The cost nobody quotes

Physical gold has a spread in both directions: you pay a premium over spot to buy, and you sell back below spot. Both together are the round trip, and it is the number that decides whether small-bar flexibility is worth what it costs.

His own figures let you estimate it. April 2023, 85 one-ounce bars, about $185,000:

$185,000 ÷ 85 one-ounce bars$2,176 / oz
Approximate spot, April 2023≈ $2,000 / oz
Implied premium over spot≈ 8–9%

Higher than the 3–5% usually quoted for one-ounce bars — consistent with his own point that small bars cost more, or with the figures being rounded. The spot price here is from memory; check it before relying on the number. Either way the method is the thing: divide what you paid by what you got, and compare it to spot on the day.

Add a sell-side discount of a few percent and the round trip on small bars can be roughly a tenth of the position. So the headline 56% is nearer 45% once you account for getting in and out — still an excellent two years, and a materially different number from the one on the thumbnail.

This is also the honest answer to “why would anyone hold paper gold?” The round trip on an exchange-traded fund or a bank account is a fraction of this. You are paying that difference for the property in move 9 — no counterparty — and if that property isn’t why you’re buying, you are paying for something you don’t want.

What you actually own

Five ways to hold gold, and the one distinction that decides everything

“Buying gold” covers five quite different things. They give you similar price exposure and wildly different legal positions, which is the part that only matters on the day it matters.

How you hold itWhat you legally ownThe trade
Physical, at homeThe metal. Nobody else is involved.No counterparty at all — and all the theft, insurance and round-trip cost is yours.
Physical, allocated vaultSpecific bars, by serial number, in your name. The vault is a custodian, holding your property.Storage fee, but the bars aren’t the vault’s asset — they sit outside its balance sheet if it fails.
Unallocated account
(the typical bank product)		A claim on the bank, denominated in grams. No particular metal is yours.Cheap, instant, no storage hassle — and you are an unsecured creditor of that bank.
Gold ETFUnits in a fund that holds allocated bullion through a custodian.Cheapest and most liquid; still a chain of institutions between you and the metal.
Mining sharesA company. Not gold.Operational, political and management risk on top of the gold price. A different asset.

So what is a bank gold savings account?

The product OCBC and other banks in the region offer under names like gold savings account or precious metals account is, in the typical case, the third row: an unallocated account. You pay in currency, the balance is expressed in grams or ounces, it tracks the gold price, and you can usually buy and sell within the app at a spread far tighter than any coin dealer will give you.

What you generally do not get is metal with your name on it. There is usually no physical delivery, or delivery only by converting into bars at extra cost, in set sizes, with notice. And because the balance is a claim rather than a deposit, it is typically outside deposit-insurance protection — in Singapore, the SDIC scheme covers qualifying deposits, and precious metals accounts are generally not among them. Verify that for your specific account; it is the single most important line in the product sheet.

The point for your questionGo back to move 3. The video’s entire thesis is that gold is money with no issuer and no counterparty — the thing that survives when paper claims don’t. An unallocated bank account is a paper claim on a bank. It is an excellent way to own the price of gold and a poor way to own the reason he gives for owning gold. If the scenario you're insuring against is one where banks are in trouble, the insurance is issued by the thing you're insuring against.

That is not an argument against the bank account. It is an argument that it answers a different question — and for most people, most of the time, the different question is the one they actually have.

13

Which one fits which reason

The decision, in the only order that works

The mistake is choosing the format first and the reason afterwards. Reason first, and the format falls out:

If your reason is…ThenBecause
A diversifier in a portfolio — I want gold’s price behaviour alongside my other holdingsBank account or ETFYou want the price, not the metal. Physical is an expensive way to buy a price.
Insurance against a systemic or banking failurePhysical, self-held or allocated vaultAn unallocated claim fails in precisely the scenario you bought it for.
“Money outside the system” — the video’s own thesisPhysical onlyEvery other format reintroduces the counterparty the thesis exists to remove.
Accumulating a bit every monthBank account or ETFNo round-trip premium on small amounts; convert to physical later if the reason changes.
I want to learn, and hold one in my handOne small physical bar or coinTreat the premium as tuition. This is how the video’s author started — a $20 silver coin.

Most people, honestly answered, are in row one — and row one is where the bank account and the ETF win comfortably on cost, liquidity and hassle. The video is written by someone in row three, for whom physical is the only option that makes sense. Both are coherent. They are just not the same purchase, and the video presents one as though it were the other.

What to ask the bank before you open anything

This is the actual deliverable. Whatever the provider, these seven questions separate the product categories from each other, and every one of them has an answer in writing somewhere:

  1. Is the account allocated or unallocated? If unallocated — am I an unsecured creditor of the bank?
  2. Is it covered by deposit insurance? Usually no. Get the answer in writing rather than assuming.
  3. What is the buy/sell spread, and are there monthly or maintenance fees? What’s the round trip on a small amount?
  4. Can I convert to physical metal? At what cost, in what bar sizes, with how much notice, and is it guaranteed or subject to availability?
  5. What happens if the bank fails, or simply decides to withdraw the product? Where do I sit in the queue?
  6. Is the quoted price the market price or the bank’s own quote, and how far can the two diverge?
  7. How is it taxed where I live? Physical investment-grade gold and paper gold products are often treated quite differently — in Singapore, qualifying physical Investment Precious Metals have a GST exemption that does not automatically extend to every paper product. Check the current rules rather than trusting any summary, including this one.
Drill — answer the prior question first

Before comparing any products, write one sentence: I want gold because ______, and I would sell it if ______.

If the first blank is “it has been going up”, you are in move 4’s territory — speculating, which he admits he was doing, and which is allowed as long as it is named. If the first blank mentions banks failing, the bank account is not the answer. The sentence does the choosing.
