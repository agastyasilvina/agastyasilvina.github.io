Not every answer at once — the wrong ones cancel.

Quantum computing from the ground up, in the order the ideas depend on each other. The chapter behind these notes has the right ingredients — superposition, measurement, interference, entanglement — but it circles through them twice, wraps each one in a new analogy, and repeats the most common mistake about the subject: that a quantum computer tries every answer at once. 1

A qubit is two amplitudes

Superposition, without the cat

In the chapterA classical bit is 0 or 1, like a light switch. A qubit can be 0, 1, or both at once until it’s measured. The chapter’s pictures are Schrödinger’s cat, alive and dead in a sealed box until someone looks, and a spinning top that can turn clockwise, anticlockwise “or any state in between”. Superposition, it says, lets a quantum computer process a massive amount of data simultaneously.

Start with the thing being replaced. A classical bit has two possible states and is always in exactly one of them. Everything your laptop does is long strings of these, pushed through logic gates.

A qubit also has two states you can read out, written |0⟩ and |1⟩ — the bracket is just a label meaning “the state called 0”. But between measurements a qubit isn’t described by which one it’s in. It’s described by two numbers called amplitudes, one attached to each outcome:

state = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩

That combination is superposition. Two rules come with it:

  • Amplitudes can be negative. In full generality they’re complex numbers, which adds a phase, but plain positive and negative numbers are enough to see every idea on this page.
  • Their squares add up to 1: α2 + β2 = 1. The next move explains why.

Because the squares add up to 1, you can draw a qubit as an arrow of length one: α is how far it points towards 0 (to the right), β how far towards 1 (upwards). |0⟩ points straight right, |1⟩ straight up, and the arrow halfway between, with α = β ≈ 0.707, is the equal superposition, called |+⟩. Every lab on this page uses this picture.

What a qubit is not

  • Not a number between 0 and 1. A dimmer at 50% is one definite setting. A qubit in |+⟩ reads 0 or 1, never 0.5. The chapter’s top that spins “in between” invites exactly this mistake.
  • Not secretly 0 or 1, with you just not knowing which. That’s a coin under a cup, and a coin under a cup can’t do what move 4 shows a qubit doing.
  • Not “both at once” in any way you can use directly. You can never read both. Move 6 is about what that costs.

Watch forSchrödinger’s cat was a complaint, not an explanation. Schrödinger dreamed it up in 1935 to show how absurd it seemed to apply superposition to something the size of a cat. It works as a picture of a superposition as long as you remember its author was objecting. And a real cat never gets into one: it’s constantly bumping into air and light, which destroys superpositions almost instantly (move 14).

Drill — which are real qubit states?

Check the squares. (a) α = 0.6, β = 0.8. (b) α = 0.5, β = 0.5. (c) α = 0.707, β = −0.707. (d) α = 0, β = −1.

Answers

(a) Yes: 0.36 + 0.64 = 1. (b) No: 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5, so the chances would only add up to a half. (c) Yes: that’s |−⟩, the equal superposition with a minus sign on the 1 part. (d) Yes: it’s |1⟩ with a minus sign in front, which move 3 shows is physically the same as |1⟩.

Measurement gives one bit

The Born rule, and what “collapse” means in practice

In the chapterMeasuring makes the qubit “collapse” to 0 or 1, and nobody can predict which. The Born rule, named after Max Born, says the probability of an outcome is the square of the wave function’s size there — “it’s a power of two”. Measurements work through observables, which the chapter pictures as the tools of a Swiss Army knife, one per property; eigenstates and eigenvalues are the states and readings that belong to a measurement, like the clear notes of a plucked guitar string. An electron’s spin might be 70% likely to be up and 30% down.

The Born rule is the bridge from amplitudes to anything you can see: the chance of an outcome is its amplitude squared. Max Born proposed it in 1926, and it’s the reason the squares have to add up to 1 — they’re probabilities, and something has to happen.

50%α = 0.707 → chance of 0
36 / 64α = 0.6, β = 0.8
36%α = −0.6: the sign squares away
70%β = 0.837 → chance of 1

Three things happen when you measure, and all three shape how quantum computers are used:

  1. You get one bit. A 0 or a 1, at random, with the Born-rule chances. Never the amplitudes themselves.
  2. The state becomes what you saw. Read 1 and the qubit is now |1⟩; read it again and you get 1 again, every time. The amplitudes it had before are gone. In practice that’s all “collapse” means.
  3. One run tells you almost nothing. To learn that a state is 70/30 you prepare it again and again and count, so quantum programs are run thousands of times — each run is called a shot. You can’t shortcut this by copying the qubit first: an unknown quantum state can’t be copied (the no-cloning theorem, 1982).

Physicists still argue about what collapse is; the rival interpretations of quantum mechanics are rival answers. Nobody argues about the rule, and the rule is all a quantum computer needs.

The vocabulary: observables and eigen-things

The chapter spends two sections on this. For a qubit it fits in three lines:

  • An observable is a question you can put to the system: “is it 0 or 1?”, or “which way does this electron’s spin point along the magnet?”
  • Its eigenstates are the states that answer it with certainty. For “0 or 1?”, those are |0⟩ and |1⟩.
  • Its eigenvalues are the possible answers — the numbers on the dial.

Eigen is German for “own” or “characteristic”: each question has its own set of states. Every other state is a combination of them and gets a random answer, with Born-rule chances. The chapter’s guitar string is a good analogy: the string’s pure notes are its eigenstates, their pitches are the eigenvalues, and a real pluck sounds several at once.

Watch forNobody has to be watching. The chapter says quantum things stay in superposition until “a conscious observer” measures them. No consciousness is needed: any interaction that leaves a record of the outcome anywhere — in a detector, a stray photon, a jostled atom — does it. That’s exactly why qubits are so hard to protect (move 14).

Two small slips in the spin example. The outcomes up and down aren’t “the observables”: the observable is the spin measurement, up and down are its eigenstates, and the two readings are its eigenvalues. And a superposed electron isn’t 70% likely to be up; it’s 70% likely to be found up. Move 8 explains why physicists don’t think it was secretly up all along.

Drill — Born-rule practice

Give the chance of reading 1. (a) α = 0.8, β = 0.6. (b) α = 0.6, β = −0.8. (c) α = β = 0.707. Then (d): what amplitudes read 1 nine times out of ten? Check (d) with the arrow.

Answers

(a) 36%. (b) 64% — the minus sign makes no difference to a measurement made now. (c) 50%. (d) β2 = 0.9, so β ≈ 0.949 and α ≈ 0.316, either of which can be negative: an arrow about 72° up from the 0 direction.

Gates move the arrow

Operators and gates, made concrete

In the chapterQuantum operators are like recalibrating a compass: they transform one quantum state into another. Quantum gates are like routes through the terrain: a qubit passing through one undergoes a distinct change, and scientists guide qubits through sequences of gates towards a desired state or an algorithm’s answer.

A gate is an operation that changes the amplitudes; on the circle, it moves the arrow. Gates obey one strict rule: the arrow must keep length one, because the chances must still add up to 1. So every gate is a rotation or a reflection, and every gate can be undone. (The textbook word is unitary; the chapter’s “operators” are the maths that describes them.)

Three single-qubit gates cover everything on this page:

GateWhat it doesOn the circle
XSwaps the amplitudes: α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ becomes β|0⟩ + α|1⟩. The quantum NOT.Reflect in the 45° line
ZFlips the sign of the 1 amplitude: β becomes −β.Reflect in the horizontal axis
HMixes them: the new α is (α + β) ÷ √2, the new β is (α − β) ÷ √2. Named after Hadamard.Reflect in the 22.5° line

H is the workhorse. From |0⟩ it makes the equal superposition |+⟩; from |1⟩ it makes |−⟩, the same superposition with a minus sign on the 1 part.

Z teaches the subtlest lesson on the page. Apply it to |1⟩ and you get −|1⟩, which reads 1 every time, exactly like |1⟩: a minus sign on the whole state changes nothing you could ever measure. Apply it to |+⟩ and you get |−⟩. Measured straight away that’s still 50/50, but it’s a genuinely different state, because the minus sign sits between the parts. A relative sign is invisible to a measurement made now and decisive for what the next gate does. Move 4 is about exactly that.

A quantum program — a circuit — always has the same shape: start every qubit at |0⟩, apply a sequence of gates, measure, and repeat the whole run many times to collect the statistics.

Interference is the engine

The idea the chapter mentions once and should have led with

In the chapterWave–particle duality: fired at two slits, electrons build up an interference pattern like waves, yet each one lands as a single dot like a particle. Quantum algorithms use interference to amplify correct solutions and diminish wrong ones, and gates cause constructive or destructive interference, like waves merging or diverging in an ocean. The chapter also credits duality with letting a qubit be both 0 and 1 until it’s measured.

Set experiment 2 beside a coin. Flip a fair coin: 50/50. Flip it again without looking: still 50/50. Randomness added to randomness stays random. Now the qubit: H makes a 50/50 state — and a second H, which on its own also makes 50/50 states, brings the qubit back to 0 with certainty. Two randomising steps, and the randomness vanished.

Probabilities can’t do that. They’re never negative, so they can only pile up. Amplitudes can, because they can be negative, so they can cancel. Follow the amplitudes from |0⟩ through H and H as routes the qubit could take:

RouteFirst HSecond HAmplitude along the route
0 → 0 → 0+0.707+0.707+0.5
0 → 1 → 0+0.707+0.707+0.5
Total for 01, so a 100% chance
0 → 0 → 1+0.707+0.707+0.5
0 → 1 → 1+0.707−0.707−0.5
Total for 10, so no chance at all

The −0.707 is H’s single minus sign, on the step from 1 to 1. Multiply along each route, add up the routes that end at the same outcome, and only then square. The two routes to 1 are equal and opposite and cancel — destructive interference — while the two routes to 0 reinforce. That’s the whole trick. It’s the only reason a quantum computer could ever beat a classical one at anything.

The other experiments now make sense:

  • H, Z, H reads 1 with certainty. Z put a minus sign on the middle 1, so now it’s the routes to 0 that cancel.
  • H, measure, H is back to 50/50. The measurement in the middle forced the qubit onto one route, so there were no longer two routes to interfere.

The double slit is the same story in a laboratory. Fire electrons one at a time at two slits and each lands as a single dot, but thousands of dots build up bands: places where the two routes, through the left slit and through the right, cancel or reinforce. Put a detector at the slits to learn which way each electron went and the bands disappear. That’s experiment 4, done with electrons.

So wave–particle duality, as far as computing is concerned, comes down to this: the amplitudes behave like waves — they add and they cancel — and the outcomes behave like particles, one definite click per run. A quantum algorithm is a way of arranging the waves so that when the click comes, it’s probably the right one.

Watch forSuperposition doesn’t come from duality, as the chapter has it. Superposition is the basic rule — any two states can be combined. Duality is the name for what you see when a superposition of routes meets a detector: wave-like interference in the statistics, particle-like single events.

Drill — predict, then check

Predict each result, then test it in the lab in move 3. (a) X, H. (b) X, H, H. (c) H, X, H.

Answers

(a) |−⟩: 50/50, with the minus on the 1 part. (b) Back to |1⟩: this time the routes to 0 cancel. (c) |0⟩ with certainty: X swaps two equal amplitudes, which changes nothing, so the second H simply undoes the first.

Uncertainty, qubit-sized

Heisenberg’s principle, on the circle

In the chapterHeisenberg, 1927: the more precisely you know a particle’s position, the less precisely you can know its momentum, and the reverse. It isn’t a shortcoming of our instruments but a property of nature. For quantum computing the chapter calls it a double-edged sword — qubits are like shifting sands that change when observed too closely — and credits the probabilistic nature that follows from it with letting quantum computers process vast amounts of information at once.

The chapter has the physics right and its role in computing wrong. Right: position and momentum can’t both be sharp, and not because instruments are clumsy — no state has both.

A qubit has its own, simpler version. There’s more than one question you can ask it. “Is it 0 or 1?” is one. “Is it + or −?” is another: you ask it by applying H and then measuring, because H turns |+⟩ into |0⟩ and |−⟩ into |1⟩. The two questions are complementary:

  • |0⟩ answers “0 or 1?” with certainty and “+ or −?” at random, 50/50.
  • |+⟩ answers “+ or −?” with certainty and “0 or 1?” at random.
  • No state answers both with certainty. On the circle it’s plain to see: an arrow can lie along an axis or along a diagonal, never both.

Certain for “0 or 1?”, a coin toss for “+ or −?”.

That’s the uncertainty principle in miniature, and it falls out of the same maths as everything else. But it isn’t what gives quantum computers their power — interference is — and the chapter’s claim that uncertainty lets them process vast amounts of information doesn’t follow. Where complementarity really earns its keep is security. In quantum key distribution (the BB84 scheme, from 1984), a key is sent one photon at a time, each prepared for one of two complementary questions chosen at random. An eavesdropper doesn’t know which question to ask, and asking the wrong one disturbs the photon, so the eavesdropping shows up as errors.

Watch forQuantum uncertainty isn’t ignorance. A coin under a cup is uncertain because you haven’t looked. A qubit in |0⟩ is completely known — you know its state exactly — and it still answers “+ or −?” at random.

Drill — trade one certainty for another

Turn the arrow to 0° and ask 100 copies each question. Then 45°. Then about 20°. Notice that whatever makes one answer more certain makes the other less certain.

Two to the n

Where the power comes from, and the myth about it

In the chapterA forest where every tree shows all four seasons until it’s photographed. If each tree is a qubit, two trees represent four states — 00, 01, 10, 11 — three trees eight, and every extra qubit doubles the possibilities, letting a quantum computer explore vast computational paths in a single command. A classical explorer checks one path at a time; a quantum explorer treads many at once.

The chapter gets the counting right. Two qubits have four possible readouts, 00, 01, 10 and 11, and their state holds an amplitude for each. Three qubits hold eight, and n qubits hold 2n:

1,024amplitudes for 10 qubits
1015for 50 qubits
1090for 300 qubits
1080atoms in the observable universe

That growth is real, and it’s why simulating a few dozen qubits strains a supercomputer: just storing the amplitudes for 50 qubits takes about 18 million gigabytes.

Here’s the catch the chapter skips: you only ever read n bits out. Measure 300 qubits and you get one string of 300 zeros and ones, chosen at random by the Born rule, and the amplitudes are gone. A theorem pins this down — Holevo’s, from 1973: n qubits can’t deliver more than n bits of readable information.

So try the “every path at once” plan. Put every qubit through H, so all 2n answers have equal amplitude. Run your calculation on all of them at once. Measure. You get one answer picked at random — no better than a guess, which an ordinary computer can make for free.

The real design problem is harder and more interesting: arrange the gates so that by the end, the amplitudes of the wrong answers cancel and the right answer’s pile up — move 4, on an enormous scale. That only works for problems with the right hidden structure, which is why quantum speed-ups exist for some problems and not for others.

A quantum computer doesn’t try every answer. It arranges for the wrong answers to cancel.

The sentence to keep

Watch forThe chapter’s comparison table calls classical computing “sequential” and quantum computing “parallel”. Classical computers are massively parallel already — a graphics card does thousands of operations at once. And quantum “parallelism” isn’t many calculations whose answers you collect; it’s one calculation on amplitudes, from which you collect a single sample.

Drill — say it plainly

Explain to a friend, in two sentences, why putting 300 qubits in superposition doesn’t let you read 2300 answers. If you can do it without the word “collapse”, you’ve got it.

Entanglement: correlation, not a message

What entangled qubits share, and what they can’t do

In the chapterWhen particles are entangled, one particle’s state “instantly influences” the other’s, however far apart they are. The chapter’s pictures: two tuning forks in distant rooms of a mansion, where striking one makes the other ring; a magical walkie-talkie that lets one team member’s discovery instantly inform the rest; two cities’ traffic systems adapting to each other with no visible communication. Entanglement lets quantum computers work on many qubits in a coordinated way.

You can make an entangled pair with two gates. Start with two qubits at |00⟩ and apply H to the first, which gives equal amplitudes on |00⟩ and |10⟩. Then apply CNOT, the controlled-NOT: a two-qubit gate that flips the second qubit only when the first is 1. The |10⟩ part becomes |11⟩, and you have a Bell pair:

(|00⟩ + |11⟩) ÷ √2

Measure both and you get 00 half the time and 11 half the time, never 01 or 10. Each qubit on its own is a perfect coin toss; together they always agree. And there’s no way to describe this state as “the first qubit is doing this, the second is doing that” — it can’t be split into two single-qubit states. That’s what entangled means: the pair has a state, and the parts don’t have states of their own.

Now the part the chapter gets wrong. Alice takes one qubit of a pair to Tokyo and Bob takes the other to Lisbon. Alice measures and reads 1, so she knows Bob will read 1. But she didn’t choose to read 1 — her result was a coin toss — so she can’t use it to say anything. Bob, looking only at his own results, sees a random string whether or not Alice has measured, whatever she did. The correlation appears only when they compare notes, over an ordinary channel, no faster than light. This is a theorem — the no-communication theorem — not a technical hurdle waiting for better equipment.

Each of the chapter’s analogies smuggles in the one thing entanglement can’t do. A struck tuning fork that makes a distant one ring is sending a signal. A walkie-talkie exists to send messages. A traffic system that adapts to a closure across town has been told about it. Entangled qubits share correlated randomness, and no more.

What entanglement does for computing is quieter and more important: it’s why n qubits need all 2n amplitudes. Without entanglement, each qubit could be described separately — n pairs of amplitudes, 2n numbers in all — and an ordinary computer could simulate the lot with ease. Entanglement is what makes the big space genuinely big.

Watch for“Spooky action at a distance” was an objection. Einstein wrote the phrase — spukhafte Fernwirkung — in a 1947 letter to Max Born, about something he thought a sensible theory shouldn’t contain. He was betting that each particle carries hidden instructions from the start. Move 8 is how that bet was settled.

Drill — be Bob

Press each button a few times and look only at Bob’s row. Could Bob tell which kind of pair he had? Now compare the two rows. That comparison is the only place the entanglement shows — and it needed Alice’s results to reach Bob the ordinary way.

Bell’s test

Why entanglement isn’t just matching socks

In the chapterIn 1935 Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen argued that measuring one particle can’t really affect a distant one, so hidden variables must fix the results in advance. In the 1960s John Bell devised a test — Bell’s inequality — of whether the correlations are genuinely quantum or explicable by hidden variables, and experiments side with quantum mechanics. The chapter adds quantum teleportation, which recreates one qubit’s state in a distant qubit, and superdense coding, which it says maximises the information transmitted.

Everything in move 7’s lab could be faked. Post two envelopes holding the same random bit to Tokyo and Lisbon, and you get random results that always agree. Bell himself called this Bertlmann’s socks, after a colleague who always wore odd ones: see that one sock is pink and you know the other isn’t, with nothing spooky involved. EPR’s hope was that entanglement is socks — hidden instructions, written at the start.

Bell’s insight, in 1964, was that the socks idea makes a testable prediction once each side can choose between different questions. The cleanest version is a game, the CHSH game, named after Clauser, Horne, Shimony and Holt, who proposed it in 1969:

  • Alice and Bob are separated and can’t communicate. Each is handed a random question — a bit, x for Alice and y for Bob — and each must answer with a bit.
  • They win if their answers agree, except when both questions are 1, when they must disagree.

With any plan agreed in advance — any hidden instructions, even shared random ones — the best possible is 75%. (One best plan: both always answer 0, which wins every round except the 1-and-1.) With a shared Bell pair, each measuring along an angle set by their question, they win about 85% of rounds: cos2 22.5° ≈ 0.854. No instructions written in advance can do that.

One caveat about that simulation: it’s a single program that sees both questions, so it can reach 85% by cheating. That’s exactly the point. To beat 75%, the two sides have to behave as if each could see the other’s question — and separated players holding entangled pairs manage it without any signal passing between them.

Experiments have run this test again and again: John Clauser’s in 1972, Alain Aspect’s in 1982, and in 2015 three teams — in Delft, in Vienna and at NIST in Boulder — closed the remaining loopholes. The results match quantum mechanics, not socks, and Clauser, Aspect and Anton Zeilinger shared the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics for the work. The conclusion: no theory in which each particle carries its own local instructions can reproduce what entangled particles do.

Two tricks, stated properly

  • Teleportation (1993) moves a qubit’s state onto a distant qubit. It uses up a shared Bell pair and needs two ordinary bits sent the normal way, so nothing travels faster than light, and the original is destroyed in the process — it has to be, since states can’t be copied. It moves quantum information across networks, not matter.
  • Superdense coding (1992) runs the other way: with a Bell pair shared beforehand, sending one qubit delivers two ordinary bits. A modest, exact gain — not efficiency “never before achieved”.

Watch forThe chapter calls Bell’s inequality “the litmus test for quantum computers” and credits non-locality with their power. Bell tests certify that entanglement is real, and some cryptography uses them that way. But quantum computers aren’t checked with Bell tests, and their power comes from interference across an enormous space of amplitudes (moves 4 and 6) — which entanglement makes possible, but which has nothing to do with distance.

Drill — check the 75%

Each player has only four fixed strategies: always 0, always 1, repeat the question, flip the question. That makes sixteen combinations. Pick three and score them against the four possible question pairs. You’ll find that none wins all four, and the best win three.

How speed is measured

Time complexity, and the haystack the chapter gets wrong

In the chapterTime complexity measures how an algorithm’s running time grows with the size of its input. On many problems classical computers slow down linearly — ten units of data in ten minutes, twenty in twenty — but some blow up exponentially, like searching a haystack, where every time the haystack doubles the search time can double too. A quantum computer is like a magical tool that probes half the haystack at once, then a quarter, and so on; for factoring it works in polynomial time where classical computers need exponential time.

Time complexity asks one question: as the input grows, how fast does the work grow? Three growth rates matter here:

  • Linear — double the input, double the work. Checking every straw in a haystack.
  • Polynomial — the work grows like n2 or n3. It climbs quickly but stays manageable; computer scientists count polynomial as efficient.
  • Exponential — add one more unit of input and the work doubles. Trying every combination of an n-switch lock.

So the chapter’s haystack is linear, not exponential: a haystack twice the size taking twice as long is the definition of linear. The difference is the whole story. At a billion steps a second:

10a microseconda microsecond
50an eighth of a millisecond13 days

30027 millisecondsa number of years 74 digits long

The universe is about 14 billion years old; the 2n column passes that at around n = 89.

The chapter’s magical tool, which probes half the haystack and then a quarter, isn’t a quantum computer either. It’s binary search, which ordinary computers already do — but only on sorted data, like a dictionary. On an unsorted haystack no computer, quantum or classical, can halve its way to the needle. What a quantum computer can do there is Grover’s algorithm: about √N looks instead of N. That’s move 10.

Watch forA speed-up claim needs two numbers: how the quantum algorithm scales, and how the best classical one does. For factoring the chapter says classical computers take exponential time; the best known classical method is actually sub-exponential — slower than any polynomial, faster than exponential. More importantly, nobody has proved a fast classical method can’t exist. Speed-ups are measured against the best algorithm we know, which is why some have vanished: in 2018 Ewin Tang, then an 18-year-old undergraduate, found a classical algorithm that matched a celebrated quantum speed-up for recommendation systems.

Drill — classify

Linear, polynomial, exponential, or better than linear? (a) Reading every page of a book. (b) Comparing every pair of students in a class. (c) Trying every subset of a shopping list to find the cheapest combination that meets a target. (d) Finding a word in a printed dictionary.

Answers

(a) Linear. (b) Polynomial: about n2 ÷ 2 pairs. (c) Exponential: 2n subsets. (d) Better than linear — logarithmic, because you halve the pages each time, which only works because the dictionary is sorted.

Grover: a square-root speed-up

What “amplifying the right answer” looks like

In the chapterAlgorithms like Grover’s and Shor’s manipulate qubits to amplify the probabilities of correct solutions, making them more likely to be observed — guided, the chapter says, by the Born rule.

Grover’s algorithm (Lov Grover, 1996) is the honest version of the haystack. You have N unsorted items and a way to recognise the one you want. Checking them one at a time takes about N/2 looks on average. Grover takes about 0.785 × √N rounds (that’s π/4 × √N).

How it works, in the picture from moves 1 to 4. Start with every item at equal amplitude. The state is an arrow pointing almost entirely at “wrong answers”, tilted only very slightly towards the right one. Each round has two steps: flip the sign of the right answer’s amplitude — a step called the oracle, which recognises the answer without revealing it — and then reflect every amplitude about their average. Two reflections make a rotation, so each round turns the arrow by the same small angle towards the right answer. The wrong answers’ amplitudes shrink, and the right one’s grows.

Try it — Grover rounds

Three things to notice in the dial:

  • Four items, one round, certainty. With N = 4 a single round lands exactly on the answer. Checking one at a time can take three looks.
  • Too many rounds overshoot. Keep going past the peak and the arrow rotates past the answer, and the chance falls again. You have to know when to stop — a very un-classical way for an algorithm to behave.
  • The gain is a square root. A million items: 785 rounds instead of about 500,000 looks. Huge — but not exponential, and it’s provably the best any quantum computer can do on an unstructured search (Bennett, Bernstein, Brassard and Vazirani, 1997).

Watch forA square-root speed-up is easily eaten by overheads. Each quantum step is far slower than a classical one, and error correction (move 14) slows it further, so for many practical searches a quadratic advantage may never pay off. Grover matters more as the cleanest example of a right answer grown by interference than as a way to search databases.

Drill — find the peak

Set 16 items. How many rounds give the best chance, and what is it? Then 64 items. Then work out, without the dial, roughly how many rounds a hundred million items would need.

Answers

16 items: 3 rounds, about 96%. 64 items: 6 rounds, about 99.7%. A hundred million: √N is 10,000, so about 7,850 rounds — against about 50 million looks one at a time.

Shor, and the locks on the internet

The algorithm that made governments pay attention

In the chapterShor’s algorithm can factorise large numbers more efficiently than the best-known classical algorithms, with profound implications for cryptography. For massive numbers, classical factoring could take years, decades or longer.

Multiplying two large prime numbers is easy. Getting them back from the product is, as far as anyone knows, enormously hard for an ordinary computer. That one-way street guards much of the internet: RSA encryption depends on it, and the elliptic-curve systems that have largely replaced RSA depend on a close cousin.

In 1994 Peter Shor showed that a quantum computer could factor in polynomial time. The engine, again, is interference. Factoring can be turned into finding the period of a sequence of numbers — how often it repeats — and a step called the quantum Fourier transform makes the amplitudes interfere so that only answers tied to the period survive. It’s the H-then-H cancellation from move 4, on a vast scale. A variant of the algorithm breaks the elliptic-curve systems too.

Then the reality check:

The 15 and 21 runs used shortcuts that relied on already knowing the answer. Nobody can break real encryption with a quantum computer yet. But anything encrypted today can be recorded and stored until someone can — “harvest now, decrypt later” — which is why the switch has already begun. In August 2024 the US standards body NIST published its first post-quantum standards (FIPS 203, 204 and 205), built on maths problems that no known quantum algorithm cracks, and major browsers and messaging apps have started using post-quantum encryption.

What quantum computers don’t break: symmetric ciphers like AES, which encrypt the data itself once a key has been agreed. Grover’s algorithm can at most square-root the work of guessing a key, so a 256-bit key keeps about 128 bits of strength. The fix is longer keys, not new maths.

Watch forPost-quantum cryptography and quantum key distribution are different things. Post-quantum cryptography is ordinary software on ordinary computers, using maths that quantum computers aren’t known to break — that’s what the world is adopting. Quantum key distribution (move 5) needs special hardware and dedicated links, and is used far more narrowly.

Drill — feel the asymmetry

Multiply 13 by 17 in your head. Now, without looking back, factor 221. The first takes a few seconds; the second is a hunt. RSA-2048 uses a number 617 digits long.

Different, not better

What quantum computers are for, and what they aren’t

In the chapter“Quantum doesn’t equate to ‘better’, it signifies ‘different’”: quantum computers excel in certain areas while classical machines remain irreplaceable in others. Among the promising areas the chapter names simulating quantum systems and optimisation, which it calls inherently probabilistic and so tailor-made for quantum machines.

That line is the best in the chapter, and the one to keep. Here’s an honest scorecard, as of 2025:

TaskQuantum advantageWhere it stands
Simulating molecules and materials — catalysts, batteries, drugspotentially exponentialthe strongest case; needs error-corrected machines
Factoring and discrete logarithms — breaking RSA and elliptic curvesexponential over the best known classical methodsneeds large error-corrected machines; the world is already switching (move 11)
Unstructured searchsquare root (Grover)real, but overheads may swallow it

Machine learningunprovenseveral claimed speed-ups later matched classically
Email, video, games, spreadsheets, chatbotsnoneordinary computers, for good

Nature isn’t classical, dammit, and if you want to make a simulation of nature, you’d better make it quantum mechanical.

Richard Feynman, 1981

Feynman’s point is why simulation heads the list. Working out how electrons arrange themselves in a molecule is a quantum problem, and its cost on a classical computer grows exponentially, for exactly the reason in move 6. A machine made of qubits sidesteps that by being the same kind of thing as what it simulates.

The realistic future isn’t a quantum laptop. It’s quantum processors as specialist co-processors, like graphics cards: an ordinary computer runs everything, and hands the quantum one the few subroutines it’s good at.

“Quantum supremacy”

In 2019 Google’s 53-qubit Sycamore chip performed a sampling task in about 200 seconds that Google estimated would take a supercomputer 10,000 years. IBM replied that a better classical method would take days, and later work narrowed the gap further. Demonstrations like this use tasks chosen to be hard for classical computers, not useful ones: they’re milestones of control, not applications.

Watch forOptimisation isn’t “inherently probabilistic” in a way that suits quantum machines. Randomness helps plenty of classical optimisation methods too, and no quantum method has yet shown a clear advantage over the best classical ones on real-world problems.

Drill — quantum-shaped or not?

Should a quantum computer help with each of these? (a) Designing a better catalyst for making fertiliser. (b) Sorting a spreadsheet. (c) Reading traffic that was encrypted with RSA in 2010. (d) Recommending films. (e) Designing a battery electrolyte.

Answers

(a) Yes — molecular simulation. (b) No. (c) Yes, once a large error-corrected machine exists; that’s the harvest-now worry. (d) Probably not: the famous quantum speed-up here was matched classically. (e) Yes — simulation again.

Energy levels and spin

What a qubit physically is

In the chapterEnergy is quantised: like a jukebox whose volume only goes up in whole steps, or the rungs of a ladder, an atom’s electrons can only hold certain energies, and quantum computers move qubits between these levels — in trapped-ion machines, tuned lasers make an electron jump from one level to another. Spin is an electron’s built-in angular momentum, not literal spinning, and comes in two values, up and down, as the Stern–Gerlach experiment showed. Superconducting qubits are tiny circuits, cooled until current flows without resistance, with quantised energy levels; labs cool them to near absolute zero and steer them with microwave pulses.

Nothing so far has depended on what a qubit is made of. Any physical system will do if it has two distinguishable states that can be put in superposition, and nature offers several.

  • Two energy levels. Anything bound — an atom, an ion, a small circuit — can only hold certain energies, like rungs on a ladder; that’s what “quantised” means, and it’s the chapter’s jukebox. Call the bottom rung 0 and the next one up 1. A pulse of light or microwaves tuned to exactly the gap between them drives the system from one to the other: a full pulse flips 0 to 1 (an X gate), and half a pulse leaves an equal superposition. The discreteness is what makes a clean 0 and 1 possible at all.
  • Spin. An electron behaves as if it carries a tiny built-in magnet, though nothing is literally spinning. Measure it along any direction and you only ever get one of two values, up or down. Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach saw this in 1922, when a beam of silver atoms passing through a magnet split into exactly two. A ready-made qubit.

Why so cold? A superconducting qubit’s energy gap is tiny — the thermal energy of about a quarter of a degree above absolute zero. At room temperature, heat would knock it between levels constantly. At about 0.01 K, more than a hundred times colder than deep space, it stays put. The chapter’s superconducting circuits have one more essential ingredient: a Josephson junction, a thin insulating barrier that makes the rungs unevenly spaced, so a pulse tuned to the bottom gap doesn’t accidentally push the circuit up to a third rung.

Watch forThe chapter says quantised energy levels let quantum computers perform some tasks exponentially faster. Quantisation is the hardware’s foundation — it’s what makes a clean qubit possible — but speed-ups come from interference (moves 4 and 6). And spin’s link to wave–particle duality is looser than the chapter suggests; what matters is simpler: it’s a natural two-outcome quantity.

Drill — why one colour?

Explain why an ion qubit needs a laser tuned to one exact colour, and what a laser of the wrong colour would do.

Answer

Light comes in packets whose energy is set by its colour. Only a packet matching the gap between the two levels can drive the jump; light of the wrong colour barely affects the ion. That’s quantisation doing the work.

Noise is the enemy

Decoherence, error correction, and the tunnelling machines

In the chapterQuantum tunnelling: like a hiker who could pass straight through a hill instead of climbing over it, a quantum system can pass through energy barriers, escaping local minima to find the lowest valley. This matters especially in quantum annealing, where the lowest valley is the optimal solution.

Move 2 said that any interaction that leaves a record counts as a measurement. That’s the central engineering problem. A qubit’s surroundings — stray heat, vibration, electrical noise, a neighbouring qubit — are constantly, accidentally measuring it. Each stray interaction leaks a little information about the qubit into the world, and its superposition fades into an ordinary random bit. This is decoherence, and it wrecks the interference that everything depends on. (The chapter leaves noise for its next chapter; it’s too central to leave out here.)

The numbers set the challenge. The best operations on today’s qubits go wrong roughly once in a thousand; Shor’s algorithm at real key sizes needs billions of operations. The answer is quantum error correction: spread one reliable “logical” qubit across many physical ones, and keep checking whether any of them have drifted — without looking at the data itself. The trick is to measure only relationships, like “do these two qubits agree?”, which reveals an error without revealing the value. It works only when the physical error rate is below a threshold, about 1% for the leading scheme, the surface code. Below it, adding more qubits makes the logical qubit better rather than worse.

In late 2024 Google’s Willow chip showed this convincingly for the first time: each step up in the size of the code roughly halved the logical error rate. The price is steep — hundreds to thousands of physical qubits for each logical one — which is why the estimates in move 11 run to a million qubits. Until then we’re in what John Preskill in 2018 named the NISQ era: noisy intermediate-scale quantum machines, too noisy for the famous algorithms, useful for experiments.

Tunnelling, and the annealers

The chapter’s tunnelling section describes a different kind of machine. A quantum annealer — D-Wave has sold them since 2011 — doesn’t run gates at all. It encodes an optimisation problem as an energy landscape and lets the system settle towards the lowest valley, with tunnelling through barriers as one hoped-for way of not getting stuck in a shallow dip. The effect is real; whether it beats good classical methods on practical problems is still argued over. Tunnelling isn’t exotic in ordinary computing either: flash memory stores bits by tunnelling electrons through an insulator, and it’s one of the things limiting how small ordinary transistors can get.

Watch forThe chapter presents tunnelling as a general source of quantum computing power. It matters for annealers, and it’s how a superconducting qubit’s Josephson junction works, but Shor’s and Grover’s algorithms don’t use it. Their power is interference.

Drill — the contradiction

In one breath: why must a quantum computer be isolated from its surroundings and controlled by them?

Answer

Gates and measurements need you to reach in with pulses of light or microwaves; every other kind of contact is an accidental measurement. Building a quantum computer is the art of managing that contradiction.
