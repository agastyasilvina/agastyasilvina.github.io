Want them to read? Read first.

At a parents’ meeting, a teacher explains, very politely, how to get children reading, and one parent answers back in her head, very casually. Two grammar points: だけ as “as much as”, in 好きなだけ, and する気を起こさせる, “to make someone want to do something”, which brings the causative with it: 読ませる, 遊ばせる, 聞かせる.

The dialogue

The whole scene, retold for review

The lesson’s dialogue, retold in my own wording so you can review it in one place: the same scene, words and grammar, but a school principal instead of a teacher, phones instead of games, and every line rephrased. The parent’s lines in brackets are thoughts, not speech. The lesson’s exact key lines are in section 3. Hide the English with the button at the top to test yourself.

Scene

A parents’ meeting at a primary school. The principal is giving a talk; one parent answers back, silently.

Principal

子こどもたちに本ほんを読よませたいなら、何なにより大切たいせつなのは、親御おやごさんご自身じしんが読書どくしょを楽たのしむことです。

If you want your children to read, the most important thing is for you, the parents, to enjoy reading yourselves.

Parent, thinking

（つまり、親おやがお手本てほんを見みせろってことね。）

(So what you’re saying is, parents should set an example.)

Principal

普段ふだんはスマホで好すきなだけ遊あそばせておきながら、テストの前まえだけ「本ほんを読よみなさい」と言いっても、子こどもはその気きになりません。

If you normally let them play on their phones as much as they like, then tell them to read a book only before a test, they won’t feel like it.

Parent, thinking

（うちは夫婦ふうふそろってスマホばかり見みてるし……最悪さいあくだわ。）

(My husband and I are both on our phones all the time… that’s the worst.)

Principal

また、親御おやごさんが読よんだ本ほんの感想かんそうを、ぜひお子こさんに話はなして聞きかせてあげてください。

And please do tell your children what you thought of the books you’ve read.

Principal

本ほんの話はなしが自然しぜんにできる家か庭てい環かん境きょうがあれば、子こどもにも読書どくしょする気きを起おこさせることができるんです。

A home where books come up naturally in conversation can make children want to read, too.

Parent, thinking

（感想かんそうねえ……最後さいごに本ほんを読よんだの、いつだったかしら。）

(What I thought of a book, hm… when did I last even read one?)

2

The scene

Natsuko, Naomi and Peter, before and after the dialogue

Peter opens with the lesson’s title, “Making the Rugrats Read”, and asks how you’d say “rugrats” in Japanese. Neither Natsuko nor Naomi has heard the word; Naomi guesses “mice?”. No, Peter says, it means kids: not so different from mice, are they? (“Rug rat” is informal English for a toddler, and Rugrats was a long-running American cartoon about babies, from 1991 to 2004.) It doesn’t sound like a very refined word, Natsuko says (あんまり品のいい言葉じゃなさそうですね), but it’s clearly a lesson about children.

The grammar points are だけ and する気を起こさせる. The setting, Natsuko explains, is a 保護者会, a parents’ meeting. A teacher is giving a talk to the parents in polite Japanese, while one parent mutters to herself in casual Japanese. When you talk to yourself, Peter points out, you use casual Japanese.

Beyond the lessonThe parent’s asides, in the lesson and in my retelling, make a small course in casual inner speech:

  • 〜ってことね = 〜ということね: “so what you mean is…”, summing up what someone has just said.
  • 見せろ, なれ — the plain command form. Out loud it’s blunt enough to be rude; inside your head, or when you sum up a rule, it’s natural.
  • 〜なんて — “the fact that…”, with dismay or surprise, as in the lesson’s 親が二人ともゲーム好きなんて.
  • だわ and 〜かしら — endings traditionally labelled feminine: だわ softly asserts, and かしら is “I wonder”, the counterpart of かな. Today they live mostly in fiction and among older women; the linguist Kinsui Satoshi counts this kind of speech as “role language”, which tells you who a character is. Plain わ is another matter: one study of recorded everyday conversation found most speakers using it, men included.

After the dialogue

What do the hosts make of it? Naomi didn’t read much as a child, but reads a lot now. Natsuko may have read more as a child, and still likes reading, but now reading has become work as well. So it’s no longer fun, Peter suggests; there are still some fun things to read, Natsuko says.

3

Key lines

The teacher’s two lines that carry the grammar

The lesson’s dialogue is only five lines long, so just these two are quoted exactly; section 1 covers the rest in my retelling. The English is my own rendering.

普段ふだん、好すきなだけゲームで遊あそばせておいて、急きゅうに「本ほんを読よみなさい」と言いっても無理むりです。

If you normally let them play games as much as they like, it’s no use suddenly telling them to read a book. — the teacher

Grammar: 好きなだけ, “as much as they like” (section 5); 遊ばせておいて: the causative 遊ばせる, “let them play”, plus 〜ておく, “and leave them to it” (section 7); 〜と言っても無理です, “even if you say…, it won’t work”; 読みなさい, the command a parent gives a child.

そういう家か庭てい環かん境きょうが、子こどもに読書どくしょする気きを起おこさせることになるんです。

That kind of home environment is what ends up making children want to read. — the teacher

Grammar: 読書する気, “the will to read” (section 6); 子どもに…起こさせる, the causative, with the person made to act marked by に; 〜ことになる, “it comes about that, it results in”; 〜んです, explaining.

4

Vocabulary

The lesson’s word list, in its order

The hosts’ notes first, then a card for each word, plus a few more from the dialogue, with an example of my own.

  • 親御さん — parents, very politely. 親 means parent or parents, and both ご and さん add politeness. 親 alone can be your own; 親御さん is always someone else’s, which is why the teacher uses it to the parents. The lesson’s example: 親御さんによろしく言って下さい, “say hi to your parents.”
  • 手本 — a model to imitate, often お手本: an example presented as the right answer. The lesson’s example: 手本をよく見て漢字を練習して下さい, “look carefully at the model and practise your kanji.”
  • 保護者 — guardian, parent: 保護 is to protect or guard, and 者 a person. It doesn’t have to be a parent; an uncle or aunt, or a grandparent, can be a child’s 保護者. The lesson’s example: 私が、Kenの保護者です, “I am Ken’s guardian.”
  • 普段 — usually, ordinarily. It’s like いつも, Naomi says, but feels less frequent: more “day to day”. The lesson’s example: ふだん日本語を話しますか？, “do you usually speak Japanese?”
  • 感想 — impressions, thoughts: 映画の感想を教えて下さい, “tell me what you thought of the film.” A 感想文, writing up what you thought of a book or an experience, was a homework staple when they were at school, Natsuko says. Naomi hated it.
  • 家庭環境 — home environment, family background: 家庭環境がいい, 家庭環境が悪い.
  • 起こす — to raise, to cause; a transitive verb. Its intransitive partner is 起きる, to wake up or to happen. The lesson’s examples: 私は交通事故を起こした, “I caused a traffic accident”, and 交通事故が起きた, “a traffic accident happened.”

Beyond the lessonThe older word for parents at school was 父兄, literally “fathers and elder brothers”. After the war, school law switched to 保護者, defined as whoever holds parental authority, or a guardian if no one does, but 父兄 lingered in everyday speech. Style guides now say not to use it: it centres men, and leaves out everyone else who looks after a child.

親御おやごさんnoun

parents (someone else’s), said with respect

親御おやごさんは、お元気げんきですか。

Are your parents well?

お手本てほんnoun

a model to copy, first one of handwriting or painting to practise from — お手本になる, “to be a role model”

姉あねは何なんでもできて、私わたしのお手本てほんだった。

My older sister could do anything; she was my role model.

保護者ほごしゃnoun

parent or guardian, the word on school forms — 保護者会, “parents’ meeting”

遠足えんそくの前まえに、保護者ほごしゃの同意書どういしょを出だしてください。

Please hand in a parent’s or guardian’s consent form before the school trip.

普段ふだんnoun · adverb

usually, ordinarily, day to day — 普段着, “everyday clothes”

普段ふだんは自転車じてんしゃで通勤つうきんしているが、雨あめの日ひはバスに乗のる。

I usually cycle to work, but on rainy days I take the bus.

感想かんそうnoun

impressions, thoughts on something — 感想文, a written response

旅行りょこうの感想かんそうを、ブログに書かいた。

I wrote up my impressions of the trip on my blog.

家か庭てい環かん境きょうnoun

home environment, family background

家か庭てい環かん境きょうは、子こどもの成せい長ちょうに大おおきく影えい響きょうする。

A child’s home environment has a big influence on how they grow up.

起おこすう-verb

to wake someone; to raise, cause, start — 事故を起こす; its intransitive partner is 起きる

明日あしたの朝あさ、6時じに起おこしてくれる？

Can you wake me at six tomorrow morning?

読書どくしょnoun · する-verb

reading books — 読書感想文, a book report

寝ねる前まえの読書どくしょが、一日いちにちの楽たのしみだ。

Reading before bed is the best part of my day.

急きゅうにadverb

suddenly, all of a sudden

急きゅうに雨あめが降ふり出だした。

It suddenly started to rain.

無理むりな-adjective · noun

impossible, unreasonable — 無理しないで, “don’t push yourself”

この量りょうを一人ひとりで食たべるのは無理むりだよ。

There’s no way I can eat this much on my own.

最悪さいあくな-adjective · noun

the worst — 最悪の場合, “at worst”

財布さいふは忘わすれるし、雨あめにも降ふられるし、今日きょうは最悪さいあくだ。

I forgot my wallet and got caught in the rain: today’s the worst.

大切たいせつな-adjective

important, precious — 大切にする, “to take good care of”

祖母そぼにもらった時計とけいを、今いまも大切たいせつにしている。

I still take good care of the watch my grandmother gave me.

5

好きなだけ

Grammar point 1: だけ as “as much as”

You know だけ as “only”. Here it sets a limit instead, Peter explains: “as much as”. 好きなだけ is “as much as you like”. The lesson’s example: 私のおごりです。好きなだけ飲んで下さい, “It’s on me. Drink as much as you like.” It’s a phrase you hear a lot, Natsuko says. Instead of 好きな, Naomi adds, you can use the adjective ほしい: ほしいだけ取って下さい, “take as much as you want.”

Beyond the lessonThe pattern is “X + だけ”, where X sets the limit: a wish, an ability, a need. Often the same verb comes back after it: 食べたいだけ食べる, “eat as much as you want to eat”.

PhraseMeaning
好すきなだけas much as you like
ほしいだけas much as you want
〜たいだけas much as you want to: 寝たいだけ寝る
できるだけas much as possible, as … as you can
必要ひつようなだけas much as you need

食たべ放題ほうだいだから、食たべたいだけ食たべていいよ。

It’s all-you-can-eat, so eat as much as you want.

休やすみの日ひは、寝ねたいだけ寝ねる。

On my days off, I sleep as long as I like.

できるだけ早はやく返事へんじをください。

Please reply as soon as you can.

彼かれは言いいたいだけ言いって、帰かえってしまった。

He said everything he wanted to say, and then just left.

Watch forDon’t lose the everyday だけ, “only”. My retelling has both in one sentence: 好きなだけ遊ばせて, “let them play as much as they like”, then テストの前だけ, “only before a test”. With a negative, “only” is しか〜ない instead: テストの前しか読まない, “they read only before tests”.

6

する気を起こさせる

Grammar point 2: making someone want to do something

Take it apart: する気 is the will, the motivation, to do something, and 起こさせる is the causative of 起こす, “to raise, to cause”. So, literally, “to make someone raise the will to do something”: to get them motivated, to make them want to. The lesson’s example: このプログラムは、子供に勉強する気を起こさせます, “this programme makes kids want to study.” Both of today’s points are quite advanced, Peter warns.

Beyond the lesson気, “spirit, mind, feeling”, makes dozens of everyday phrases. The ones closest to する気:

  • やる気 — motivation, drive: やる気が出る, “to get motivated”; やる気がない, “to have no drive”.
  • 〜する気になる — to come to feel like doing something. その気になる is “to be won over, to get in the mood”, as in my retelling: 子どもはその気になりません.
  • 〜する気がない — to have no intention of doing something.
  • 〜する気か — “do you really mean to…?”, as in Upper Intermediate S4 #2: そんな情けない理由でやめる気か.

今日きょうは寒さむくて、出でかける気きにならない。

It’s so cold today, I don’t feel like going out.

彼かれには、謝あやまる気きなどまったくないようだ。

He doesn’t seem to have the slightest intention of apologising.

友達ともだちがあまり楽たのしそうに話はなすので、私わたしも始はじめる気きになった。

My friend made it sound like so much fun that I felt like starting too.

Make or let

Beyond the lesson: the causative, all through the dialogue

Beyond the lessonThe lesson’s second point is one causative, but the dialogue is full of them: 読ませる, to get children to read; 遊ばせておく, to let them play and leave them to it; 聞かせる, to let someone hear, that is, to tell them; and 起こさせる. One form covers both “make” and “let”, and context decides which.

VerbCausative
読よむ読よませる
遊あそぶ遊あそばせる
起おこす起おこさせる
食たべる食たべさせる
するさせる
来くる来こさせる

For う-verbs, change the final u sound to a and add せる: 読む → 読ま + せる. For る-verbs, drop the る and add させる. The result is a る-verb itself: 読ませて, 読ませた.

  • Who acts: when the verb already has an object, the person you make or let act takes に: 子どもに本を読ませる. When it has none, the person can take を or に: 子どもを遊ばせる. を leans towards making, に towards letting.
  • 〜させてください — “let me…”, the polite way to ask or offer to do something yourself.
  • 〜て聞かせる — to tell, read or explain something to someone. 読み聞かせ, reading aloud to children, is an everyday word in Japanese homes, nurseries and schools.

母ははは毎晩まいばん、私わたしに野菜やさいを食たべさせた。

Every night, my mother made me eat my vegetables.

娘むすめには、好すきな習ならい事ごとをさせている。

We let our daughter take whatever lessons she likes.

今日きょうは私わたしに払はらわせてください。

Please, let me pay today.

寝ねる前まえに、子こどもに絵本えほんを読よんで聞きかせる。

Before bed, I read my child a picture book.

8

Reading in Japan

Beyond the lesson: book reports, morning reading, and how much people really read

Beyond the lessonThe dreaded 感想文. Naomi’s least favourite homework has a national stage. The Young Readers’ Book Report Contest, 青少年読書感想文全国コンクール, run by the School Library Association (全国学校図書館協議会) and the Mainichi newspaper since 1955, received just over two million essays from about 23,500 schools in 2025. Pupils write about one of the year’s set books (課題図書) or a book of their own choosing (自由読書), and many schools make it summer-holiday homework: in a 2018 survey of mothers, 41% of first-years had it, rising to 73% by the sixth year.

Ten minutes before class. 朝の読書, “morning reading”, began in April 1988, when two teachers at a girls’ high school in Funabashi, 林公 and 大塚笑子, set aside time before lessons. Its four rules: everyone does it, every day, any book you like, and you just read (みんなでやる・毎日やる・好きな本でよい・ただ読むだけ). In May 2026, 25,291 schools were doing it.

From the very start. Bookstart, which began in Britain in 1992, came to Japan in 2001 as ブックスタート: at a baby’s health check-up, the family is given picture books and a first experience of sharing one. By September 2026, 1,127 of Japan’s 1,741 municipalities were taking part.

So how much do people actually read? The School Library Association’s survey of May 2025:

PupilsBooks a monthRead none in May
Primary12.19.6%
Junior high3.924.2%
High school1.455.7%

Adults read less still. In the Agency for Cultural Affairs’ language survey published in 2024, 62.6% said they read no books at all in a month, where earlier surveys had found figures in the high 40s. (The survey switched to postal questionnaires in 2020, so the agency treats older figures only as a rough reference.)

And the dialogue’s games have a real-life sequel. Kagawa Prefecture’s 2020 ordinance on internet and game addiction asks parents to treat 60 minutes of computer games a day as a guide, 90 on school holidays, and to have children off their smartphones by 9 p.m., or 10 p.m. for older teenagers. There’s no penalty: it only asks families to try.

Quiz

Pick the word that fits. Each answer comes with the reason.

1. 遠慮えんりょしないで、好すきな＿＿＿食たべてね。

Don’t hold back: eat as much as you like.

好きなだけ: as much as you like. しか needs a negative after it: 少ししか食べない.

2. できる＿＿＿早はやく来きてください。

Please come as early as you can.

できるだけ: as much as possible, as … as you can.

3. 子こどもに野菜やさいを＿＿＿のは大変たいへんだ。

Getting children to eat vegetables is hard work.

The causative 食べさせる: make or get someone to eat. 食べられる is the potential or passive; 食べたがる means “to want to eat”.

4. 先生せんせいは学生がくせいに作文さくぶんを＿＿＿。

The teacher had the students write an essay.

書く → 書かせる: made them write. 書けた means “could write”; 書かれた, “was written”.

5. その仕事しごと、ぜひ私わたしに＿＿＿ください。

Please let me do that job.

〜させてください: “let me…”. やってください would ask them to do it.

6. 先生せんせいにほめられて、やる＿＿＿が出でてきた。

Getting praised by my teacher fired me up.

やる気: motivation, drive. やる気が出る, “to get motivated”.

7. 車くるまの事故じこを＿＿＿しまった。

I caused a car accident.

Transitive 起こす: you cause it. 事故が起きた uses the intransitive: an accident happened.

8. （書類しょるいで）＿＿＿氏名しめい

On a form: “name of parent or guardian”.

Forms say 保護者氏名. 親御さん is a polite spoken word for someone else’s parents.

9. 映画えいがの＿＿＿を聞きかせてください。

Tell me what you thought of the film.

感想: impressions, thoughts. And 聞かせてください, “let me hear”, is the causative again.

10. ＿＿＿はコンタクトだけど、家いえではめがねをかけている。

I usually wear contacts, but at home I wear glasses.

普段: usually, day to day.
