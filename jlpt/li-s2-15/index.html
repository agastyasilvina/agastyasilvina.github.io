The dialogue

The whole scene, retold for review

The lesson’s dialogue, retold in my own wording so you can review it in one place. It keeps the same scene, characters, words and grammar, and carries on from my retelling of lesson 14. Every line is rephrased, and the last line is new: なら after a noun, 急行なら. The lesson’s exact key lines are in section 3. Hide the English with the button at the top to test yourself.

Scene

Ikebukuro Station, on the Tōbu Tōjō Line platform.

Yasumi

あの、すみません。この電車でんしゃ、小川町おがわまちまで行いきますか？

Um, excuse me. Does this train go as far as Ogawamachi?

Passenger

ええ、行いきますよ。でも、これは各駅停車かくえきていしゃですからね。小川町おがわまちまで行いくなら、急きゅう行こうに乗のったほうが早はやく着つきますよ。

Yes, it does. But this one’s a local, you see. If you’re going all the way to Ogawamachi, you’ll get there sooner on an express.

Yasumi

実じつは川越かわごえで降おりたいんですけど、急きゅう行こうは川越かわごえにも止とまりますか？

Actually, I want to get off at Kawagoe. Does the express stop there too?

Passenger

たしか止とまると思おもいますよ。……あ、ほら、向むかいのホームに来きたのが急きゅう行こうです！

I’m fairly sure it does… Oh, look, the train coming in across the platform is the express!

Yasumi

本当ほんとうだ！ご親切しんせつに、ありがとうございます！

So it is! That’s very kind of you, thank you!

Passenger

いえいえ。お気きをつけて。

Not at all. Take care.

Scene

Yasumi’s phone rings.

Yasumi

はい、もしもし？

Yes, hello?

Hajime

あ、ヤスミ？今いまどのへん？

Oh, Yasumi? Whereabouts are you?

Yasumi

まだ池いけ袋ぶくろ。これから東とう上じょう線せんの急きゅう行こうに乗のるところ。

Still at Ikebukuro. I’m just about to get on the Tōjō Line express.

Hajime

早はやくおいでよ！ちょうど彼女かのじょがピザを焼やき上あげたところでさ、超ちょういいにおいなんだ。

Hurry up and get here! My girlfriend’s just taken a pizza out of the oven, and it smells amazing.

Yasumi

急きゅう行こうなら三さん十じゅっ分ぷんぐらいだから、冷さめないうちに着つけるかも！

On the express it’s only about thirty minutes, so I might make it before it goes cold!

2

The scene

Naomi, Yuichi and Peter, before and after the dialogue

Part two of “Party in the ’Burbs”. Hajime and Yasumi were friends, Yuichi begins. Were? Naomi pounces on the past tense, and Yuichi corrects it: they’re still friends. Hajime lives in Kawagoe, and today Yasumi is on the way there to hang out (遊びに行こうとしています). Yasumi’s probably at the station, Naomi guesses. The conversation is between Yasumi and a passenger (乗客), in polite Japanese, and then Yasumi talks to Hajime on the phone, so, Peter says, listen for the change in politeness.

After the dialogue

この電車は〜に行きますか？, “does this train go to…?”, is a very handy phrase, Naomi says. Not as it stands, Peter points out: you have to change the station. この電車は赤坂へ行きますか？, Naomi tries; この電車は、川越に行きますか, Yuichi. Naomi gets asked it on trains all the time, Yuichi often by elderly people (お年寄り), and foreigners ask it too; Peter asks it a lot. Everyone uses it, Yuichi concludes. Sometimes people ask Peter, and answering feels a bit odd (微妙). Japanese people asking Peter? They must have the wrong person, Naomi suggests. Not at all, Peter says: lots of people don’t know Tokyo, people up from the regions (地方) looking around, and Peter will ask them 大丈夫ですか？, “are you all right?” How kind, Naomi says (やさしい！), and Peter agrees (でしょ？); people are a little surprised that a foreigner is helping them. The other day, five women from Osaka (大阪のおばちゃん, Naomi supplies) asked Peter the way to Tokyo Tower, and Peter offered to call them a taxi: お願いします, they said. You called them a taxi? すごいね、えらいね, Naomi marvels. まーね, says Peter.

Phrases worth taking from the chat:

  • 遊びに行こうとしています — “is on the way over to hang out”: 〜ようとする, “to be about to, to set out to”.
  • お年寄りによく聞かれます — “elderly people often ask me”: the passive 聞かれる.
  • 微妙な感じがします — “it feels a bit odd”: 微妙, delicate, iffy, hard to call.
  • 東京タワーはどちらですか？ — “which way is Tokyo Tower?”: どちら, the polite “which way, where”.
  • タクシーお呼びしますか？ — “shall I call you a taxi?”: the humble お〜します.
  • すごいね、えらいね — “wow, good for you!”: えらい, admirable.

Key lines

The stranger’s advice, and the phone call

The lesson’s dialogue is ten lines long, so these three are quoted exactly; section 1 covers the rest in my retelling. The English is my own rendering.

はい、ですが各駅停車かくえきていしゃですよ。小川町おがわまちに行いくなら、急きゅう行こう電車でんしゃの方ほうが速はやいですよ。

Yes, but it’s a local. If you’re going to Ogawamachi, the express is faster. — the passenger

Grammar: ですが, a polite “but”; 行くなら, “if you’re going”, built on what Yasumi has just said (section 5); 急行電車の方が速い, “the express is faster”, 〜の方が for comparing.

いま、池いけ袋ぶくろ駅えき。東武東上線とうぶとうじょうせんに乗のるところ。

I’m at Ikebukuro Station. Just about to get on the Tōbu Tōjō Line. — Yasumi

Grammar: casual, with no です and no particle after いま; 乗るところ, “about to get on” (section 6).

早はやく来きなよ！彼女かのじょがピザを焼やいたところなんだ。超ちょううまそうだよ。

Hurry up and get over here! My girlfriend has just baked a pizza. It looks seriously good. — Hajime

Grammar: 来なよ, a casual command (section 7); 彼女, “my girlfriend”; 焼いたところ, “has just baked” (section 6), with なんだ explaining; 超うまそう, “looks seriously tasty”: うまい + そう.

Vocabulary

The lesson’s word list, in its order

The hosts’ notes first, then a card for each word, plus more from the dialogue and the chat, each with an example of my own.

  • かくえきていしゃ (各駅停車) — a local train. 各 is each, 駅 station, 停 is 止まる, to stop, and 車 a car: the train that stops at each station. 各駅 for short, Yuichi says, or 各停, Naomi adds. Yuichi’s example: 各駅に停車します, “it stops at every station.”
  • きゅうこう (急行) — an express. There are lots of kinds of train, Naomi says, so which is fastest? 特急, says Yuichi. Isn’t it the Shinkansen, Peter asks, and claims the win for the foreigner. But the Shinkansen is a 特別急行 too, Naomi points out, since you need a 特急券: 特別 is special or super, 急行 express, so 特別急行 is a special express, 特急 for short. Next comes 急行, then 快速, rapid; a little slower than 快速 is 準急, semi-express; and then the 各駅停車. The rules differ from company to company, Naomi says, but that’s the basic order. ややこしいですね, says Yuichi, with lesson 14’s word; just knowing the names is enough, Naomi concludes. Yuichi’s example sounds like an announcement: まもなく一番線に急行電車がまいります, “the express will shortly arrive at platform 1.” (Lesson 14’s section 7 has more on train types.)
  • やく (焼く) — to bake, to grill: ケーキを焼く, to bake a cake; ピザを焼く, a pizza; ステーキを焼く, to grill a steak. There’s also 手を焼く, “to have no end of trouble with someone”, and Naomi’s example is Peterにいつも手を焼いています, “Peter is always giving me trouble.”
  • うまい — two meanings. “Good at”, like じょうずな: この絵、うまいですね, “this painting is really good.” And “delicious”, like おいしい: このピザうまいですよ, “this pizza is good.”
  • じょうきゃく (乗客) — a passenger: 乗, from 乗る, to ride, and 客, from お客さん, a guest or customer, so a riding customer. Swap 客 for 車, car, and you get 乗車, getting on board.

The lesson notes add 超, “super-, hyper-”. The cards below cover them all, with more words from the dialogue and the chat.

Beyond the lessonうまい or おいしい? For taste, the dictionary says, おいしい is the more polite and refined of the two, and still the everyday word; but only うまい can mean skilful, as in 歌がうまい, “a good singer”. Another dictionary adds that おいしい grew out of いしい, a word of the court ladies, and that women tend to say おいしい rather than うまい. Hajime’s 超うまそう suits a casual call between friends.

超 started as a prefix: 超満員, packed beyond full; 超音速, supersonic. As an adverb, “really”, dictionaries mark it as slang (俗に), with a first example from 1981, 超忙しい. A 2013 study notes it came to be seen as youth slang from the late 1980s, entering a yearbook of new words as such in 1988, and is now on its way to being an ordinary word.

彼女 was born as a translation of the Western “she”, and only spread as an everyday pronoun from the Taishō era (1912–26); its sense “girlfriend”, as in 彼女ができた, appears in a 1931 dictionary of new words.

5

〜なら

The grammar point: “if that’s the case”

なら, Peter explains, is the hypothetical form of the copula だ, working as a conjunction that makes conditional clauses. These lessons are a three-part look at conditionals, and lesson 14 did と, where the first part has to be fulfilled for the main clause to happen. With なら, the clause before it states the speaker’s position on whether something is true, now or in the past, or will come about: roughly “if it’s true that”, “if that’s the case”. Naomi’s example, 酒を飲むなら、車を運転しちゃだめだ, “if you’re going to drink, you mustn’t drive”, shows a speaker who feels strongly about it. Yuichi’s, 来週テストがあるなら、勉強したほうがいい, “if there’s a test next week, you’d better study”, is advice from the speaker’s experience or beliefs. And the passenger’s 小川町に行くなら、急行電車の方が速いですよ is advice from what they know. Unlike と, たら and ば, Peter says, なら doesn’t have that strict link between a condition and its result. It was news to Peter (勉強になりました); you don’t really think about it, Naomi agrees.

1

Plain form, or a noun, + なら

行くなら, 飲むなら; 急行なら. The informal form of the verb, or a noun on its own.

2

Built on what you’ve heard

Someone says they’re going to Ogawamachi; you advise them on that basis. Its typical use, the lesson notes say.

3

The order can run backwards

The なら part can happen after the main clause: tomorrow’s trip, tonight’s preparation. と, たら and ば can’t do this.

The lesson notes explain the third point with a trip to an amusement park tomorrow and homework to finish today: only なら works, because the main clause happens first. The same goes for this pair:

If you’re leaving early tomorrow, go to bed early tonight
○明日あした早はやく出でかけるなら、今夜こんやは早はやく寝ねなさい
×明日あした早はやく出でかけたら、… (after you’ve left)
×明日あした早はやく出でかけると、… (と can’t take a command)
×明日あした早はやく出でかければ、… (nor ば, after an action verb)

Beyond the lessonTwo things only なら does. A grammar module from the Tokyo University of Foreign Studies puts it plainly: with なら, the two halves can happen in either order, while with と, ば and たら the first always comes first; and なら is the one you use to take what the listener has just said as your condition, which the other three can’t do. Its example is close to the lesson’s: あの大学へ行くなら、自転車が便利です, “if you’re going to that university, a bike is handy.” One caution: the reversed order belongs to the dictionary form, 行くなら; an older study found that with the past form, 行ったなら, the main clause usually comes later. And after a noun, なら mostly marks a topic, “as for…”: a 2017 thesis found that was its commonest use after nouns in two large collections of real Japanese.

沖縄おきなわに行いくなら、日焼ひやけ止どめを忘わすれないでね。

If you’re going to Okinawa, don’t forget sunscreen.

「今夜こんやは雨あめらしいよ。」「雨あめなら、出でかけるのはやめよう。」

“Apparently it’s going to rain tonight.” “If it’s raining, let’s not go out.”

日本語にほんごを勉べん強きょうするなら、毎日まいにち少すこしずつがいちばんだ。

If you’re going to study Japanese, a little every day is best.

Naomi signs off with one more: 日本語を勉強するなら、Japanesepod101.comがいいですよ. うまい！, says Peter: “nicely put”, the skilful うまい from section 4.

Watch forなら isn’t for results that simply follow. “Go to France and you can see the Arc de Triomphe” takes ば: フランスに行けば、凱旋門を見ることができます (lesson 14’s section 6). Save なら for advice, requests and decisions about what someone is going to do.

ところ, about to and just done

Beyond the lesson: Yasumi’s 乗るところ and Hajime’s 焼いたところ

Beyond the lessonThe dialogue uses ところ twice without stopping to explain it. ところ means a place, and after a verb it marks a point in time: where you are in an action. The verb’s form tells you which point.

FormMeaningExample
〜るところabout to電車でんしゃに乗のるところ
about to get on the train
〜ているところin the middle ofピザを焼やいているところ
baking a pizza right now
〜たところhas justピザを焼やいたところ
has just baked a pizza

Beyond the lessonThree moments. A Japan Foundation grammar column for teachers sets them out with a shower: 今からシャワーを浴びるところだ, about to start; 今シャワーを浴びているところだ, in progress; 今シャワーを浴びたところだ, just finished. The dictionary sense is “that very point, the moment”, with the example さっき着いたところだ, “I’ve only just got here.”

ところ or ばかり? Both 〜たところ and 〜たばかり come out as “just did”, but they measure time differently. ところ marks the stage right after the action; ばかり is how recent it feels to the speaker, so it can stretch to last week, or even years. A grammar handbook for teachers gives the pair: この子は一昨日生まれたばかりです, “this baby was born only the day before yesterday”, works, but not 生まれたところ; and 今電車に乗ったところです, “I’ve just this moment got on the train”, sounds better than 乗ったばかり. A clear past time word, like きのう, rules out たところ altogether.

Two more uses. 〜るところだった is “nearly”: もうちょっとでぶつかるところだった, “I very nearly crashed”, about something that didn’t happen. And 〜たところ、… in mid-sentence means “when I did…, I found”: 電話で問い合わせたところ、締め切りは来週までということがわかった, “when I phoned to ask, I found the deadline was next week.”

「もしもし、今いま大丈夫だいじょうぶ？」「ごめん、今いま会議かいぎが始はじまるところなんだ。」

“Hello, is now a good time?” “Sorry, a meeting’s just about to start.”

今いま、部屋へやを片付かたづけているところだから、少すこし待まって。

I’m in the middle of tidying my room, so hang on a bit.

ちょうど今いま、駅えきに着ついたところだよ。

I’ve just this minute got to the station.

Polite, then casual

Beyond the lesson: Peter’s “listen for the change in politeness”, line by line

Beyond the lessonYasumi speaks to two people in the space of a minute, and it shows in every line: polite です and ます to a stranger, plain forms between friends. Here are lines from my retelling, with Hajime’s 来なよ from the lesson.

Polite, with a strangerCasual, between friends
あの、すみませんあ、ヤスミ？
この電車でんしゃ、小川町おがわまちまで行いきますか？今いまどのへん？ (no です, no particle)
急きゅう行こうは川越かわごえにも止とまりますか？まだ池いけ袋ぶくろ。 (a noun on its own)
ご親切しんせつに、ありがとうございます早はやく来きなよ！

Beyond the lessonSwitching is a skill. A 2019 Kyushu University study describes native speakers moving between です・ます and plain forms skilfully, keeping the conversation going and relationships smooth, while even advanced learners find it hard to switch and can end up sounding unnatural. Yasumi’s two conversations are the textbook case.

来なよ. The な is short for なさい and goes on the ます-stem, so 来な is きな, “come”, not the 来るな of “don’t come”. Dictionaries call it a plain, even offhand, command, which is why it belongs between friends; the よ on the end adds push, as in はやく来いよ.

Does anyone say どういたしまして? Less than textbooks suggest. A 1977 study argued that answering thanks with いいえ or どういたしまして can sound standoffish, so people use gestures instead. A street survey in Kagoshima, which recorded how people replied when thanked for giving directions or taking a photo, found that half said nothing at all, just smiling or nodding, and only 2 of 284 said どういたしまして. Students in a 2020 comparison said things like いやいや, 全然いいよ or いいえ、とんでもないです; the passenger in my retelling says いえいえ.

まもなく…がまいります. Station announcements use まいる, a humble word for “come”. The government’s 2007 guidelines on keigo class it as 謙譲語Ⅱ, which describes things courteously for the person you’re addressing, and note that it works for things as well as people: バスが参りました, “here’s the bus”. It’s more formal than plain です and ます, and comes with ます: 参るよ to a friend would sound odd. Kyoto’s subway still announces まもなく１番のりばに竹田行がまいります; at two of its tram stops, Hiroshima’s Hiroden changed in 2025 to まもなく電車が到着します, dropping the destination so that nobody would be told the wrong one.

まもなく、二番線にばんせんに各駅停車かくえきていしゃ小川町おがわまち行いきがまいります。

The local train for Ogawamachi will shortly arrive at platform 2.

もう始はじまってるよ。早はやく来きなよ！

It’s already started. Come on, hurry up!

8

The many jobs of 焼く

Beyond the lesson: from pizza to photos, jealousy and Naomi’s 手を焼く

Beyond the lessonOne verb, eleven senses. The dictionary gives 焼く eleven senses, and Naomi’s two are only the start. In order: to burn (枯れ草を焼く, burn off dead grass); to cook over a fire (魚を焼く); to make something by heat, which puts roof tiles, charcoal and cookies together (クッキーを焼く); to tan (肌を小麦色に焼く, to a golden brown); to print photos from a negative; to cauterise, or scald (スープで舌を焼く, burn your tongue on soup); to torment yourself (恋に身を焼く, to be consumed by love); to fuss and fret, as in 世話を焼く and 手を焼く; to be jealous; to burn data onto a CD or DVD; and, in the pleasure quarters of early Edo-period Kyoto and Osaka, to flatter.

Fussing and fretting. 手を焼く is not knowing how to deal with something: 反抗期の子供に手を焼く, to be at your wits’ end with a rebellious child. 世話を焼く runs the other way: to look after other people without being asked, as in 同窓会の世話を焼く, to organise the class reunion.

Jealousy, with rice cake. To be jealous is 焼き餅を焼く, and the dictionary explains that 焼き餅, a toasted rice cake, is the jealous 焼く with 餅 added. A proverb puts the two idioms together: 焼き餅焼くとて手を焼くな, “toast your rice cakes, but don’t burn your hands”: jealousy taken too far brings trouble, so keep it in moderation. The jealous sense can also be written 妬く.

The 焼き foods. 焼き鳥, chicken on skewers, once meant small birds such as thrushes and sparrows grilled whole. 目玉焼き, a fried egg, is named for its two circles, white and yolk, which look like an eyeball. たこ焼き began in the Kansai, sold from stalls at the fairs of Osaka’s Tenma Tenjin shrine since the start of the Shōwa era. Then there’s お好み焼き, 焼き肉, and lesson 14’s 焼き芋.

And the pizza. Pizza came to Japan after the Second World War by way of America, and from the 1970s it spread around the world as fast food. Roppongi’s Nicolas, opened in 1954 by an Italian-American New Yorker who had worked for the occupation authorities, is said to have been the first restaurant to introduce pizza to Japan. Pizza Hut arrived in 1973, when pizza was still unfamiliar, and on 30 September 1985 Domino’s opened what it calls Japan’s first pizza-delivery shop, in Ebisu. Japanese tastes followed: a tuna special in 1986, Domino’s first Japanese-style pizza, 炭火焼チキテリ, charcoal-grilled teriyaki chicken, in 1992, and マヨじゃが, mayonnaise and potato, in 1994.

母ははは、頼たのんでもいないのに、いつも世話せわを焼やいてくれる。

My mother always fusses over me, even when I haven’t asked.

海うみで一日いちにち遊あそんだら、顔かおが真まっ赤かに日焼ひやけした。

After a day playing at the beach, my face got badly sunburnt.

Quiz

Pick the word or form that fits. Each answer comes with the reason.

1. 「京都きょうとに行いこうと思おもってるんだ。」「京都きょうとに＿＿＿、紅葉こうようの時期じきがいいよ。」

“I’m thinking of going to Kyoto.” “If you’re going to Kyoto, autumn-leaf season is best.”

Advice that picks up what the other person just said: なら. The advice comes before the trip, so not 行ったら.

2. 「ピザが食たべたいな。」「ピザ＿＿＿、駅前えきまえの店みせがおいしいよ。」

“I feel like pizza.” “If it’s pizza you want, the place by the station is good.”

Noun + なら: “if it’s … you mean”.

3. 明日あした早はやく＿＿＿、今夜こんやは早はやく寝ねなさい。

If you’re leaving early tomorrow, go to bed early tonight.

The なら clause can come later in time than the main one; と and たら can’t do that.

4. 車くるまで＿＿＿、お酒さけは飲のまないでね。

If you’re coming by car, don’t drink, OK?

A request about something someone plans to do: なら. と can’t take a request, nor can ば after an action verb.

5. 今いまから出でかける＿＿＿です。

I’m just about to go out.

Dictionary form + ところ: about to.

6. 今いま、晩ばんご飯はんを作つくっている＿＿＿だから、あとで電話でんわするね。

I’m in the middle of making dinner, so I’ll call you later.

ている + ところ: right in the middle of it.

7. たった今いま、駅えきに着ついた＿＿＿です。

I’ve just this moment arrived at the station.

た + ところ: have just done it.

8. 電車でんしゃに＿＿＿ところだから、またあとでかけるね。

I’m about to get on the train, so I’ll call you back later.

About to: the dictionary form + ところ. 乗ったところ would be “just got on”.

9. この電車でんしゃは＿＿＿なので、全部ぜんぶの駅えきに止とまります。

This train is a local, so it stops at every station.

各駅停車: the train that stops at every station.

10. 急きゅう行こうは、この駅えきに＿＿＿か。

Does the express stop at this station?

止まる: the train stops. 止める is stopping something else.

11. 新幹線しんかんせんは、飛行機ひこうきほど＿＿＿ない。

The Shinkansen isn’t as fast as a plane.

速い is speed; 早い is early or soon.

12. 息子むすこのいたずらには、本当ほんとうに手てを＿＿＿いる。

I’m really at my wits’ end with my son’s mischief.

手を焼く: to have no end of trouble with.

13. 姉あねは、絵えを描かくのがとても＿＿＿。

My sister is very good at drawing.

うまい also means skilful; おいしい only means tasty.

14. このケーキ、＿＿＿うまそう！

This cake looks seriously good!

超 in casual speech: “really, seriously”.
