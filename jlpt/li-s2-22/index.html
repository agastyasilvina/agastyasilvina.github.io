Key lines

The teller’s keigo: respectful for Taiga, humble for the bank

The lesson’s dialogue is thirteen lines long, so these three are quoted exactly; section 1 covers the rest in my retelling. The English is my own rendering.

ありがとうございます。本日ほんじつ、おいくらご入にゅう金きんなさいますか。

Thank you. How much will you be paying in today? — Teller

Grammar: 本日, the formal “today”; おいくら, a polite いくら; ご入金なさいますか, the grammar point: ご + 入金 + なさる, respectful because it’s the customer who pays in (section 5).

かしこまりました。用意よういが出来できましたら、お名前なまえをお呼よびいたしますので、少しょう々しょうお待まちください。

Certainly. I’ll call your name when everything’s ready, so please wait a moment. — Teller

Grammar: かしこまりました, “certainly”; 出来ましたら, a polite たら (lesson 16); お呼びいたします, humble: the teller’s own action, done for the customer; ので, “so”; 少々お待ちください, “one moment, please”.

こちらが、虎畑大河とらばたけたいが様さまの通つう帳ちょうでございます。キャッシュカードは1週しゅう間かんから10日とおか後ごに、ご自宅じたくにお送おくりいたします。

Here is your passbook, Torabatake-sama. Your cash card will be sent to your home in a week to ten days. — Teller

Grammar: でございます, an extra-polite です; 〜から〜後に, “in … to …”; ご自宅, your home, with a respectful ご; お送りいたします, humble again: the bank sends it.

Vocabulary

The hosts’ words, in their order

The hosts’ notes first, then a card for each word, plus more from the dialogue, each with an example of my own.

  • もうしこみようし (申し込み用紙) — an application form: 申し込み, an application or proposal, from 申し込む, to apply or propose; 用, use or purpose; and 紙, paper.
  • きにゅう (記入) — filling in a form: 記, to write, and 入, to put in, so 書いて入れることを記入といいます, Yuichi explains. 申し込み用紙に記入して下さい, “please fill out the application form”; the dialogue adds the polite ご: 申込用紙にご記入下さい.
  • にゅうきん (入金) — a deposit, payment, money received: 自分の口座に1万円入金する, “I paid 10,000 yen into my account.” The opposite is 引き出し, from lesson 21.
  • つうちょう (通帳) — a bank book, passbook: 通帳を3冊持っています, says Yuichi, “I have three passbooks.” Some accounts have no passbook these days, Naomi says: online banks, Yuichi guesses. If Peter opened an account now, it would definitely be at Shinsei Bank: everything online, in English too, and free transfers, for personal accounts at least, Peter thinks. No passbook, Naomi notes. Online banking is further ahead in America, Peter says, where people move money almost entirely online. Naomi finds it scary and can’t do it; Peter puts that down to age and is quickly told off. Many people in the US won’t bank online either, Peter adds: what if you log in and the balance says zero? Still, Naomi is now thinking of opening a Shinsei account.
  • そしな (粗品) — a small present from a company: 粗, rough or coarse, and 品, product, a humble expression, used only in business, Naomi says, never privately: not 「誕生日おめでとう、Peter。これ、粗品」. Business bait, えさ, Peter calls it; to get you to come to the shop, Yuichi says. In a shop you’ll hear いつもありがとうございます。こちらは粗品でございます. Researching this story, Naomi talked to a teller at Sumitomo Mitsui and came home with armfuls: a memo pad, a mouse pad and more. Tissues too, says Yuichi. Naomi felt a little guilty.
  • けっこう (結構) — splendid, nice, well enough: a tricky word, Naomi says, whose meaning depends on the context. As an adverb, 結構いいです, “it’s pretty good”; and 結構です can mean “that’s enough, I’m good”. Offered coffee, コーヒーでいいですか？, Yuichi says コーヒーで結構です, “coffee’s fine”; asked 何か飲みますか？, plain 結構です is “no thanks”. In the dialogue, 1円でも結構です: “even one yen is enough.”

The lesson notes add キャッシュカード, 自宅 (one’s home), 名刺 (a business card) and 馬鹿 (a fool). The cards below cover them all, with more words from the dialogue.

Beyond the lessonWhat the dictionaries say. 記入 is writing into a set form, as in 必要事項を記入する, “fill in the required details”, and 用紙 is paper in a fixed format for a particular use: 所定の用紙に書く, “write on the form provided”. 通帳 is a book for recording the dates and amounts of savings, or of goods bought and sold on credit; it can also be read かよいちょう.

名刺 comes from China, where a 刺 was a slip of shaved bamboo or wood with a name written on it. In Japan, handwritten cards of washi paper with just a name on them were in use by the early 19th century at least, and they were left at a house to show you had called while nobody was in. Printed cards came from the West: officials who dealt with foreigners as Japan opened up at the end of the Edo period used them first, and in 1854 cards giving titles and names were exchanged with the American envoys. They spread from the Meiji era on, and Japan is now said to use business cards more than any other country. The basic manners today: the junior person, the visitor, offers a card first, with both hands.

5

なさる and いたす

The grammar point: raise the customer, lower yourself

Honorific expressions, Peter explains, are for talking about someone else’s action: you raise that person up, as if looking up to them. Here the honorific verb that replaces する is なさる: exactly the same meaning, “to do”, just far more polite. It’s an old verb that behaves like くださる: drop the る and add います, なさいます. So おいくらご入金なさいますか means the same as いくら入金しますか, Naomi says, but the plain version isn’t polite enough for business.

The humble form is for your own action: you lower yourself. お名前をお呼びいたします, “I’ll call your name”, is built in steps, Peter shows: 名前を呼ぶ; polite, 名前を呼びます; humble, the prefix お plus the ます-stem plus する, お呼びする, politely お呼びします; and with いたす, the humble verb for “to do”, in place of する, お呼びいたします. All the same meaning, at a higher level of respect. And you’ve used this pattern all along, Peter says: お願いします is お + 願い + します, and very polite people say よろしくお願いいたします. Naomi sums it up: for someone else’s action, します becomes なさいます; for your own, いたします.

At Japanese school, Peter says, it was always honorific Japanese with the teacher: Naomi先生、昨日何をなさいましたか？ 何もしませんでした。Peterさんは何をしましたか？ 私は勉強いたしました: raising the teacher, lowering yourself, about as polite as Japanese gets. Then a role-play. Classmates: 今夜、何する？ 新宿に行く. Co-workers: 今夜何しますか？ 新宿に行きます. A student to a teacher: Naomi先生、今夜、何をなさいますか？, and the teacher, being higher up, can answer plainly: 新宿に行きます. The teacher asks back, 今夜何しますか、Yuichiさんは？, and the student answers humbly: テストがあるので、勉強いたします. Would a normal student really talk to a teacher like that? Not usually, Naomi says; Yuichi just uses します with a professor. But with customers, you must.

Some verbs have their own words

する → なさる, 来る・いる → いらっしゃる, 見る → ご覧になる, 寝る → お休みになる. The lesson notes’ list.

The lesson notes add that お usually goes on Japanese words and everyday words, and ご on words from Chinese, though with many exceptions; since most する compounds come from Chinese, you’ll meet ご〜なさる far more often than お〜なさる: ご旅行なさる, ご出発なさる, but お引越しなさる. Their examples: 明日は、何時にご出発なさいますか; ここでお待ちになりますか; and for the humble side, お部屋までご案内いたします, “I’ll show you to your room.”

Beyond the lessonWhat the dictionaries say. なさる is the respectful word for する or なす, and as a helper verb it comes as お…なさる or ご…なさる. Before ます the usual form is なさいます, not なさります; くださる does the same, becoming ください before ます, just as Peter says. いたす is the humble する, used mostly as いたします, and the dictionary’s example of its helper use is the lesson’s pattern: お静かにお願いいたします, “please be quiet”.

The government’s rules. The 2007 guide to keigo, more in section 6, lists ご〜なさる among the respectful forms, with 御利用なさる as its example: it can only be made from する verbs, and only where ご suits the word, as it does 入金. It files ご覧になる and お休みになる as irregular お〜になる forms, next to おいでになる, for going, coming or being somewhere, and お召しになる, for wearing. And the humble お〜する needs someone on the receiving end: you can お届けする or ご案内する, deliver something to someone or show someone the way, but there’s no お食べする, since eating isn’t done toward anybody. The bank’s お呼びする and お送りする both have a receiver: Taiga. Using a humble form for the customer’s own action is the classic mistake; lesson 21 has the guide’s example, お持ちしますか.

部ぶ長ちょうは、もうお帰かえりになりました。

The department head has already gone home.

明日あした、こちらからお電話でんわいたします。

We’ll call you tomorrow.

先生せんせいは、何なにをお飲のみになりますか。

What would you like to drink, Sensei?

The map of keigo

Beyond the lesson: the government’s five kinds of keigo, and double keigo

Beyond the lessonFive kinds, not three. The lesson works with the classic split into respectful, humble and polite language. In 2007 the government’s Council for Cultural Affairs published a guide, 敬語の指針, that divides keigo into five kinds instead:

TypeThe guide’s examples, and what it does
尊敬語そんけいごいらっしゃる, おっしゃる: raises the person whose action, thing or state you describe, someone on the listener’s side or a third person
謙譲語けんじょうごⅠ伺うかがう, 申もうし上あげる: for an action from your side toward someone, raises the person it’s directed at
謙譲語けんじょうごⅡ
(丁重語ていちょうご)		参まいる, 申もうす: describes your own side’s actions courteously, for the sake of the person you’re talking to
丁寧語ていねいごです, ます: speaks politely to the person you’re talking to
美化語びかごお酒さけ, お料理りょうり: makes things sound more refined

The old 謙譲語 is split in two, Ⅰ and Ⅱ, and the old 丁寧語 becomes 丁寧語 plus 美化語. The lesson’s forms fit like this. The general humble form お(ご)〜する, as in お届けする and ご案内する, is 謙譲語Ⅰ. いたす is 謙譲語Ⅱ, alongside 参る, 申す and おる. And お(ご)〜いたす is both at once. The guide’s example is 駅で先生をお待ちいたします, “I’ll wait for the teacher at the station”: お待ちする raises the teacher, the person you wait for, and いたす adds courtesy toward whoever you’re talking to, who may be the teacher or someone else. So the teller’s お名前をお呼びいたします raises Taiga, whose name will be called, and is courteous to Taiga as the listener too.

Double keigo. Using the same kind of keigo twice on one word is 二重敬語: お読みになられる, for instance, makes お読みになる and then adds a second respectful れる. Double keigo is generally considered wrong, the guide says, though some have become established by custom: お召し上がりになる and お見えになる on the respectful side, and お伺いする, お伺いいたす and お伺い申し上げる on the humble side. Joining two keigo verbs with て, as in お読みになっていらっしゃる, isn’t double keigo, and is basically acceptable.

Whose side are you on? The teller lowers the bank, お送りいたします, because to a customer the bank is the teller’s own side. The guide calls this ウチ and ソト, inside and outside, and it reaches your boss too: talking to someone from a client company about 田中部長, Department Head Tanaka, you can say 田中 or 部長の田中, but 田中部長 is inappropriate, because it doesn’t treat the boss as one of your own. In 文化庁’s latest survey of attitudes to the language, published on 2 October 2026, people were asked how a company receptionist should mention Section Chief Suzuki to an outsider: 37.4% chose 鈴木は…, 35.8% 課長の鈴木は…, 22.3% 鈴木課長は… and 3.6% 鈴木さんは…. Schools are the exception. By ウチ and ソト, a teacher should tell a parent 田中はおりません, but most people prefer 田中先生, the guide notes, because schools put the pupil first; in the new survey 71.9% chose 田中先生は…, and 8.1% plain 田中は…. And on keigo’s future, 54.4% said it should be treasured as beautiful, traditional Japanese with a rich range of expression, against 32.7% who wanted it simple and easy to understand, to suit a new age.

明日あしたは一日いちにち、会社かいしゃにおります。

I’ll be at the office all day tomorrow. — 謙譲語Ⅱ: おる for いる

申もうし訳わけございません。部ぶ長ちょうの田中たなかは、ただいま席せきを外はずしております。

7

結構, 粗品, 馬鹿

Beyond the lesson: a yes that means no, a humble gift, and a Buddhist fool

Beyond the lesson結構. The word’s original sense is a construction, putting something together. As a な-adjective, the dictionary gives three senses: splendid and without a flaw, 結構な眺め, “a fine view”; fine, satisfactory, お値段はいくらでも結構です, “any price will do”, the teller’s sense; and needing nothing more, もう結構です, which the larger dictionary says is used to decline politely: 『お茶はいかがですか』『結構です』. As an adverb it’s “quite”, more than you’d expected: 結構おもしろい.

Yes or no? Even the government warns about it. A 2018 report from its language council on communication gives the restaurant case: asked whether your plate can be taken away, answer 結構です or いいです and the staff may not know whether to clear it, while one extra word, まだ結構です or もういいです, prevents the mix-up. The 2020 guidelines on Easy Japanese, for writing to foreign residents, list 結構です among the expressions to avoid because it has two meanings, and suggest 良いです or 要りません instead.

粗品. 粗 is rough, careless, and it’s also how you show modesty when you offer something: 粗品, 粗酒, 粗茶, as in 粗茶ですが一服どうぞ, “it’s only poor tea, but do have a cup”. The dictionaries define 粗品 a little more broadly than Naomi: a humble word for a present or a giveaway that you give someone, also read そひん. Why run down your own gift? The 2007 keigo guide explains the convention: you may describe things of your own as small, low or poor, as in 小社, “our small company”, 愚見, “my foolish opinion”, and 拙稿, “my clumsy draft”, but that’s a convention of language, not necessarily what you actually think; making your own things small is a way of showing consideration for the other person.

馬鹿. Horse and deer are there only for the sound: the kanji are 当て字. The dictionaries trace the word to Sanskrit, moha or mahallaka, both about ignorance or folly, said to have spread as monks’ slang. Another theory takes it from 破家, foolish enough to wreck your household, and the 日本大百科全書 says the origin is disputed. The famous story from the 史記, of the eunuch chancellor Zhao Gao presenting a deer to the Second Emperor and insisting it was a horse, is only a folk etymology, the encyclopedia says. It’s basically an insult, 大辞泉 notes, but it can be said with sympathy, warmth or affection: 馬鹿だな。そんなに思いつめなくていいのに, “you idiot, you didn’t have to take it so hard”.

And it’s an eastern word: 馬鹿 is commoner in the Kantō region, 阿呆 in Kansai. Kansai people do say バカ, the 日本国語大辞典 adds, but there it often sounds harsher than アホ. Where アホ ends and バカ begins is the question behind 全国アホ・バカ分布考, by Matsumoto Osamu, a producer at the broadcaster 朝日放送: the book follows the hunt, through enquiries to towns and villages all over Japan and old dictionaries, to a finished dialect map, and the 1991 episode of 探偵！ナイトスクープ that completed the map won awards.

The passbook

Beyond the lesson: what happened to passbooks, cash cards and Shinsei Bank

Beyond the lessonA week to ten days. The teller’s timing still holds. MUFG posts a new cash card to your registered address by 簡易書留, registered mail, marked not to be forwarded, and asks you to get in touch if it hasn’t arrived after a week to ten days; SMBC gives the same “about a week to ten days”. A change-of-address notice at the post office won’t help: the card isn’t forwarded but goes back to the bank, MUFG says. Banks can check your address by post in the same way, as lesson 21 explains.

Paper now costs extra. Since 2021 the three big banks have charged for paper passbooks on new accounts. At Mizuho, accounts opened from 18 January 2021 pay ¥1,100 for each passbook issued or renewed, with customers aged 70 and over exempt. At SMBC, personal accounts opened from 1 April 2021 pay ¥550 a year for a paper passbook, except for under-18s and customers aged 75 and over. At MUFG it’s also ¥550 a year, on accounts opened from 1 April 2022, except for under-18s and customers aged 70 and over. The way round the fee is a web passbook, which shows your balance and history on a phone or computer: Mizuho’s みずほe-口座, SMBC’s Web通帳, MUFG’s Eco通帳.

Two yen is allowed. Mizuho has no rule on how much you pay in when you open an account, and you can open one with ¥0; MUFG takes deposits of ¥1 or more, in steps of ¥1; and a ゆうちょ銀行 account can be opened with ¥0. Leaving the account untouched is another matter. At SMBC, an account opened from 1 April 2021 that sees no deposits or withdrawals for two years pays ¥1,100 a year, unless, for example, it holds ¥10,000 or more or has internet banking set up; and if the balance is less than the fee, the bank takes the lot and closes the account. MUFG, for accounts opened from 1 July 2021, charges ¥1,320 a year once an account has gone two years unused and three months have passed since it was told, with some exceptions, and it too closes an account whose balance won’t cover the fee. Taiga’s two yen wouldn’t last.

Shinsei Bank. Peter’s choice took the name 新生銀行, “new life bank”, in June 2000, when it was renamed from the Long-Term Credit Bank of Japan, 日本長期信用銀行. Its English page in June 2008 began “Welcome! We provide an online banking service in English”, adding that the branches mainly worked in Japanese. From November 2007, customers got up to 10, 5 or 3 free online transfers to other banks a month, depending on their business with the bank, then paid ¥300 a transfer; transfers within Shinsei stayed free whatever the balance. On 4 January 2023 it became SBI新生銀行, SBI Shinsei Bank, and its English page still offers English internet banking and customer support, with at least one free domestic online transfer a month.

Who banks online? In the government’s survey for 2008, the year of this lesson, 12.6% of people who went online from a computer had used the internet for “financial transactions (net banking, online trading and so on)”. In the survey for 2025, published in May 2026, the figure was 29.4% of internet users, on any device. The two surveys ask differently, and both lump banking in with trading and the like, so they’re only a rough guide.

Quiz

Pick the word or form that fits. Each answer comes with the reason.

1. おいくらご入にゅう金きん＿＿＿か。

How much would you like to pay in?

The customer is the one paying in, so it’s respectful: ご〜なさいます.

2. お名前なまえをお呼よび＿＿＿。

I’ll call your name.

Your own action, toward the customer, so it’s humble: お〜いたします.

3. ここでお待まちに＿＿＿か。

Will you be waiting here?

お + ます-stem + になる: the respectful form, for someone else’s action.

4. 社しゃ長ちょうは、今いまどちらに＿＿＿か。

Where is the company president now?

いらっしゃる: the respectful いる. おります and まいります are for yourself.

5. この写真しゃしんを＿＿＿ましたか。

Have you seen this photo?

ご覧になる: the respectful 見る. 拝見する is humble, for your own looking.

6. 先生せんせいは、もう＿＿＿。

The teacher has already gone to bed.

寝る has its own respectful form: お休みになる.

7. お部屋へやまで、＿＿＿いたします。

I’ll show you to your room.

案内 is a word from Chinese, so it takes ご: ご案内いたします.

8. カードは、ご自宅じたくに＿＿＿いたします。

We’ll send the card to your home.

お + ます-stem + いたす: お送りいたします.

9. 「コーヒー、もう一杯いっぱいいかがですか。」「いえ、もう＿＿＿です。」

“Another cup of coffee?” “No, I’m fine, thank you.”

結構です: “that’s enough”, a polite no.

10. 口座こうざを開ひらいたら、＿＿＿にティッシュをもらった。

When I opened an account, I got a packet of tissues as a free gift.

粗品: a small gift from a business.

11. この用紙ようしに、お名前なまえをご＿＿＿ください。

Please write your name on this form.

ご記入ください: please fill it in.

12. 給きゅう料りょうが口座こうざに＿＿＿された。

My salary was paid into my account.

入金: money going in. 出金 is money coming out.

13. ATMで、＿＿＿に記帳きちょうした。

I updated my passbook at the ATM.

通帳に記帳する: to update a passbook.

14. 初はじめて会あった人ひとと、＿＿＿を交換こうかんした。

I swapped business cards with someone I’d just met.

名刺交換: swapping business cards.
