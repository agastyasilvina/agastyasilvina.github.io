I’ll tell her to call you. Promise?

Part four of Tom and Junko. Tom won’t give up, so he asks Junko to introduce a friend instead. Junko offers to call Misa and tell her to come over, and when Misa doesn’t pick up, promises to tell her to call Tom directly. The grammar point is 〜ように言う, “to tell someone to”, the indirect way of passing on a request: 来るように言ってみましょうか, 電話をかけるように言っておきます.

The dialogue

The whole scene, retold for review

The lesson’s dialogue, retold in my own wording so you can review it in one place. It keeps the same scene, characters, words and grammar, and carries on from my retelling of lesson 11. Every line is rephrased, Junko asks with 頼む where the lesson has 言う (the lesson notes give both), and the last line is new: one more instruction, in the negative. The lesson’s exact key lines are in section 3. Hide the English with the button at the top to test yourself.

Scene

The same café. Tom still hasn’t given up.

Tom

それじゃあ、誰だれかお友達ともだちを紹しょう介かいしてもらえませんか。

Well then, could you introduce me to one of your friends?

Junko

あ、いいですよ。みさっていう友達ともだちが、いつも暇ひまそうにしてるんです。ちょうどいいかもしれません。

Oh, sure. I have a friend called Misa who always seems to have time on her hands. She might be just right.

Junko

今いまから電話でんわして、このカフェに来くるように頼たのんでみましょうか。

Shall I call her now and ask her to come over to the café?

Tom

ぜひぜひ、お願ねがいします！どんな子こですか？かわいいですか？

Yes, please, please! What’s she like? Is she cute?

Scene

Junko calls. The phone rings and rings.

Junko

あれ、出でませんね……。じゃあ、あとでトムさんに直ちょく接せつ電話でんわするように、伝つたえておきますね。

Huh, she’s not picking up… Then I’ll pass on a message for her to call you directly later.

Tom

本当ほんとうですね？絶対ぜったいですよ！

Really? Promise?

Junko

はい、はい。

Yes, yes.

Junko, thinking

（……みさには、知しらない番号ばんごうの電話でんわには絶対ぜったいに出でないように言いっておこう。）

(…And I’ll tell Misa never to answer calls from numbers she doesn’t know.)

2

The scene

Naomi, Yuichi and Peter, before and after the dialogue

Tom again, Peter notes: the American, Naomi adds, with the Ibaraki accent. So is today the last part? It carries on, says Yuichi (まだ続きます); Peter hasn’t read ahead, Naomi realises, and Peter admits it: ばれましたね. The grammar is ように言う, asking someone to do something. Yuichi recaps: Tom, an American university student majoring in Japanese, met Junko at a café and asked for her number, but Junko gave him a fake one so that he couldn’t call. Has that ever happened to Yuichi? No. But Tom doesn’t seem to be giving up, Naomi says: he’s trying hard again today.

After the dialogue

Isn’t this conversation strange, Peter asks? Junko doesn’t much like Misa, Naomi thinks. Worse, says Peter: is this even legal, sending a stalker off in someone else’s direction? Tom isn’t a stalker, Yuichi objects. Not yet, all three agree. Junko isn’t a good person, Yuichi decides, and seems mean-spirited (性格悪そう), Naomi agrees. Why, asks Peter? Because Misa is Junko’s friend, Yuichi explains, stumbling on the way (ですもん isn’t quite right, Yuichi admits, and Naomi teases that Yuichi’s Japanese is getting clumsy). It’s like selling out a friend, Naomi says: 友達を売る. Not selling, Peter suggests, more like passing the problem along: is there a word like 問題回し? There isn’t, Naomi says, but Yuichi has the real one: たらい回し. A たらい is a big bucket, a washtub, Naomi explains, and 回す means to turn, but also to pass to someone: passing the tub, the responsibility, to somebody else. Not as a verb on its own, though: Peter tries Naomi先生に、たらい回してあげます, and Naomi says you wouldn’t really. You’d say 問題をたらい回しにする: not wanting any responsibility, you just hand the problem on.

Phrases worth taking from the chat:

  • まだ続きます — “there’s more to come”.
  • 読んでないんだ — “so you haven’t read it”: 〜んだ, realising something.
  • ばれましたね — “you’ve caught me out”: ばれる, from lesson 9.
  • 友達を売ってるみたいなもの — “it’s like selling out a friend”: 〜みたいなもの, “something like”.
  • 日本語つたなくなってきた — “your Japanese is getting clumsy”: つたない + なってくる, a change coming on (lesson 6).
  • がんばってくだせぇ！ — Yuichi’s sign-off: a jokey, rough-sounding がんばってください.

Key lines

Junko’s two offers, both with ように言う

The lesson’s dialogue is only six lines long, so just these two are quoted exactly; section 1 covers the rest in my retelling. The English is my own rendering.

今いまから電話でんわして、ここに来くるように言いってみましょうか。

Shall I call her now and tell her to come here? — Junko

Grammar: 今から, “right now”; 電話して, the て-form linking two actions; 来るように言う, “tell her to come” (section 5); 〜てみましょうか, “shall I try…?”, an offer built on てみる (section 4).

あれ、電話でんわに出でませんね。じゃあ後あとでトムさんに直ちょく接せつ電話でんわをかけるように言いっておきます。

Huh, she’s not answering. Then I’ll tell her to call you directly later, Tom. — Junko

Grammar: 電話に出ない, “not answering”; トムさんに直接, “to you, Tom, directly”; 電話をかけるように言う, “tell her to call”; 〜ておきます, done in advance, ready for later (section 4).

4

Vocabulary

The lesson’s word list, in its order

The hosts’ notes first, then a card for each word, plus a few more from the dialogue and the chat, each with an example of my own.

  • だれか (誰か) — someone, somebody, anyone. だれ is “who”; だれか is “anyone”, used especially in questions, Yuichi says, and だれも “anyone” in negative sentences: nobody. So: 誰がひまですか？, “who’s free?”; 誰かひまですか？, “is anyone free?”; 誰もひまじゃないです, “nobody’s free”. Yuichi adds the same set for なに: 何を食べましたか？, “what did you eat?”; 何か食べましたか？, “did you eat anything?”; 何も食べませんでした, “I didn’t eat anything.”
  • ぜひ (是非) — by all means, please, certainly: like どうしても or 必ず. People double it for emphasis: ぜひぜひ日本に来てください, Naomi says; ぜひぜひJapanesepod101.comを聞いて下さい, says Yuichi. ぜひぜひ is stronger, and more casual: you wouldn’t say 是非是非契約を結んで下さい in a business meeting, Peter notes, and with friends you can stack up four.
  • 電話に出る, 電話をかける — to answer the phone, and to make a call. Naomi’s example: 昨日Peterさんに電話をかけたけど、出なかった, and with the subjects filled in, 昨日私はPeterさんに電話をかけたけれど、Peterさんは電話に出なかった. Mind the particles, Naomi says, に and を, and the verbs, Peter adds: 出る means to exit, to leave, so “appear on the phone” took Peter a while; English says “pick up”.
  • 直接 (ちょくせつ) — direct, personal: 今日私は家から直接スタジオに来ました, “I came straight to the studio from home today”; 社長と直接話をしたい, “I’d like to talk to the president directly.” It means じかに, and its opposite is 間接, indirect.
  • 言ってみる, 言っておく — after a て-form, みる and おく are auxiliary verbs. 〜てみる is making an attempt, to see what it’s like or what happens: テストを受けてみる, “I’ll give the test a go.” 〜ておく is doing something in advance, for later: テストを受けておく, taking it this year to know what it’s like for next year. ておく took Peter a long time, until vegetables made it click: you chop them before the oil is hot, because there won’t be time once the cooking starts. 切っておく.

The lesson notes add 紹介する, to introduce; 暇な, free; ちょうどいい, just right; and 絶対に, absolutely, without fail. The cards below cover them, with a few more from the chat.

Beyond the lessonPeter’s 出る. The dictionary doesn’t give 電話に出る a sense of its own. It files it under the 出る of presenting yourself somewhere to do a job or take part, right beside 会議に出る, to attend a meeting, and 競技大会に出る, to enter a competition. So you don’t exit to the phone: you attend to it.

ておく has a second job, and a short form. Besides “in advance” (話だけは聞いておこう, “I’ll at least hear them out”), it means leaving something as it is: 窓を開けておく, “leave the window open”. In speech it shrinks to とく, or どく where the て-form ends in で: 言っとく, 伝えとくよ, 読んどく. てみる, for its part, is defined as doing something as a try, or just going ahead and doing it; the dictionary’s example is 一口、味わってみる, “have a taste”.

是非 is also a noun: 是, right, and 非, wrong, as in 是非を問う, “to question whether something is right”. As an adverb it has three shades: determination, the hosts’ どうしても (計画を是非やり遂げたい, “I want to see the plan through, whatever it takes”); heartfelt hope (是非おいでください, “please do come”); and “without fail”, their 必ず.

5

〜ように言う

The grammar point: telling someone to do something

ように言う, Peter explains, is saying something so that someone does something, and it follows a verb in the non-past plain form: 電話する becomes 電話するように言う, “tell someone to call”; 働く, Yuichi supplies, 働くように言う, “tell someone to work”. It took Peter a long time, and the key is that three people are involved: the speaker, the listener, and a third person the speaker wants to do something. With three of them in the studio, they act it out:

1

Peter, out of the office, calls Yuichi

Yuichiさん、Naomi先生に、私に電話するように言って下さい: “please tell Naomi-sensei to call me.” The listener is to pass it on to a third person, so it’s ように言う.

2

Yuichi answers

わかりました。Naomi先生に、Peterに電話するように言っておきます: “Will do. I’ll tell Naomi-sensei to call you.” ておく, because it’s done ready for Naomi’s call.

3

Or with 頼む

In place of 言う, Naomi adds, you can use 頼む, “to ask”: 電話するように頼む.

The lesson notes fill in the rest. Because you’re asking for something that hasn’t happened yet, the verb before ように is non-past, never past. And between male friends, you might quote the command itself, ここに来いって言おうか, “shall I tell her ‘come here’?”; ここに来るように言う sounds milder and more polite, because it reports the request instead of quoting an order.

Watch for〜と言う reports what someone said; 〜ように言う reports what someone was told to do. 先生は宿題があると言った: the teacher said there was homework. 先生は宿題をするように言った: the teacher told us to do it.

Someone, anyone, no one

Beyond the lesson: the whole か and も family

Beyond the lessonThe hosts’ だれか and だれも belong to a bigger family: add か to a question word for “some-” or “any-”, and も with a negative for “no-”.

Question word+ か+ も, with a negative
誰だれ, who誰だれか someone誰だれも no one
何なに, what何なにか something何なにも nothing
どこ, whereどこか somewhereどこにも nowhere
いつ, whenいつか some day—
どれ, whichどれか any oneどれも none
  • Where the particles go. が and を are often dropped after か (何か食べた) and give way to も (誰も来ない). Other particles come after か (誰かに聞く, どこかで会った) but before も: どこにも行かない, 誰にも言わない.
  • The odd one out. いつも isn’t “never”: it means “always”. For “never”, use 一度も〜ない or 決して〜ない.
  • With a positive, も means “every”: どれもおいしい, “they’re all delicious”; 誰もが知っている, “everyone knows”.

Beyond the lessonThe Japan Foundation’s teaching sheets set these out the same way: question word + か stands for something unspecified, a thing, a time or a place, and question word + も with a negative denies it completely, with any other particle going in between, as in だれにも. The teachers’ note leaves いつも out on purpose, as an adverb of frequency. One more row worth knowing: question word + でも, with a positive, means “any at all”: 誰でもいい, “anyone will do”; いつでもどうぞ, “come any time”.

夏休なつやすみは、どこにも行いかずに家いえでゆっくりした。

In the summer holidays I didn’t go anywhere, just took it easy at home.

いつか、富士山ふじさんに登のぼってみたい。

Some day, I’d like to climb Mount Fuji.

7

Passing on a message

Beyond the lesson: ように言う in real life, on the phone and at work

Beyond the lessonJunko’s 電話をかけるように言っておきます is the real thing: it’s what you say when the person a caller wants isn’t there. Lesson 11’s section 7 has the phone words; this is the part where you take a message.

Why ように, and not a quote

Peter’s three people are the key. A message always involves someone asking, someone it’s for, and you in the middle, and in the middle you report the request with ように. Learners find this surprisingly hard. In a 2012 paper from Akita University, only about one in five of 42 intermediate and advanced learners produced a well-formed ように sentence where reported speech was called for. Even among those who had passed the old JLPT Level 1, fewer than half did, some of their sentences didn’t suit the situation, and the rest quoted directly with と. The paper’s advice: learn which situations want reported speech, then practise it, politeness and all.

ように reports requests and warnings with other verbs too: 伝える, to pass on; お願いする, to ask politely; 注意する, to warn.

大家おおやさんに、夜中よなかに洗濯機せんたくきを回まわさないように注意ちゅういされた。

The landlord warned me not to run the washing machine in the middle of the night.

Taking a message

  • Offer to pass something on with ておく and “shall I?”: 何か、伝えときましょうか, “shall I pass anything on?”, or, more formally, 伝言、伺いましょうか, “may I take a message?” A textbook for teachers of Japanese notes that 〜とく is simply the spoken form of 〜ておく.
  • Offer a call back with Junko’s pattern: 神崎にお電話するように言っておきましょうか, “shall I tell Kanzaki to call you?” At work, the same book also gives the causative from lesson 3: 村田から、電話させましょうか, “shall I have Murata call you?” Outside work, it warns, the causative only works when you have the authority to tell the absent person what to do, as a parent does a child: 帰ったら息子に電話させますね, “I’ll have my son call you when he’s back.”
  • Leave a message yourself with 伝言をお願いできますか, “could I leave a message?”, which the book counts as beginner to lower-intermediate. A 伝言 is a message passed on through someone else, also called a ことづて.
  • At work, it’s 申し伝えます. On the phone to someone outside the company, your colleagues count as your own side, even your boss. A business-Japanese column calls 部長にお伝えします an in-group, out-group mix-up, because it raises your own boss in front of the caller. 申し伝える, the humble form of 言い伝える, shows respect to the caller instead: 係の者に申し伝えます, “I’ll pass that on to the person in charge.”

A call to the office

申もうし訳わけございません。田中たなかはただいま席せきを外はずしております。

I’m very sorry, Tanaka is away from the desk at the moment. — the receptionist

では、お戻もどりになりましたら、お電話でんわをいただけるようお伝つたえください。

Then could you ask Tanaka-san to call me when they’re back? — the caller

かしこまりました。戻もどりましたら、折おり返かえしお電話でんわするように申もうし伝つたえます。

Certainly. I’ll have Tanaka call you back as soon as they’re back. — the receptionist

Watch forEach side’s honorifics point outward. The caller raises Tanaka, お戻りになりましたら; the receptionist, on Tanaka’s side, uses plain 戻りましたら and no さん, and saves the polite forms for the caller: お電話する, 申し伝えます.

8

たらい回し

Beyond the lesson: Yuichi’s word, from a juggling act to a hospital problem

Beyond the lessonIt started as a stunt. The first sense of たらい回し in the dictionaries is an acrobatic act: lying on your back and spinning a tub with your feet. Foot juggling, 足芸, was popular in Edo and in the Kyoto–Osaka region from the 1820s: a performer on their back would balance a ladder or a barrel on the soles of their feet, and a child of five to eight would climb up and perform. Many performers toured abroad early on, and one of them, 浜碇定吉, appeared at the 1867 Paris Exposition. The dictionaries describe one performer keeping the tub spinning, not a tub handed between people. The passing on is the second sense, which one dictionary dates to a kabuki play of 1782: moving something along in turn, round a limited circle.

Then it turned darker. Under the Meiji constitution, police would detain someone, release them and immediately detain them again, or send them on to another station, a way round the law that was also called たらい回し. And a 1917 glossary of stock-exchange terms uses it for a ring of traders buying the same shares in turn to push up the price.

Today, it’s the word for ambulances turned away. When a crew rings hospital after hospital and none will take the patient, the press calls it たらい回し; the dictionary’s own example is 病院を転々と盥回しにされる. In August 2007, a woman in Nara, seven months pregnant, was taken ill in a supermarket at about 2:45 a.m. The ambulance made 16 requests to 12 hospitals, and around 5 a.m. her baby was stillborn. The media all called it たらい回し, and there was pushback. The news site J-CAST called the coverage “medical bashing”, and reported the first hospital’s own account: its two obstetricians on duty that night had six patients to deal with, including a birth and an emergency caesarean, and went on to the next day’s work without sleep.

The official term is neutral. The Fire and Disaster Management Agency counts 救急搬送困難事案, “cases where ambulance transport was difficult”: four or more requests to hospitals, and 30 minutes or more at the scene. In the week of 5 to 11 October 2026, the 52 fire departments it surveys reported 1,608 such cases (preliminary figures), against 1,488 in the same week a year before; Tokyo alone had 946. Every one of those patients did reach a hospital in the end. Across the country, of the 6,769,172 people taken to hospital by ambulance in 2024, 77.0% were accepted at the first request and 5.7%, about 386,000 people, needed four or more; 20,938 needed eleven or more. The agency counts each request as a phone call.

Passing the buck, in other words

  • 責任転嫁 (せきにんてんか) — shifting the blame: making someone else carry what you should, 責任を他になすりつける.
  • 丸投げ — handing a whole job to someone else. In the building trade, it’s a main contractor passing an entire job to a subcontractor and keeping a fee; elsewhere, leaving work that’s yours to others, as in the dictionary’s 入試問題作成を予備校に丸投げする, “to leave setting the entrance exam entirely to a cram school”.
  • 押し付ける — to force on someone: 責任を押し付ける, “to push the responsibility onto someone”.
  • 友達を売る — Naomi’s phrase. This 売る is betraying your own side for your own gain: 仲間を売る, “to sell out your friends”; 国を売る, “to sell out your country”. One dictionary quotes Mushanokōji Saneatsu’s 1919 novel 『友情』, Friendship, where one friend won’t steal the woman the other loves: それは友を売ることです, “that would be selling out a friend.” Naomi stretches it, for laughs, to Junko handing an admirer on to a friend.

自分じぶんのミスなのに、部下ぶかに責任せきにんを押おし付つけるなんてひどい。

Blaming your staff for your own mistake? That’s awful.

Quiz

Pick the word or form that fits. Each answer comes with the reason.

1. 先生せんせいは学生がくせいたちに、静しずかにする＿＿＿言いった。

The teacher told the students to be quiet.

Telling someone to do something: plain form + ように言う.

2. 弟おとうとに、部屋へやで騒さわが＿＿＿ように言いった。

I told my little brother not to make a racket in his room.

Telling someone not to: 〜ないように言う.

3. 友達ともだちに、駅えきまで迎むかえに来くるように＿＿＿。

I asked a friend to come and pick me up at the station.

〜ように頼む: to ask someone to do something. 頼まれた would mean I was asked.

4. 医者いしゃに、お酒さけを控ひかえる＿＿＿。

The doctor told me to cut down on alcohol.

Being told to: the passive, 〜ように言われた.

5. ＿＿＿、手伝てつだってくれませんか。

Could somebody give me a hand?

誰か: someone, anyone, in a question or request.

6. 教きょう室しつには＿＿＿いなかった。

There was nobody in the classroom.

誰も + a negative: no one.

7. お腹なかがすいた。＿＿＿食たべたいな。

I’m hungry. I feel like eating something.

何か: something.

8. 朝あさから＿＿＿食たべていない。

I haven’t eaten anything since this morning.

何も + a negative: nothing.

9. 何度なんどかけても、彼かれは電話でんわ＿＿＿出でない。

However many times I call, he doesn’t pick up.

電話に出る: to answer the phone. 電話をかける is making the call.

10. 夜よる遅おそくに電話でんわを＿＿＿のは、失礼しつれいだ。

It’s rude to call late at night.

電話をかける: to make a call.

11. 本人ほんにんに＿＿＿聞きいたほうがいいよ。

You’d better ask the person directly.

直接: directly, in person. 間接 would need 間接的に, and means the opposite.

12. 近ちかくに来きたら、＿＿＿寄よってください。

If you’re ever in the area, do drop in.

ぜひ: the warm “by all means” of an invitation.

13. 明日あしたお客きゃくさんが来くるので、部屋へやを掃除そうじして＿＿＿。

We have guests tomorrow, so I’ll clean the room beforehand.

Doing something in advance, ready for later: 〜ておく.

14. おいしいかどうか分わからないけど、一口ひとくち食たべて＿＿＿。

I don’t know if it’s any good, but I’ll try a bite.

Trying something to see what it’s like: 〜てみる.
