The recipe

The whole recipe, retold for review

The lesson’s recipe, retold in my own wording so you can review it in one place: the same dish, steps, words and grammar, but a different cooking show and chef, amounts for four people instead of two, and every step and tip rephrased. The lesson’s exact lines are in section 3. Hide the English with the button at the top to test yourself.

Host

「みんなのキッチン」へようこそ！今日きょうは、山田やまだシェフの人気にんきメニュー、和風わふうから揚あげを作つくりましょう。とってもおいしいんですよ！

Welcome to “Everyone’s Kitchen”! Today let’s make Chef Yamada’s much-loved Japanese-style fried chicken. It’s really delicious!

Ingredients

材ざい料りょう（4人分にんぶん）：
鶏とりもも肉にく　600g、
にんにく　2かけ、
しょうが　2かけ、
しょうゆ　大おおさじ2、
酒さけ　大おおさじ2、
片栗粉かたくりこ　大おおさじ4、
揚あげ油あぶら　適宜てきぎ

Ingredients (serves 4):
600 g chicken thigh
2 cloves of garlic
2 knobs of ginger
2 tbsp soy sauce
2 tbsp sake
4 tbsp potato starch
oil for frying, as needed

Step 1

鶏とりもも肉にくは余分よぶんな脂あぶらを取とって、一口大ひとくちだいに切きる。

Trim the excess fat from the chicken thighs and cut them into bite-size pieces.

Step 2

にんにくとしょうがは皮かわをむいて、すりおろす。

Peel the garlic and the ginger, and grate them.

Step 3

ボウルにしょうゆと酒さけをそれぞれ大おおさじ2杯はいずつ入いれ、すりおろしたにんにくとしょうがを加くわえて混まぜる。

Put 2 tablespoons each of soy sauce and sake in a bowl, add the grated garlic and ginger, and mix.

Step 4

3のたれに鶏肉とりにくを入いれ、味あじがよくしみこむように、手てで軽かるくもむ。そのまま冷蔵庫れいぞうこで30分ぷんほどおく。

Put the chicken into the sauce from step 3 and rub it gently with your hands so the flavour soaks in well. Leave it in the fridge like that for about 30 minutes.

Step 5

ビニール袋ぶくろに片栗粉かたくりこを入いれ、鶏肉とりにくを加くわえたら、袋ふくろを振ふって全体ぜんたいにまんべんなくまぶす。

Put the potato starch in a plastic bag, add the chicken, then shake the bag to coat every piece evenly.

Step 6

深ふかめのフライパンに揚あげ油あぶらを入いれる。量りょうは、鶏肉とりにくを入いれたときにかぶるくらいまで。

Pour the frying oil into a deep frying pan: enough to cover the chicken when you put it in.

Step 7

油あぶらを180度どくらいまで熱ねっしたら、中火ちゅうびにして鶏肉とりにくを入いれ、4〜5分ふんかけてからりと揚あげる。

Heat the oil to about 180 °C, turn the heat down to medium, add the chicken, and fry for 4–5 minutes until crisp.

Tip

片栗粉かたくりこがなければ、代かわりに小麦粉こむぎこを使つかってもよい。

If you have no potato starch, you can use wheat flour instead.

Tip

スーパーで売うっている「から揚あげ粉こ」を使つかう場合ばあいは、下味したあじがついているので、手順てじゅん2と3は省しょう略りゃくできる。粉こなを鶏肉とりにくに直ちょく接せつまぶすだけでよい。

If you use the karaage mix sold in supermarkets, it’s already seasoned, so you can skip steps 2 and 3. Just coat the chicken with it directly.

Tip

粉こなをまぶしたら、揚あげる前まえに軽かるくはたいて、余分よぶんな粉こなを落おとした方ほうがいい。

Once it’s coated, it’s better to pat off the excess flour before frying.

Tip

揚あげ時間じかんは肉にくの大おおきさによって変かわる。心配しんぱいなときは、試ためしに一ひとつ切きってみて、中なかまで火ひが通とおっているか確たしかめるとよい。

Frying time depends on the size of the pieces. If you’re not sure, cut one open as a test and check that it’s cooked all the way through.

Host

やっぱり揚あげたてのから揚あげは最高さいこうですね！みなさんも、ぜひおうちで作つくってみてください。

Freshly fried karaage really is the best! Do try making it at home.

2

The chat around it

Natsuko, Naomi and Peter, before and after the recipe

It’s recipe number one, so Natsuko welcomes the cooks among the listeners, and Naomi apologises to the vegetarians: today it’s chicken, 鳥のから揚げ. What comes to mind when you hear から揚げ? For Natsuko, boxed lunches, and the menu at an 居酒屋, a Japanese-style pub. Peter points out a difference: in the US, “fried chicken” suggests big pieces on the bone, but Japanese karaage comes as small, round, boneless pieces. A bit like chicken nuggets, Natsuko says, though Naomi prefers karaage. The theme, Naomi says, is giving instructions.

After the recipe

Everyone is hungry. Listeners in Japan, or near a Japanese food shop, can buy potato starch, Naomi says; if you can’t, wheat flour is still plenty tasty. Natsuko doesn’t deep-fry at home, so rarely makes karaage, but loves eating it.

So where should a visitor to Japan go for good karaage? It’s everywhere, Natsuko says: bento shops (お弁当屋さん) and 居酒屋. Naomi adds stations, even airports — Natsuko’s plan for stepping off the plane: grab a から揚げ弁当 — and convenience stores. And Peter’s tip for the 居酒屋: if you can’t read the menu, just order karaage, because they’re bound to have it.

Key lines

The lesson’s own lines that carry its patterns

Five lines from the lesson’s recipe, quoted exactly for study. The English is my own rendering.

3で作つくったたれに鶏肉とりにくを入いれて、味あじがしみこむように軽かるくもむ。味あじがしみこむまで、約やく30分間ぷんかんおく。

Put the chicken in the sauce made in step 3 and rub it lightly so the flavour soaks in. Leave it for about 30 minutes, until the flavour has soaked in. — step 4

Grammar: 〜ように, “so that”, the ように of UB lesson 1; 〜まで, “until”; おく, “leave it, let it stand”, here a verb in its own right. Every verb is plain: もむ, おく.

ビニール袋ぶくろに片栗粉かたくりこを入いれ、そこに鶏肉とりにくを入いれて、まんべんなく片栗粉かたくりこをまぶす。

Put the potato starch in a plastic bag, put the chicken in it, and coat the chicken evenly with the starch. — step 5

Grammar: 入れ、 is the stem of 入れる used as “and”, the written partner of 入れて (section 6); そこに, “into it”; まんべんなく, “evenly”; まぶす, “coat, dust”.

油あぶらの温度おんどが約やく180度どになったら、中火ちゅうびにし、鶏肉とりにくを入いれて、4〜5分ふん程度ていど揚あげる。

When the oil reaches about 180 degrees, turn the heat to medium, put the chicken in, and fry for about 4–5 minutes. — step 7

Grammar: 〜になったら, “once it reaches”; 中火にし = 中火にして, “set it to medium heat”; 約 before a number and 程度 after it both mean “about”.

片栗粉かたくりこが手てに入はいらない場合ばあいは、小麦粉こむぎこでもよい。

If you can’t get potato starch, wheat flour will do. — a tip

Grammar: 手に入る, “to get hold of”; 〜場合は, “if, in cases where”; 〜でもよい, “… is fine too”.

揚あげる時間じかんは、肉にくの大おおきさによるので、不安ふあんなときは、中なかまで火ひが通とおっているか、試ためしに切きって確認かくにんしてみるとよい。

The frying time depends on the size of the meat, so if you’re unsure, it’s a good idea to cut a piece as a test and check whether it’s cooked all the way through. — a tip

Grammar: 〜による, “depends on”; 火が通る, “to be cooked through”; 〜か marks an embedded question, “whether”; 試しに, “as a test”; 〜てみるとよい, “it’s a good idea to try…”.

4

Vocabulary

The lesson’s word list, in its order

The hosts’ notes first, then a card for each word with an example of my own.

  • 材料 — ingredients, or materials in general: 材料は何ですか？
  • 片栗粉 — potato starch. 片栗 (katakuri) is a plant, the “dogtooth violet”, Naomi says, and 粉, read こ here, is powder or flour. So it’s literally “katakuri powder”, but real katakuri starch is now very expensive, so the powder is made from potatoes instead. About 80% potato starch, Natsuko has heard. Is katakuri native to Japan? Natsuko isn’t sure, but you see a lot of it in the mountains. Section 8 has the answer.
  • 小麦粉 — wheat flour: 小麦, wheat, plus 粉 again. The lesson’s example: 片栗粉の代わりに、小麦粉を使いました, “I used wheat flour instead of potato starch.”
  • から揚げ粉 — a ready-made flour mix just for karaage, sold in Japanese supermarkets. Naomi’s mother uses it a lot.
  • 鶏もも肉 — 鶏, chicken; もも, thigh; 肉, meat. Not a whole leg on the bone: it’s the big piece of meat, sold boneless. With the bone, it’s 骨付きもも肉; the lower leg is sometimes sold as ドラムスティック.
  • にんにく, garlic, and しょうが, ginger.
  • たれ — a sauce, for dipping or basting. You’ll be asked at yakitori shops: たれにしますか？塩にしますか？, “sauce or salt?”
  • 一口大 — bite-size: 一口 is one bite, and 大 here means size, 大きさ. “One mouthful’s size”.
  • 一かけ — a piece, a clove, a knob; originally 一かけら, where かけら is a fragment, a scrap, a shard. The lesson’s example: チョコレートを、一かけ食べました, “I ate a piece of chocolate.”
  • まんべんなく — an adverb, “evenly, all over”: 壁をまんべんなく塗ってください, “please paint the wall evenly.”
  • 中火 — medium heat; low heat is 弱火, high heat 強火: 強火から中火にして下さい, “turn it down from high to medium.” In the kitchen, Peter says, kanji are extremely useful: even if you can’t read a word aloud, knowing what its kanji mean will get you through.

Watch for“Thigh” is the usual translation, and Japan’s farm ministry uses it too, but a boneless pack of 鶏もも肉 is really the whole leg taken off the bone, drumstick meat included. On the bone, the whole leg is 骨付きもも. And 手羽元, which looks like a small drumstick, is part of the wing.

Spoons and cups

The lesson’s warning about measurements

The lesson translates 大さじ as “tablespoon” and 小さじ as “teaspoon”, but Naomi warns that the sizes may not match yours: in Japan, 大さじ is 15 ml and 小さじ is 5 ml. Other countries use different sizes, Natsuko adds. And the scariest, says Naomi, is the cup. So check what a recipe’s measures mean before you start.

Beyond the lessonNaomi is right to fear the cup. A Japanese measuring cup, 1カップ, holds 200 ml; a US cup is about 237 ml (240 ml on American food labels), and the metric cup used in Australia and elsewhere holds 250 ml. Rice has a cup of its own: one 合, about 180 ml, the size of the cup that comes with a rice cooker. Even spoons differ: an Australian tablespoon holds 20 ml.

MeasureHow much
大おおさじ115 ml
小こさじ15 ml
1カップ200 ml
1合ごうabout 180 ml, for rice
少しょう々しょうa pinch between thumb and forefinger: about ⅛ teaspoon, 0.6 g of salt
ひとつまみa three-finger pinch: about ⅕ teaspoon, 1 g of salt
適てき量りょうthe right amount: put some in, and judge how much
適宜てきぎas you see fit; sometimes it can be left out

Real pinches tend to be smaller: when one study weighed people’s actual pinches, the averages were just 0.2 g and 0.5 g. The 杯 in 大さじ1杯 is the counter for spoonfuls and cupfuls, and ingredient lists often drop it: 大さじ1. Half a spoon is written 大さじ1/2 and read 大さじ半分 or 大さじ2分の1. And temperatures are Celsius: the recipe’s 180度 is about 356°F.

6

Instructions, plain form

The lesson’s focus: how written instructions sound

There’s no grammar point as such, Naomi says; the theme is reading instructions in Japanese. The one thing to notice: instructions are usually written in the plain non-past form. Today it happens to be a recipe, but computer manuals and game instructions are written the same way, so it pays to get used to it.

Beyond the lessonWhat “plain non-past” means in practice: the dictionary form, with no ます and no ください. 切る, 混ぜる, 揚げる read like English instructions: “cut”, “mix”, “fry”. A cooking show says the same things politely, 切ります or 切ってください; a written recipe doesn’t need to. Notice よい where speech says いい: the same word, in its written form.

Written instructions also link their steps in a way speech rarely does. The verb’s stem, the form before ます, does the job of the て-form, “and”: 入れ、 = 入れて、, and for する, し、 = して、, as in 中火にし、. You’ll meet it in every manual, news story and set of rules.

電源でんげんボタンを押おし、画面がめんが明あかるくなったら指ゆびを離はなす。

Press the power button, and when the screen lights up, let go.

8

から揚げ and 片栗粉

Beyond the lesson: the name, the cousins, and the hosts’ open question

Beyond the lessonThe answer to the hosts’ question. Katakuri, Erythronium japonicum, is native to Japan, from Hokkaido to Kyushu, though rare in Shikoku and Kyushu. It also grows in Korea, north-east China, Sakhalin and the Kuril Islands. It’s a spring ephemeral of moist woodland floors: it flowers in early spring, before the trees come into leaf, then dies back. “Dogtooth violet” is the English name for its whole genus; Japan’s tourism agency calls this species the Asian fawnlily.

From katakuri to potato. Starch from katakuri bulbs was so prized that the plant was over-harvested, and by the end of the Edo period it had become scarce. From the Meiji era on, starch from potatoes grown in Hokkaido, which behaves much the same in cooking, took its place. Real katakuri starch is hardly produced any more.

Watch forNatsuko’s “about 80%” undersells it. Japan’s agricultural agency says almost all 片栗粉 on sale today is potato starch, and an encyclopedia adds that some is cornstarch. Only the name stayed.

The name, and some cousins

Dictionaries disagree about the から. One reading is 唐揚げ, “Chinese-style frying”, with 唐 meaning China in general. The other is 空揚げ, “empty frying”: food fried with no batter, at most a dusting of flour or starch. デジタル大辞泉 files it under 空揚げ and notes the 唐揚げ spelling; other dictionaries keep 唐揚げ for the Chinese-style kind.

  • 竜田揚げ — marinated in soy sauce and mirin, then coated in 片栗粉 or flour. Its red-brown colour is likened to the autumn maples of the Tatsuta River, famous in classical poetry.
  • ザンギ — Hokkaido’s name for karaage, with a more strongly seasoned marinade. The farm ministry traces it to a chicken restaurant in Kushiro around 1955.
  • 中津からあげ — Nakatsu, in Ōita, has eaten karaage widely since around 1970, and the name is a registered regional trademark.

Karaage is serious business. The Japan Karaage Association, 日本唐揚協会, was founded in October 2008 and has run a karaage grand prix, the からあげグランプリ, since 2010. As for Naomi’s convenience stores: Lawson’s からあげクン, small pieces of chicken breast, has been on sale since 15 April 1986.

Frying it right

Japanese frying guides sort oil by temperature. 中温, medium, is about 170–180℃: dip dry chopsticks in and fine bubbles rise all along them, or drop in a little batter and it sinks halfway before floating back up. That’s the range for karaage; Nisshin OilliO suggests 170℃ for 5–6 minutes, so the lesson’s 180℃ is at the top end. 高温, 180–190℃, is for crisp finishes and 二度揚げ, frying twice: fry, let it rest, then fry again, while the heat left in each piece finishes cooking the inside.

から揚あげは、二度揚にどあげすると、外そとはカリッと、中なかはジューシーに仕上しあがる。

Fry karaage twice and it comes out crisp outside and juicy inside.

Watch forTwo safety notes from Japan’s ministries. Chicken can carry Campylobacter, so the health ministry advises heating it until the centre reaches 75℃ or more for at least a minute; the lesson’s “cut one open to check” tip is there for exactly this. And the farm ministry says don’t wash raw chicken: the water splashes the bacteria around your sink.

Quiz

Pick the word that fits. Each answer comes with the reason.

1. 玉たまねぎとにんじんを＿＿＿に切きる。

Cut the onion and carrot into bite-size pieces.

一口大: bite-size. 一かけ is a piece of garlic, ginger or chocolate.

2. にんにく＿＿＿をすりおろす。

Grate one clove of garlic.

一かけ: one piece, here a clove. 大さじ would need a number: 大さじ1.

3. 焦こげないように、＿＿＿でゆっくり焼やく。

Cook it slowly over low heat so it doesn’t burn.

弱火: low heat. Slowly, without burning, means low; 強火 is high heat.

4. 揚あげる前まえに、鶏肉とりにくに片栗粉かたくりこを＿＿＿。

Before frying, coat the chicken with potato starch.

まぶす: coat or dust all over. すりおろす is to grate; しみこむ is what a flavour does, soaking in.

5. しょうがを＿＿＿、スープに入いれる。

Grate the ginger and add it to the soup.

すりおろす: grate. You coat (まぶす) with a powder, and pat off (はたく) the extra.

6. 玉たまねぎが茶色ちゃいろになる＿＿＿、中火ちゅうびで炒いためる。

Fry the onion over medium heat until it turns brown.

〜まで: until.

7. バターがない＿＿＿、サラダ油あぶらを使つかってください。

If you don’t have butter, use vegetable oil.

〜場合は: if, in cases where. ように would mean “so that”.

8. 生なまクリームがなければ、牛ぎゅう乳にゅう＿＿＿。

If you don’t have cream, milk will do.

A noun + でもよい: “… will do”. ばよい and た方がよい follow verbs: 入れればよい, 入れた方がよい.

9. 焼やき時間じかんは、オーブンに＿＿＿。

Baking time depends on your oven.

〜による: depends on.

10. ボウルに卵たまごを＿＿＿、よく混まぜる。

Break the eggs into a bowl and mix well.

In writing, a verb’s stem works as “and”: 割り、混ぜる = 割って混ぜる (section 6).
