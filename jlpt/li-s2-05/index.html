Have a taste before you go.

A new story begins. Wasabi, a high-school student with a part-time job at a sushi bar, hates wasabi, and one morning the boss finds every bit of the shop’s wasabi gone, swapped for bright yellow mustard. The grammar point is 〜ていく, “do something, then go”, as in the boss’s 試食していきな, “have a taste before you go”. The boss also gives a free lesson in rough, casual speech.

The dialogue

The whole scene, retold for review

The lesson’s dialogue, retold in my own wording so you can review it in one place. It keeps the same scene, characters, words and grammar, but every line is rephrased, the wasabi goes missing from the fridge, and the last four lines are new. The lesson’s exact key lines are in section 3. Hide the English with the button at the top to test yourself.

Scene

A small sushi bar, an hour before opening. Wasabi, a high-school student who works there part-time, arrives for work.

Wasabi

大たい将しょう、おはようございまーす！

Morning, boss!

Boss

おう、わさびちゃん、来きたな。今日きょうもしっかり頼たのむぞ！

Hey, Wasabi, there you are. I’m counting on you again today!

Boss

あ、そうだ。ゆうべ、たこわさを仕込しこんだんだ。着替きがえに行いく前まえに、ちょっと試食ししょくしていきな。

Oh, that reminds me. I made up some tako-wasa last night. Have a little taste before you go and get changed.

Wasabi

あ……すみません。私わたし、わさびだけは大嫌だいきらいなんです。

Oh… sorry. Wasabi is the one thing I really hate.

Boss

あー、そっかそっか。名前なまえはわさびなのに、わさびは大嫌だいきらいなんだったな！すまんすまん！

Ah, right, right. Your name’s Wasabi, but you hate the stuff! Sorry, sorry!

Scene

A little later, a shout from the kitchen.

Boss

うわっ、なんじゃこりゃあ！

Whoa, what the heck is this?!

Wasabi

大たい将しょう、どうしたんですか！？

Boss, what’s wrong?!

Boss

冷れい蔵ぞう庫このわさびが、一本いっぽん残のこらず無なくなってる……。代かわりに入はいってるのは、真まっ黄色きいろいからしばっかりだ……。

The wasabi in the fridge is gone, every last root… All that’s in there instead is bright yellow mustard…

Boss

寿司屋すしやが、からしで寿司すしなんか握にぎれっか……。開店かいてんまであと一いち時じ間かんだってのに、どうすりゃいいんだ、ちくしょう！

A sushi bar, making sushi with mustard? As if… We open in an hour, and what am I supposed to do? Damn it!

Wasabi

私わたし、スーパーまで走はしっていって、わさび買かってきましょうか？

Shall I run down to the supermarket and get some wasabi?

Boss

チューブのわさびなんか使つかえっか！うちは本ほんわさびしか使つかわねえんだ。

As if I’d use wasabi out of a tube! We use nothing but the real thing here.

Wasabi

じゃあ今日きょうは、全部ぜんぶさび抜ぬきで出だすしかないですね！

Then today we’ll just have to serve everything without wasabi!

Boss

……わさびちゃん。お前まえ、なんだか嬉うれしそうだな。

…Wasabi. You look awfully pleased about this.

2

The scene

Natsuko, Naomi and Peter, before and after the dialogue

Peter opens with the title, “Wasabi Kunoichi, Part 1”. A くのいち, Natsuko explains, is a female ninja. Naomi has a trick: write a hiragana く, a katakana ノ and a kanji 一 on top of each other, and you get 女, “woman”. A good way to remember the kanji, Natsuko says.

The heroine (主人公) is Wasabi, わさびちゃん. Not a normal name, the hosts agree: wasabi is the Japanese horseradish every sushi lover knows, green and with a kick that goes straight up your nose (鼻にツンとくる). Wasabi is a high-school student with a part-time job at a sushi bar. Did they even have high schools back then, Peter asks? It isn’t a period piece, Naomi says: it’s set today. Whether ninjas still exist is another question; perhaps only in the writer’s head. The grammar point is 〜ていく.

The conversation is between Wasabi and the head of the sushi bar, and people call the head of a sushi bar 大将. Not so much the owner, Natsuko says, as the one running the place (店長), and you hear it at yakitori bars and ramen shops too: Japanese-style places. Peter knew 店長 and マスター, but had never heard 大将. It isn’t for calling the staff, Natsuko explains, but for the most senior person, and people say it when chatting across the counter. Naomi has heard others use it, but never tried it. Can you? If others in the shop do, Natsuko says, go ahead: what the boss likes to be called varies from shop to shop. Is it only for regulars (常連客), Peter wonders? It depends on the shop, and on the customer’s manner: something like “Hey, boss!” in English. And 大将 originally means “general”. So, Peter sums up: a general and a female ninja.

Beyond the lessonくノ一. The dictionary backs Naomi up: the word is 女 split into く, ノ and 一, and it means a female ninja, or simply a woman. The plain “woman” came first: in the Edo period it was a coded word for women, and the ninja meaning spread through fiction from the 1960s, above all through Yamada Fūtarō’s ninja novels.

大将 is the army’s top rank, general (in the navy, admiral). Dictionaries also give it as a friendly or teasing way to hail a man, よう大将、元気か, and as what people call the master of a small workshop or shop, a use recorded as early as 1836. The hosts’ お山の大将 is in section 4.

After the dialogue

Does Natsuko like wasabi? Quite a lot. Natsuko’s trick (裏技): a little wasabi in pasta sauce, and it goes especially well with a creamy one. Naomi is astonished. Peter can’t picture it at first, since pasta brings tomato sauce to mind, but a white cream sauce, yes. Naomi had assumed Natsuko meant soy-sauce pasta, which would probably be good too, and promises to try it. Peter often has wasabi with beef: some people eat horseradish with steak, Naomi points out, so wasabi in its place makes sense. Which reminds the hosts of わさビーフ, a wasabi potato chip with a beef flavour, and from there of wasabi ice cream, wasabi beer, wasabi rice crackers (せんべい) and wasabi mayonnaise. If you spot them, the hosts say, try them.

Phrases worth taking from the chat:

  • これちょっと裏技なんですけど — “this is a bit of a secret trick, but…”.
  • 試してみて下さい — “do give it a try”: 〜てみる, “try doing”.
  • トマトソースが頭に浮かんでくる — “tomato sauce comes to mind”: 浮かぶ plus てくる, the partner of today’s grammar (section 6).
  • 相性いいですよね — “they go well together”.
  • 見かけたら是非試してみて下さい — “if you come across them, do try them”.

Key lines

The boss’s three lines that carry the grammar and the new words

Three of the dialogue’s nine lines, quoted exactly; section 1 covers the rest in my retelling. The English is my own rendering.

あ、そうだそうだ、この前まえ、たこわさを作つくったんだけど、仕事しごとに行いく前まえにちょっと試食ししょくしていきなよ。

Oh, right, right: I made some tako-wasa the other day, so have a little taste before you start work. — the boss

Grammar: そうだそうだ, “oh, right”, remembering something; この前, “the other day”; 〜んだけど, setting the scene for a suggestion; 仕事に行く前に, dictionary form + 前に, “before doing”; 試食していきな, today’s 〜ていく (section 5), with な as a casual command, short for 〜なさい.

わさびが全部ぜんぶ無なくなってる……代かわりに全部ぜんぶ、真まっ黄色きいろいからしになってるぞ……

All the wasabi’s gone… and instead it’s all turned into bright yellow mustard… — the boss

Grammar: 無くなってる, “has gone”, with 〜ている shortened to 〜てる; 代わりに, “instead”; 真っ黄色い, “bright yellow” (section 4); からしになってる, “has turned into mustard”; ぞ, a rough, emphatic ending (section 7).

寿司すしにからしなんか、入いれられっか……どうすりゃ良いいんだ、ちくしょー！

As if I could put mustard in sushi… What am I supposed to do? Damn it! — the boss

Grammar: からしなんか, “something like mustard”, with なんか brushing it aside; 入れられっか = 入れられるか, a rhetorical “as if I could”; どうすりゃ = どうすれば; ちくしょー, “damn it”. All unpacked in section 7.

Vocabulary

The lesson’s word list, in its order

The hosts’ notes first, then a card for each word, plus a few more from the introduction and the chat, each with an example of my own.

  • 寿司屋 (すしや) — sushi restaurant. In conversation it’s often dressed up with お in front and さん behind: お寿司屋さん. The lesson’s example: 寿司屋でランチを食べました, “I had lunch at a sushi restaurant.”
  • 大将 (たいしょう) — general, admiral, boss. To Peter it’s like walking into a New York shop and saying “Hey, boss!”, which surprises Natsuko and Naomi: do people really say that? Some do. Natsuko adds an expression: お山の大将, someone who throws their weight around (いばってる人), or “king of the hill”, as Naomi puts it. お山 is just 山, mountain, with the polite お.
  • たこわさ — octopus with wasabi, which all three love. Peter and Naomi call it たこわさび; Natsuko says たこわさ. It may depend on the menu, they decide, since some shops write one and some the other, and Peter suspects たこわさ belongs to friendly yakitori places with handwritten menus rather than chains like Watami. Worth researching, Natsuko says. たこ is octopus, and わさ is short for わさび.
  • 試食 (ししょく) — sampling food. 試 is the kanji of 試す, “to try, to test”, and 食 that of 食べる, so: test-eating. Try a drink and it’s 試飲 (しいん), a word Peter had never heard: いん is the Chinese reading of 飲む. Try on clothes and it’s 試着 (しちゃく); a fitting room is a 試着室. All three take する. Naomi’s supermarket line: みかんいかがですか？試食して下さい。おいしいですよ. It’s a little formal, Peter notes: with friends you’d say 食べてみる.
  • そっか — oh, I see. そうですか is polite; shorten it to そうか and then そっか, and it gets more and more casual. You can double it, too: そっかそっか.
  • すまん — sorry, very casually. Men use it a lot, Naomi says; Natsuko uses it now and then with friends, and in emails as a jokey apology: すまんすまん. Swap the ん for ぬ and you get すまぬ, which sounds like a samurai film. Peter loves すまぬ.
  • 代わりに — instead of: やせたいので、ジュースのかわりに水を飲む, “I want to lose weight, so I drink water instead of juice.” It took Peter a long time to get this one: it’s noun + の + かわりに, the reverse of the English order. Mind the order, Natsuko says.
  • 真っ黄色い (まっきいろい) — bright yellow. 黄色 is yellow, and 真 means genuine, true, so 真っ makes it really, purely that colour: まっしろ, pure white; まっくろ, pitch black. Not every colour takes it: nobody says まむらさき. Just the basic colours, Naomi thinks.
  • からし — mustard, though not quite the same thing as マスタード, which Japanese also uses: they’re made differently, Naomi has read. からし goes with Japanese food, and Chinese food; マスタード is the Western kind. Ask for からし at McDonald’s and you’d be understood, but you’d sound like somebody’s grandfather: マスタード is natural there. からし is about twice as hot, Peter says, and マスタード a little sour, Naomi adds, so a hot dog smothered in からし would be fiery. When Peter proposes hot dogs for lunch, Naomi promises to load Peter’s hamburger steak with からし instead. “Hot hot dogs,” says Natsuko.

Beyond the lessonお山の大将 began as a children’s game: whoever reaches the top of a mound shouts お山の大将おれ一人, “I’m the one and only king of the hill”, and pushes the others off. By the 1820s it already meant someone who lords it over a small world, and in 1921 the poet Saijō Yaso (西条八十) wrote the shout into a song for children.

すまん and すまぬ are both 済まない, literally “it isn’t settled”, the root of すみません, with older negative endings: ぬ is the classical one, and ん grew out of it. That’s why すまぬ sounds like a samurai. More on ぬ in Upper Intermediate S4 #2.

The 真っ colours can change their sound as well as their strength: 真っ赤 is まっか, bright red, and 真っ青 is まっさお, deep blue, or white as a sheet. Both readings are on the official list of special readings. Dictionaries give 真っ黄色 as a noun, used as 真っ黄色な or 真っ黄色の. The boss’s 真っ黄色い follows the pattern of 真っ白い and 真っ黒い and is heard in speech, but dictionaries don’t list it, so in writing, use the noun.

5

〜ていく

The grammar point: do something, then go

いく on its own means “to go”. After a て-form it has two meanings, Peter explains, and this lesson covers one: do something and then go. In the dialogue: 仕事に行く前にちょっと試食していきなよ, “before you go to work, have a taste, then go.” That’s a command, so to make it easier, the hosts change it to the plain 試食していく and take it apart:

1

仕事に行く前に

Before going to work. The verb before 前 is in the plain non-past: 行く前に, before you do it, as in 食べる前, “before eating”.

2

ちょっと試食して

ちょっと, a little; 試食, try the food: try a little something.

3

いく

And then go: do this, then 行く. Depending on the context, Peter adds, the meaning can change, and the lesson notes have the full story.

The lesson notes fill in the rest. 〜ていく is the て-form + いく, and its first use (the one in this lesson) is for moving away from the speaker, or from the speaker’s point of view, after or while doing something. Its second use, an action or a change that carries on, waits for part 3 of the story. The notes sort the first use into three kinds of verb:

Kind of verb〜ていく
An action食たべていく, eat before you go; 休やすんでいく, rest before you go
A way of moving走はしっていく, go running; 歩あるいていく, go on foot
Putting something on着きていく, wear (clothes) to go out; はいていく, wear (shoes or trousers)

The boss’s 〜ていきな is 〜ていきなさい cut short: a friendly, casual order from someone older, “go on, have some before you go”. Politely, you’d invite someone with 〜ていきませんか or 〜ていってください.

雨あめがやむまで、お茶ちゃでも飲のんでいきませんか。

Won’t you stay for a cup of tea until the rain stops?

帰かえりに、本屋ほんやに寄よっていこう。

Let’s drop into the bookshop on the way home.

子こどもたちは、校こう庭ていへ走はしっていった。

The children ran off to the playground.

明日あしたの面めん接せつには、スーツを着きていくつもりだ。

I’m going to wear a suit to tomorrow’s interview.

雨あめが降ふりそうだから、傘かさを持もっていきなさい。

It looks like rain, so take an umbrella with you.

Watch forTwo なs that mean opposite things. After the ます-stem, な is a casual “go on”: 食べな, “eat up”; 試食していきな, “have a taste before you go”. After the dictionary form, it’s “don’t”: 食べるな, “don’t eat that”; 行くな, “don’t go”.

Going and coming

Beyond the lesson: 〜ていく and its partner 〜てくる

Beyond the lesson〜ていく points away from where the speaker is. Its partner, 〜てくる, points the other way: towards the speaker, or there and back again. Most everyday verbs of taking, bringing and moving come in pairs.

〜ていく · away〜てくる · this way
持もっていく
take it with you		持もってくる
bring it here
連つれていく
take someone along		連つれてくる
bring someone here
走はしっていく
run off		走はしってくる
come running
買かっていく
buy it to take with you		買かってくる
go and buy it, and come back
  • 行ってきます, what you say as you leave home, is てくる too: “I’ll go, and come back.” Retold Wasabi’s 走っていって、わさび買ってきましょうか has both: run off there, buy it, come back.
  • English “bring” isn’t always 持ってくる. Japanese measures from where you are now. “I’ll bring drinks to your party” is 飲み物を持っていきます, since the drinks are going away from you; 持ってきます would mean bringing them to where you are now.
  • The second meaning, coming in part 3: with verbs of change, 〜ていく means a change carrying on from now, and 〜てくる a change that has built up until now: これから寒くなっていく, “it’ll keep getting colder from here”, but 最近寒くなってきた, “it’s been getting colder lately”. Peter’s 頭に浮かんでくる, “it comes to mind”, has the same てくる: something rising up towards you.

ちょっとコンビニでアイス買かってくるね。

I’m just popping out to the convenience store for an ice cream.

犬いぬが、しっぽを振ふりながら走はしってきた。

The dog came running up, wagging its tail.

日本語にほんごが、だんだん分わかってきた。

Japanese has gradually started to make sense to me.

7

The boss’s rough talk

Beyond the lesson: what the boss says, in plain Japanese

Beyond the lessonWasabi speaks to the boss politely, with です and ます. The boss talks the way a gruff, old-school shop owner talks in Japanese fiction: blunt, clipped, and warm underneath. Here is every rough form in the dialogue, unpacked.

  • Warmth. おう is a gruff “hey”, or “yeah”. 今日もよろしく頼むよ is a boss’s casual version of よろしくお願いします: “I’m counting on you today too.” Saying it twice keeps it light: そうだそうだ, “oh right, right”; そっかそっか, “oh, I see, I see”; すまんすまん, “sorry, sorry”.
  • Endings. 〜な after the ます-stem is a casual order, from 〜なさい: 試食していきな. 〜んだな is realising something: 大嫌いだったんだな, “so you hate it, right”. 〜ぞ is a rough, emphatic “I tell you”: からしになってるぞ.
  • Squashed words. なんじゃこりゃ is なんだこれは, with じゃ for だ and こりゃ for これは. 無くなってる is 無くなっている. 入れられっか is 入れられるか, a question that means “as if I could!”; the retold boss says 握れっか and 使えっか. どうすりゃ is どうすれば. And ちくしょう, written 畜生, “beast”, is a mild “damn it”.
  • In the retelling, too: 使わねえ is 使わない, with ない roughened to ねえ; だってのに is だというのに, “and yet”; ばっかり is ばかり, “nothing but”; and お前 is a rough “you”, fine from a boss to a young worker, rude almost anywhere else.

Beyond the lessonなんじゃこりゃあ！ is also one of the most famous lines on Japanese television. In the police drama 太陽にほえろ！, Matsuda Yūsaku’s young detective, nicknamed Jeans (ジーパン), is shot and dies crying なんじゃこりゃあ！, in episode 111, broadcast on 30 August 1974. The line wasn’t in the script: Matsuda ad-libbed it. So when the boss finds the mustard, the line comes with a famous echo.

Watch forGood to understand, risky to copy. From a learner, the boss’s Japanese sounds rude, or like a comedy routine. Wasabi’s polite replies are the model for talking to anyone above you.

8

Wasabi and からし

Beyond the lesson: real wasabi, tube wasabi, and why からし isn’t mustard

Beyond the lessonReal wasabi and the tube kind. 本わさび, real wasabi (Wasabia japonica), is native to Japan; good sushi bars grate the root fresh. 西洋わさび, “Western wasabi”, is horseradish, which comes from eastern Europe; both belong to the cabbage family. Most packet and tube wasabi mixes the two: the powder is mainly dried horseradish, and the paste is powdered real wasabi plus horseradish. The makers’ association has a labelling rule for it: 本わさび使用 means real wasabi is at least half of the wasabi inside, and 本わさび入り means less than half. Hence the retold boss’s scorn for the tube.

Where it’s grown. Wasabi farming began about 400 years ago, in the Keichō era (1596–1615), in the mountains of what is now Shizuoka City. In 2018 the UN’s Food and Agriculture Organization named Shizuoka’s traditional way of growing it, in terraced beds fed by running spring water, a Globally Important Agricultural Heritage System.

からし or マスタード? The hosts are right on both counts. Japanese からし is made mainly from oriental mustard seed, whose heat comes from a very volatile oil: sharp, and straight up the nose (ツーンとする). Western マスタード is made mainly from white, or yellow, mustard, whose oil is gentler, and it usually has vinegar and seasonings added: the sourness Naomi noticed. Neither powder is hot until it meets water, which sets off the reaction that makes the heat.

さび抜き. At a sushi bar, wasabi is often just さび, so sushi made without it is さび抜き: what Wasabi would order, and what children usually get. Conveyor-belt chains such as Kura Sushi make all their sushi さび抜き and leave you to add the wasabi yourself.

Words from behind the counter. Sushi bars have their own names for things: なみだ, “tears”, for grated wasabi, because it makes your eyes water; ガリ for the pickled ginger; シャリ for the rice, named after 舎利, the Buddha’s relics; あがり, short for 上がり花, for freshly made tea; and むらさき, “purple”, another name for soy sauce. おあいそ, the bill, was originally the shop’s own word, from an apology, 愛想がなくてすみません, “sorry we weren’t more attentive”, so etiquette writers advise customers to ask with お会計お願いします instead.

わさビーフ, the chip the hosts remember, was launched by the snack maker 山芳製菓 in September 1987: a potato chip with a kick of wasabi and the taste of beef.

Quiz

Pick the word or form that fits. Each answer comes with the reason.

1. （出でかける子こどもに）寒さむいから、コートを＿＿＿。

(To a child going out) It’s cold, so wear your coat.

The child is leaving you: ていく, away from the speaker. 着てきなさい would be “come here wearing it”.

2. 時間じかんがあるなら、晩ばんご飯はんを＿＿＿よ。

If you’ve got time, stay and have dinner before you go.

〜ていきな: “have it here, then go”, the boss’s casual offer. 食べるな means “don’t eat”.

3. 駅えきまでは、いつも＿＿＿。

I always walk to the station.

Going away from where you are now: 歩いていく, “go on foot”. 歩いてくる would be coming here on foot.

4. 帰かえりに、コンビニで牛ぎゅう乳にゅうを＿＿＿くれる？

Could you pick up some milk at the convenience store on your way back?

Buy it and bring it back here: てくる. 買っていく is buying something to take away with you.

5. 明日あしたのパーティーには、飲のみ物ものを＿＿＿。

I’ll bring drinks to tomorrow’s party.

From where you are now to the party is away from you: 持っていく, even where English says “bring”.

6. 今日きょうは、パン＿＿＿ご飯はんを食たべた。

Today I had rice instead of bread.

Noun + の代わりに. The thing you didn’t have comes first: the reverse of English.

7. 「明日あした、雨あめだって。」「＿＿＿、じゃあ試合しあいは中止ちゅうしかな。」

“They say it’ll rain tomorrow.” “Oh, right. Then maybe the match is off.”

そっか: oh, I see. Casual for そうですか.

8. 「おい、俺おれのケーキ食たべただろう！」「＿＿＿、おいしそうだったから。」

“Hey, you ate my cake, didn’t you!” “Sorry, it looked so good.”

すまん: a very casual “sorry”, between friends.

9. 恥はずかしくて、顔かおが＿＿＿になった。

I was so embarrassed my face went bright red.

Embarrassment turns your face 真っ赤, bright red. Going pale with fear is 真っ青, まっさお.

10. 酒蔵さかぐらで、新酒しんしゅを＿＿＿させてもらった。

At the brewery, they let me taste the new sake.

試飲: tasting a drink. 試食 is for food, and 試着 for clothes.

11. このズボン、＿＿＿してもいいですか。

May I try these trousers on?

試着: trying clothes on, in a 試着室, a fitting room.

12. おでんには、＿＿＿をつけて食たべる。

I eat oden with hot Japanese mustard.

Japanese dishes like oden take からし. マスタード is for hot dogs and Western food; ガリ is sushi ginger.

13. 危あぶないから、その水みずは＿＿＿！

It’s dangerous, so don’t drink that water!

Dictionary form + な is “don’t”: 飲むな. Stem + な is a casual “go on”: 飲みな.

14. 「どうすりゃいいんだ」は、普通ふつうに言いうと「どう＿＿＿いいんだ」。

Unpacked, どうすりゃいいんだ is “どう＿＿＿いいんだ”: what am I supposed to do?

〜りゃ is a rough contraction of 〜れば: すれば → すりゃ.
