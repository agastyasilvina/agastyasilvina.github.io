One friend saves ¥20,000 a month. The other takes out the lot.

Part 3 of “Mo’ Money, Mo’ Problems”, and out of the bank. Taiga touts for customers in the street, hears that a friend saves ¥20,000 a month, then empties an account at the ATM for dinner with a generous lawyer. The grammar points are なんか, which plays yourself down (俺なんか) or waves something away, and ちょっと, which makes an errand sound quick. Plus what the dictionaries say about なんか and ちょっと, what the law says about touting and the 2025 rules for host clubs, and how paydays, ATMs and transfers have changed since 2008.

Scene

A busy street at night. Taiga is out with a friend, Seiya.

Taiga

ねえねえ、お姉ねえさんたち！今いま、時間じかんある？これからうちの店みせ、来こない？

Hey, ladies! Got a minute? Fancy coming to our club?

Women

ごめん、今いま急いそいでるの。

Sorry, we’re in a hurry.

Taiga

ちぇっ、だめか。今月こんげつピンチだから、お客きゃくさん一人ひとりでも連つれて行いきたいんだけどなあ。

Tch, no luck. Money’s tight this month, so I really want to bring in at least one customer.

Seiya

俺おれ、最近さいきんお金かね使つかいすぎだから、積つみ立たて用ようの口座こうざを別べつに作つくったんだ。

I’ve been spending too much lately, so I opened a separate account for saving.

Seiya

給きゅう料りょうが入はいったら、毎月まいつき2万円まんえんずつそっちに積つみ立たててる。

When my pay comes in, ¥20,000 goes into it, every month.

Taiga

へえ、えらいなあ。感心かんしん感心かんしん。

Wow, good for you. Very impressive.

Taiga

俺おれなんか、月末げつまつになると毎回まいかいスッカラカンだよ。

Me, I’m flat broke by the end of every month.

Taiga

あ、ちょっとお金かねおろしてくるから、そこのATM寄よってもいい？

Oh, I just need to take some money out. Mind if we stop at that ATM?

Sound

The ATM beeps.

Seiya

え、待まって。残高ざんだか、2万円まんえんしかないじゃん！給きゅう料りょう日びまで、まだ2週しゅう間かんもあるのに。

Hang on, your balance is only ¥20,000! And there are still two weeks to go till payday.

Seiya

えっ、全額ぜんがくおろしちゃうの？本気ほんきで？

What, you’re taking out the whole lot? Seriously?

Taiga

うん。今夜こんやはハツコさんとディナーなんだ。

Yep. I’m having dinner with Hatsuko tonight.

Seiya

ハツコさんって……あの弁護士べんごしの人ひと？

Hatsuko… the lawyer?

Taiga

そう。一緒いっしょにごはん食たべると、あとで大金たいきんを振ふり込こんでくれるんだよね。

That’s the one. Whenever we eat together, a big transfer comes my way afterwards.

Seiya

えっ、なんで？

Huh? Why?

Taiga

なんでって、決きまってるじゃん。俺おれのこと愛あいしてるからでしょ。

Why? Isn’t it obvious? Because Hatsuko loves me.

Seiya

……大河たいが、時間じかんある？ちょっと話はなしがあるんだけど。

…Taiga, got a minute? There’s something I’d like a quick word about.

Taiga

ごめん、今いまちょっと急いそいでるから！また今度こんどね！

Sorry, I’m in a bit of a hurry! Some other time!

The scene

Naomi, Yuichi and Peter, before and after the dialogue

“More Money, More Problems”, part 3, and still useful expressions about the bank, Naomi says, though we’re not in the bank any more, Peter points out: on the street, maybe, 道かな？ Taigaさんがお友達のSeiyaさんとお金の話をしているようです, Naomi says: Taiga is talking about money with a friend, Seiya. They’re friends, so the Japanese is casual.

After the dialogue

What does Taiga do for a living, Yuichi asks. ホストだと思います, Naomi thinks: a host. Peter got to know the word on coming to Japan, starting with the hostess bar: you pay quite a lot of money to sit and talk with very pretty women. Whether they’re pretty depends on the bar and the price, Naomi says; it’s a hard business if you’re not pretty or charming, Peter thinks; women who are good at talking can make good hostesses too, Yuichi says, 話が上手な人もホステスになれます. A host, then, is the male version, Peter says: a place where women go to be pampered by men, with the same set-up, expensive drinks and staff who make the customer feel appreciated. There are lots in Japan, Peter says. Naomi isn’t sure about lots, but there are some; not a few, Peter says, though fewer than hostess bars, and Naomi agrees, probably.

Taiga is out looking for new customers in the street, Naomi says. The way Taiga talks, though, Peter says: 話し方は、ちょっとですね. But that’s what you meet walking around Shinjuku, Naomi says, 新宿歩いてるといますよ: they talk to women. Still, 店に来ない？ is very casual Japanese, Peter says, and these places are expensive, hundreds of dollars a night, so shouldn’t a tout use smoother, more refined language? だから、スッカラカンなんじゃないですかね, Naomi suggests: that’s why Taiga is broke. Needs to work on those skills, Peter says, 技術をちょっと身に付けないと. And スッカラカン is a fun word, Yuichi says: totally empty, and nearly always about money, Naomi says; in fact, Naomi thinks it’s used only for money.

Phrases worth taking from the chat:

  • 〜だと思います — “I think it’s …”, Naomi’s guess at Taiga’s job.
  • 話が上手な人 — someone who’s good at talking.
  • 技術を身に付ける — to pick up a skill.
  • だから〜なんじゃないですかね — “maybe that’s why …”, a soft guess.

Key lines

A humble なんか, a quick ちょっと, and a look at the balance

The lesson’s dialogue is fifteen lines long, so these three are quoted exactly; section 1 covers the rest in my retelling. The English is my own rendering.

俺おれなんか月末げつまつにはいつもスッカラカンだよ。

Me, I’m always flat broke by the end of the month. — Taiga

Grammar: 俺なんか, the grammar point: “the likes of me”, playing yourself down next to Seiya the saver (section 5); 月末には, “by the end of the month”, with は picking out the time; スッカラカン, “completely empty”.

あ、ちょっと金かねをおろしたいから、ATM寄よっていい？

Oh, I need to take some money out, so can we stop by an ATM? — Taiga

Grammar: ちょっと, the other grammar point: a quick errand (section 5); 金, a blunt お金, rough men’s talk like 俺; おろしたい, “want to withdraw”; ATM寄っていい？, a casual ATMに寄ってもいい？, with に and も dropped.

え？大河たいが、残高ざんだかが2万円まんえんしかないの？給きゅう料りょう日びまであと2週しゅう間かんもあるよ。

What? Taiga, your balance is only ¥20,000? There are still two whole weeks till payday. — Seiya

Grammar: 2万円しかないの？, しか with a negative, “only”, plus a questioning の; 給料日まで, “until payday”; あと2週間, “two more weeks”; も after the number stresses how many: a whole two weeks.

Vocabulary

The hosts’ words, in their order

The hosts’ notes first, then a card for each word, plus more from the dialogue, each with an example of my own.

  • つみたてようのこうざ (積立用の口座) — an account for instalment savings: 積立, the noun of 積み立てる, to accumulate, save or set aside; 用, for the use of; 口座, an account. 積立用の口座というのは、毎月少しずつお金をためていく口座のことです, Naomi explains: an account you put a little money into every month. 貯金ですね, says Yuichi. Is the amount fixed, Peter asks? The customer decides, Naomi says, and normally you can’t withdraw any of it for a set period.
  • おかねをおろす (お金をおろす) — to withdraw money from the bank. お金を引き出す is fine too, Yuichi says, 引き出す, “to pull out”, from lesson 21. The opposite is お金を預ける or 入金する, from lesson 22.
  • ふりこむ (振り込む) — to transfer money, to pay money into someone’s account. The noun is 振り込み, and 送金 also means a money transfer: basically the same, Naomi says, but banks normally say 振り込み, and 送金 can also mean sending money somewhere else. Within Japan, bank to bank, it’s a 振り込み, Peter says, and when Peter sends money to America, it’s called 送金. To pay into someone’s account at the counter you’d fill in a ふりこみようし, a transfer form, and the teller would say この振込用紙にご記入下さい. Peter has never heard it, though: transfers at the ATM need no form, just a touch screen.
  • ざんだか (残高) — a balance, the remainder: 預金残高, the balance in an account. Since 預金 and 貯金 mean much the same (lesson 21), you can also say 貯金残高.
  • きゅうりょうび (給料日) — payday: 給料, salary, and 日, day. In Japan it’s usually the 25th, Peter says; it depends on the company, says Naomi, but the 23rd to the 26th are common, and the banks are always crowded then, so avoid them, Peter advises: long queues at the ATMs, Yuichi says. Or go to a convenience store, Naomi says. Peter calls that being loose with money, お金にルーズですね, since it costs more. It’s the same, Naomi says; well, there’s a bit of a fee, Yuichi says, 手数料がちょっと, usually a small surcharge, depending on the place, Peter says. Not if your bank has a tie-up with the store, Naomi says, 手数料無料の時もあります: Naomi banks with Sumitomo Mitsui, and withdrawals at am/pm ATMs are free. When Peter first came to Japan, convenience stores had no ATMs at all. You’d struggle to find a convenience store in Ibaraki anyway, Naomi teases; Peter apologises to the people of Ibaraki for a colleague’s remarks, and Naomi insists it was a joke: うそです.

The lesson notes add 連れて行く (to take someone along), 感心 (being impressed), スッカラカン (flat broke), 全額 (the full amount) and 弁護士 (a lawyer). Seiya’s マジで？ is from lesson 17. The cards below cover them all, with more words from the dialogue.

Beyond the lessonWhat the dictionaries say. すっからかん is empty, with nothing at all left. The dictionaries don’t limit it to money, though every example they give is about money, from すっからかんの空財布, an empty wallet, to 財産を使い果たしてすっからかんになる, blowing a whole fortune and ending up with nothing; a Japanese–English dictionary adds 米びつがすっからかんだ, “there’s no rice left in the rice bin”. So Naomi’s “only for money” is nearly right. 感心 can mean admirable, deserving praise, as in 親思いの感心な少年, “an admirably devoted son”: that’s Taiga’s 感心感心. Used the other way round, it can also mean being appalled.

Can you take it out? Naomi is half right. MUFG’s terms say an instalment deposit can’t be cancelled before it matures unless the bank accepts that it’s unavoidable, but its product page says you can withdraw while you’re still saving: break the term early and you get a lower interest rate, not a refusal. And these days 積立 is often about investing. Since January 2024, the government’s NISA scheme has let you put up to ¥1.2 million a year, tax-free, into funds through its つみたて投資枠, its “instalment investment allowance”, three times the limit of the old つみたてNISA of 2018, with a lifetime cap of ¥18 million. By the end of June 2026 there were 30.48 million NISA accounts. Seiya’s ¥20,000 a month would fit with room to spare.

Hatsuko’s line of work. On 31 March 2008, three months before this lesson, Japan had 25,041 lawyers, 14.4% of them women. By 2025 there were 46,243, 20.3% of them women, and on 1 October 2026 the Japan Federation of Bar Associations counted 48,037 members, 10,088 of them women.

なんか and ちょっと

The grammar points: playing yourself down, and a quick errand

なんか is a particle that gives something as an example while implying something negative about it, Peter explains. Used about yourself or someone on your side, it shows you humbling yourself, as in the dialogue: 俺なんか月末にはいつもスッカラカンだよ, “I never have any money left at the end of the month.” Taiga has just heard about Seiya’s ¥20,000 a month, so 俺なんか is “me, on the other hand”. It also carries negative feelings, Naomi adds: 私はうそなんか言いません, “I don’t tell lies or anything like that.” The lesson notes say など and なんて can do the same job in speech, and that なんか is the most casual of the three. Their examples include 昨日の夕飯の残りものなんか、食べたくないよ, “I don’t want to eat yesterday’s leftovers.”

The second point is the ちょっと in ちょっとお金を下ろしたいから、ATM寄っていい？ Here it’s an adverb for doing something casually, or without any particular motive, Peter says. Naomi shows the difference: 銀行に行ってきます, “I’m going to the bank”, but ちょっと銀行に行ってきます, “I’m popping to the bank”, like “for a bit” in English. Or 時間ある？話があるんだけど, “got time? I want to talk to you about something”, which becomes 時間ある？ちょっと話があるんだけど, a quick word. Peter uses it for things to take care of quickly, ちょっとお金を下ろしてきます, and Naomi agrees: it suggests something that won’t take long, 時間がかからないようなニュアンス. Yuichi offers 近くまでちょっと行ってきます, “I’m just popping out nearby.”

1

なんか about yourself: humble

俺なんか, 私なんか, うちの息子なんか. Noun + なんか: “the likes of me”, often next to someone who’s doing better.

2

なんか about something else: dismissive

うそなんか言いません, 残りものなんか食べたくない. Often with a negative: “not that”.

3

ちょっと before an action: quick

ちょっと行ってくる, ちょっと話がある. It makes the errand sound casual and short.

俺おれなんか、まだ一度いちども海外かいがいに行いったことがないよ。

Me? I’ve never even been abroad.

宿しゅく題だいなんか、後あとでいいよ。先さきにごはん食たべよう。

Forget the homework for now. Let’s eat first.

ちょっと手伝てつだってくれない？すぐ終おわるから。

Could you give me a hand for a sec? It won’t take long.

なんか, なんて, ちょっと

Beyond the lesson: what the dictionaries add, and ちょっと… as a polite no

Beyond the lessonWhere なんか comes from. なんか is a sound change of なにか, “something”, both big dictionaries agree. The 日本国語大辞典 calls it a spoken word used much like など, and says that when it singles something out, it marks it as low in value more often than など does. Its first example of that use is from Natsume Sōseki’s 吾輩は猫である (1905–06): うちなんかいくら大きくたって腹の足しになるもんか, “a house, however big, won’t fill your belly.” 大辞泉’s examples: 彼の言うことなんか聞くな, “don’t listen to a word that guy says”, and 君になんかわからない, “you wouldn’t understand.”

Is it humble? Not in itself: no dictionary calls these words humble. The humility comes from aiming the belittling sense at yourself. 大辞泉’s entry for など gives exactly that sense, which it says is used mostly in negative sentences, with the example わたしのことなどお忘れでしょう, “you’ll have forgotten all about the likes of me.”

WordWhere it comes from, and an example
などthe oldest: なにと → なんど → など. 金かねなどいるものか, “who needs money?”
なんかfrom なにか, a spoken word, and not always negative: 映画えいがなんかよく行いく, “I often go to films and the like”
なんてfrom などと, など plus と. 手伝てつだいなんてできるか, “as if I could help!”

The exclamation 何て, as in 何てだらしないんだ, “how sloppy!”, is a different word, from なんという. On formality, 大辞泉 says that in modern Japanese なんぞ, like なぞ and なんか, is casual, and the 日本国語大辞典 says なんか gradually took over from なんぞ. Neither ranks なんか against なんて, so “the most casual of the three” is the lesson notes’ own call. など has a gentler side, too: the Tokyo University of Foreign Studies’ grammar notes say it can soften what you say, お茶などいかがですか, “would you care for some tea?”, or hint that something is trivial, つまらない仕事などに時間を使いたくありません, “I don’t want to waste time on boring work and the like.”

ちょっと. It’s a sound change of ちっと, itself from ちと, “a little”; the kanji 一寸, one 寸 of about 3.03 cm, and 鳥渡 are only borrowed for the sound. The 日本国語大辞典’s first example is from 1477. 大辞泉’s senses run from a small amount or a short time, ちょっと昼寝をする, “have a quick nap”, to the lesson’s: something done lightly, ちょっとそこまで行ってくる, “I’m just popping out”. With a negative, it means something can’t easily be done, 私にはちょっとお答えできません, “I can’t really answer that”; on its own, it calls to someone, ちょっと、お客さん, “excuse me!” And it’s the slightly casual way of saying 少し, 大辞泉 notes.

The polite no. The 日本国語大辞典 adds a sense for blurring anything awkward: 私はお酒はちょっと…, and 『今夜どう』『今夜はちょっと…』. The Japan Foundation’s course いろどり teaches it at its starter level: 〜はちょっと… is a soft way of saying something negative, and by not finishing the sentence you mean ちょっとだめです or ちょっと苦手です. Turning down an invitation: 「日曜日、いっしょに買い物に行きませんか？」「すみません。日曜日はちょっと…。」 The hosts do it themselves in this lesson: Peter’s 話し方は、ちょっとですね about Taiga’s patter, and Yuichi’s 手数料がちょっと about convenience-store ATMs. Peter’s ちょっと！ at Naomi’s Ibaraki joke is the calling-out kind.

「今度こんどの土曜日どようび、映画えいがに行いかない？」「ごめん、土曜日どようびはちょっと……。」

“Want to see a film this Saturday?” “Sorry, Saturday’s a bit…” — a polite no

Hosts and the law

Beyond the lesson: what Taiga’s street patter and Hatsuko’s “love” look like to the law

Beyond the lessonIs Taiga allowed to do that? Not under today’s rules, it seems. Japan’s law on the entertainment trades, the 風営法, doesn’t define a host club, but a 2024 report by a panel set up by the National Police Agency puts host clubs in the law’s first category, with kyabakura and snack bars: places where staff entertain customers, 接待, “in a way that creates a festive mood”, while they eat and drink. Article 22 bars anyone running such a business from touting for it, 客引き, or from blocking or following people in public places in order to tout.

Where’s the line? The police’s guidelines spell it out. Just calling out お時間ありませんか and waiting for a reaction isn’t touting yet; inviting a particular person to become a customer is. Host-club staff who approach women in the street as if asking for a date or recruiting staff, then block their way or follow them, are caught too. So Taiga’s 今、暇？ on its own might pass, but 今からうちの店に来ない？, said to particular women, is the kind of invitation the definition describes. The penalty is up to six months in prison or a ¥1 million fine, or both, and the club can be fined and suspended as well. In June 2025, Kanagawa’s public safety commission suspended a host club for 90 days after an employee touted a passing woman: ホストに興味ある。今なら安い。 “Interested in hosts? It’s cheap right now.”

Shinjuku’s own rules. Shinjuku ward has had its own ordinance against touting since 1 September 2013. As strengthened in 2016, it bans touting in public places: picking someone out of the passers-by to steer them to an izakaya, a karaoke bar, a kyabakura or a host club, among others. Since 1 June 2016, someone who keeps touting in a designated district after a formal warning can be fined ¥50,000; the districts include Kabukicho 1- and 2-chome. Calling いらっしゃい、いらっしゃい to everyone in front of a shop is still fine. The Tokyo police call Kabukicho “said to be the biggest entertainment district in the East”, “the town that never sleeps”.

The 2025 law. The problem, as the police describe it: women customers of some host clubs were saddled with large debts as unpaid tabs, 売掛金, and then pressed by hosts or owners to sell sex or work in the sex industry to pay. In May 2025 the Diet amended the 風営法 unanimously, and most of it took effect on 28 June 2025. Intimidating customers into ordering or paying, or demanding, by intimidation or enticement, that they raise the money through prostitution, sex work, adult videos or prostitution abroad, is now a crime, with the same penalty as touting. Running such a business without a licence now risks up to five years in prison or a ¥10 million fine, and up to ¥300 million for a company.

“Because Hatsuko loves me.” The amendment also added rules of conduct for licensed clubs, enforced with orders, suspensions and licence withdrawal rather than criminal penalties. One reads like a footnote to Taiga’s last line. A club must not take advantage of a customer who has romantic or other warm feelings for a host and wrongly believes they are returned, and get them spending by saying the relationship will end if they don’t, or that the host will be demoted or moved unless they do. The police guidelines say admiration counts as well as romance, including a fan’s devotion, 推し活. Consumer law has a similar rule: in 2023 the Consumer Affairs Agency pointed out that a host-club contract won this way from someone with little experience of life may be cancelled.

How many? At the end of 2025 the police counted about 1,100 premises nationwide that look like host clubs, about 33% of them in Tokyo and 19% in Osaka. In 2025 they booked 143 people in 71 cases involving malicious host clubs.

The words. A ホスト, in this sense, is a man who looks after customers at a club for women, and ホストクラブ, made in Japan from “host” and “club”, is first cited in the big dictionary in 1972. A ホステス, a woman who looks after customers at a bar or nightclub, is cited from 1957. The working names used in such places are 源氏名, “Genji names”: taken from the titles of the 54 chapters of The Tale of Genji, they were first given to ladies at court and in samurai households, later to courtesans and geisha, and today to bar hostesses. And an encyclopedia separates 客引き from 呼込み, calling out loudly from the shop front to catch the attention of anyone passing.

駅前えきまえを歩あるいていたら、客引きゃくひきに声こえをかけられた。

I was walking past the station when a tout called out to me.

Payday and the ATM

Beyond the lesson: paydays, convenience-store ATMs and transfers, then and now

Beyond the lessonPayday by law. Japan’s Labour Standards Act says wages must be paid in currency, directly to the worker and in full, at least once a month, on a fixed day; the health ministry calls these the five principles of wage payment. A fixed day means one day: a payday “some time between the 20th and the 25th”, or “the fourth Friday”, isn’t allowed, a regional labour bureau explains, so the hosts’ 23rd to 26th is a spread across companies. Is the 25th really the favourite? No official survey says so. The Bank of Japan does note that salary payments and other settlements bunch up late in the month, when more banknotes go out. National civil servants, for one, are paid mid-month, on the 16th, 17th or 18th, depending on the ministry.

五十日. The days ending in 5 or 0 are 五十日, ごとおび: payment days, and days when the roads jam, the dictionaries say. The 日本国語大辞典 also has 五十払, a merchants’ word, mainly in Osaka, for paying up on every 5th and 10th. MUFG still warns that its branches get crowded on 五十日, at the start and end of the month, and around long weekends.

Wages by app. Since 1 April 2023, with a worker’s consent, wages can also go into an account at a money-transfer company designated by the health ministry, 賃金のデジタル払い. PayPay was the first, on 9 August 2024, and four had been designated by 1 July 2026. Balances are capped at ¥1 million, no one can be made to take it, and the ministry reminds workers that such an account is for paying and sending money, not for saving.

Naomi’s free am/pm ATMs were Sumitomo Mitsui’s own. From 1 March 1999, さくら銀行 opened unstaffed branches inside am/pm convenience stores, named さくら銀行＠BΛNK, “at bank”. By July 2000 there were machines in 949 stores, and in April 2001 Sakura merged with Sumitomo to form SMBC. SMBC’s fee page in late 2008 shows its cards free at its own ATMs and at the am/pm machines on weekdays from 8:45 to 18:00, and ¥105 at other times; at E-net, Lawson or Seven Bank ATMs they cost ¥105 to ¥210. On the 25th and 26th of every month, the hosts’ paydays, the ¥105 out-of-hours fee was waived. That perk survives: today an SMBC card costs ¥220 at a convenience-store ATM on weekday daytimes and ¥330 at other times, but on the 25th and 26th, weekday daytimes are free.

Convenience-store ATMs. E-net, founded by banks, FamilyMart and others on 17 September 1999, first put shared bank ATMs into convenience stores on 8 October 1999, a date it has since named Convenience-Store ATM Day. アイワイバンク銀行 began its ATM service in May 2001 and was renamed セブン銀行, Seven Bank, in October 2005; Lawson’s ATM company started its service in October 2001 and became Lawson Bank in 2018. Today Seven Bank has 28,730 ATMs and E-net 11,706 (both at the end of September 2026), and Lawson Bank 14,092 (end of June 2026). As for am/pm, FamilyMart took it over in December 2009 and had rebranded about 730 of its roughly 1,100 stores by December 2011. In March 2026 FamilyMart signed up with Seven Bank for ATMs of its own design, about 16,000 of them over some four years.

Transfers around the clock. In 2008, Japan’s interbank transfer system, the 全銀システム, ran on weekdays from 8:30 to 15:30. Since 9 October 2018 its モアタイムシステム has made bank transfers possible 24 hours a day, 365 days a year. Peter’s form-free ATM transfer is still the cheaper way: SMBC charges ¥165 or ¥275 to send money to another bank from its ATMs, against ¥605 or ¥990 at the counter. And since October 2022, the ことら service has handled transfers of up to ¥100,000 between individuals.

振り込め詐欺. 振り込む has a darker compound: 振り込め詐欺, “transfer-it fraud”, from the command form 振り込め. The name first appears on the National Police Agency’s website on 11 December 2004, covering “it’s me” scams, オレオレ詐欺, fake bills and fake loan deposits. Losses dropped sharply when cash transfers at ATMs were capped at ¥100,000 in January 2007, the police say, and in June 2008, the month of this lesson, the agency set up an office to fight it, and a law to return frozen money to victims took effect. Since 2026 the police call the whole family 特殊詐欺, “special fraud”: in 2025 there were 27,832 cases and losses of ¥142.3 billion, nearly double the year before. Osaka has gone further: since 1 October 2025 its ordinance has made banks cap ATM transfers at ¥100,000 a day for customers aged 70 and over, living in Osaka, who haven’t made one in three years, the first rule in Japan to make such measures compulsory.

今いまは夜中よなかでも、すぐに振ふり込こめるようになった。

These days you can transfer money instantly, even in the middle of the night.

Quiz

Pick the word or form that fits. Each answer comes with the reason.

1. 私わたし＿＿＿、全然ぜんぜん料理りょうりができないよ。

Me? I can’t cook at all.

なんか after yourself plays you down: “the likes of me”.

2. 虫むし＿＿＿、絶対ぜったい食たべたくない。

Bugs? I’m never eating those.

なんか before a negative waves something away as not worth it.

3. ＿＿＿コンビニに行いってくるね。

I’m just popping out to the convenience store.

ちょっと makes the errand sound quick and casual.

4. 時間じかんある？＿＿＿話はなしがあるんだけど。

Got a minute? There’s something I’d like a quick word about.

ちょっと: a quick word, nothing that will take long.

5. ATMで、お金かねを＿＿＿。

I took money out at the ATM.

お金をおろす: to withdraw. 預ける and 振り込む put money in.

6. 家賃やちんは、毎月まいつき大家おおやさんの口座こうざに＿＿＿いる。

I pay the rent into the landlord’s account every month.

振り込む: to pay into someone’s account.

7. 通つう帳ちょうを見みたら、＿＿＿が1000円えんしかなかった。

I looked at my passbook, and my balance was only ¥1,000.

残高: what’s left in the account.

8. ＿＿＿の前まえは、いつもお金かねがない。

I never have any money just before payday.

給料日: the day the salary comes in.

9. 代金だいきんは、＿＿＿返かえしてもらった。

I got the full amount refunded.

全額: the whole amount.

10. 旅行りょこうのために、毎月まいつき少すこしずつ＿＿＿いる。

I’m putting a little aside every month for a trip.

積み立てる: to save up bit by bit.

11. 毎日まいにち勉べん強きょうするなんて、＿＿＿だね。

You study every day? That’s admirable.

感心: being impressed by someone.

12. 友ともだちを、好すきなお店みせに＿＿＿。

I took a friend to my favourite restaurant.

連れて行く is for people; 持って行く is for things.

13. 財布さいふに、500円えん＿＿＿ない。

I’ve only got ¥500 in my wallet.

しか + a negative: only.

14. 給きゅう料りょう日びまで、あと2週しゅう間かん＿＿＿あるよ。

There are still two whole weeks till payday.

も after a number stresses how much: a whole two weeks.
