Out of the station, turn right. So why the long way round?

Part three of “Party in the ’Burbs”. Yasumi walks from the station following Hajime’s emailed directions, gets stuck where the road splits, asks a passer-by, and finds that the road straight to the station was there all along. The grammar point is たら, “when” or “if”, the conditional that works almost everywhere: 駅を出たら右に曲がって. Plus finding your way in Japan, Yasumi’s dialect どうするべ, and the shopping streets, laundromats and phones along the way.

2

The scene

Naomi, Yuichi and Peter, before and after the dialogue

Part three of “Party in the ’Burbs”. This is the last lesson in the series on conditionals, Peter announces. Wrong, says Naomi: there’s one more. So it’s part three of four: first と, then なら, and today たら, another “if”. Where are we, and with whom? On the street again, Yuichi says: Yasumi is heading for Hajime’s, and talks to a passer-by (通行人), so in polite Japanese.

After the dialogue

Naomi has a question about the dialogue’s どすべ、どすべ: does anyone say it? Not Yuichi; never, says Naomi. It means どうしよう, “what shall I do?”, and said slowly it’s どうするべ, Yuichi explains. It’s Tōhoku dialect, Peter says, which came up in the show’s Aomori dialect series, and Naomi reckons Peter knows the べ from living in Ibaraki. That べ was originally べし, Peter says, which is news to Naomi; べし is べき, べきだ, “should”, Yuichi adds. So どうするべ is どうしたらいい？, “what should I do?” Who says it? None of Naomi’s friends; Yuichi might say どうするべ, but only among friends, and only men, Yuichi thinks, never women. In Tokyo it may be mostly men, Peter says, but in Aomori dialect it’s simply the normal way to say it, for everyone. Peter rarely hears it in Tokyo but heard it a lot in Ibaraki, and it’s casual. Yasumi is talking out loud alone, Yuichi points out, hence the very casual おれ and めんどくさ: めんどくさい with the い dropped, Naomi says. (Section 7 has more on both.)

Phrases worth taking from the chat:

  • 担当お願いします — Naomi handing over to Yuichi: “over to you”, literally “the person in charge, please”.
  • もともと「べし」ですね — “it was originally べし”: もともと, originally.
  • 友達同士のときだけ — “only between friends”: 同士, fellows, people of the same kind.
  • 雲泥の差 — “a world of difference”, literally cloud and mud: Peter on dry cleaners’ prices (section 4).
  • 笑いのツボ — “your funny bone”: Yuichi on Naomi’s phone story (section 4).
  • じゃ、また。 and さよなら。 — the sign-off.

Key lines

The directions, the question, and the penny dropping

The lesson’s dialogue is eleven lines long, so these three are quoted exactly; section 1 covers the rest in my retelling. The English is my own rendering.

「駅えきを出でたら右みぎに曲まがって、そば屋やが見みえるまでまっすぐ進すすむ。」ああ、ここか！

“Come out of the station and turn right, then go straight on until you see a soba shop.” Ah, this is it! — Yasumi, reading

Grammar: 出たら, the たら conditional (section 5); 右に曲がって, with に for the direction; 見えるまで, “until it comes into view”; まっすぐ進む, in the plain form, as directions in a message often are; ここか, “so this is the place”.

「大おおきな携帯けいたいのオブジェ」を探さがしているんですが、どっちの道みちに行いったらいいですかね？

I’m looking for “a big sculpture of a mobile phone”. Which road should I take, do you think? — Yasumi

Grammar: 探しているんですが, “I’m looking for…, but”, the polite lead-in to a question; どっち, a casual どちら; 行ったらいい, asking for advice with 〜たらいい (section 5); ですかね, softening the question.

あの道みちをまっすぐ行いくと駅えきに着つくんだよね。。。じゃあなんで一はじめはこんなめんどくさい道みちを俺おれに教おしえたんだ？

So if you go straight down that road, you get to the station… Then why did Hajime give me such a long-winded route? — Yasumi

Grammar: 行くと, lesson 14’s と; 着くんだよね, “so it’s the case that…, right?”; 一, Hajime’s name written in kanji; 俺, the casual masculine “I”; 教えたんだ, the explaining んだ in a “why” question.

Vocabulary

The lesson’s word list, in its order

The hosts’ notes first, then a card for each word, plus more from the dialogue and the chat, each with an example of my own.

  • そばや (そば屋) — a buckwheat noodle shop: そば, buckwheat, and や, a shop or a place of business. Careful, Naomi says: や can’t stand on its own; a shop by itself is みせ, as in 店へ行きます.
  • まっすぐ — straight, upright, direct: Yuichi’s この道をまっすぐ行って下さい, “go straight down this road.”
  • ちょくしん (直進) — going straight ahead: 直 means straight, まっすぐ, and 進 to move ahead. 直進する, to go straight on.
  • コインランドリー — a laundromat. Ordinary dry cleaners are クリーニング屋. Cleaners are interesting, Peter says: their prices are worlds apart (雲泥の差), ¥200 a shirt at one, ¥1,000 at another, and who knows how they decide? By the customer, Naomi suggests. Yuichi doesn’t use cleaners, and Naomi leaves it to Naomi’s mother (お母さんにたのんでます). ちょっと！, says Peter.
  • しょうてんがい (商店街) — a shopping district: 商店, shops, and 街, also read まち, an area or town. Yuichi’s 商店街を直進して下さい, “go straight through the shopping street”, is the same as 商店街をまっすぐ行ってください.
  • けいたい (携帯) — a mobile phone, 携帯電話 in full. 携帯 first meant portable, carried with you, but now means the phone on its own. Has anyone dropped a phone in the toilet? Not Yuichi. Naomi has, once: it fell out of a bag just as the toilet flushed, and went. Calling it got no answer. Only Naomi could manage that, Peter says, since heavy things don’t flush; congratulations. That story hit Peter’s ツボ, Yuichi observes: 笑いのツボ, the funny bone.
  • めんどくさい — troublesome, tiresome; short for めんどうくさい. You can say あー、めんどくさい, or, very casually, あー、めんどくさ: “ugh, what a pain!”
  • つうこうにん (通行人) — a passer-by. 通 means to pass and 行 to go, so 通行 is going through, 通って行く; add 人, a person: someone passing by. Naomi’s example: 私は、通行人に道を聞きました, “I asked a passer-by the way.”

The lesson notes add オブジェ, from the French objet d’art. The cards below cover them all, with more words from the dialogue.

Beyond the lesson〜屋, more than shops. After a noun, 屋 means the person or business that sells it: 本屋, a bookshop; 菓子屋, a sweet shop. It also names a type of person, sometimes with a sneer or a wry smile: がんばり屋, a hard worker; 気取り屋, a poser; わからず屋, someone who won’t see reason. And it marks a specialist, said proudly, enviously or with contempt: 技術屋, a technical person; 政治屋, a mere political operator. Old shop names use it too, like 越後屋.

めんどくさい is listed in the dictionaries as the same word as めんどうくさい, “awfully troublesome”, and the short form isn’t new: one dictionary’s earliest example comes from a collection of comic verse published between 1772 and 1817.

5

〜たら

The grammar point: “when”, “if”, and first things first

Next in the series, Peter says, is たら, the たら conditional. It shows that the action or state before たら happens, or may happen, before the one in the main clause, and the first part, the “if”, has to be satisfied for the second to take place: when this happens, that happens; if this is so, that follows. It’s very like と. Naomi’s example is the dialogue’s 駅を出たら右に曲がって, “when you come out of the station, turn right.” Yuichi’s is 君が歌ったら僕も歌うよ, “if you sing, I’ll sing too”: the singing comes first, Naomi explains, so it means 君が歌った後で、僕も歌うよ, “after you sing, I’ll sing too.”

1

Plain past + ら

歌った → 歌ったら; 飲んだ → 飲んだら. If you can make the past, you can make たら.

2

First things first

The たら part happens before the main clause. Out of the station first, then right.

3

Anything can follow

A request, an invitation, a plan: 晴れたら、海に行こう, “if it’s sunny, let’s go to the beach”. The one thing と can’t do.

The lesson notes call たら the most commonly used conditional, and point out the dialogue’s どっちの道に行ったらいいですかね: 〜たらいい makes a suggestion, literally “if you do this, it would be good”, so as a question it asks for advice. The one job たら can’t do is なら’s: advice given before the event, built on what someone plans to do. Their examples are a recommendation for someone who’s going to study Japanese, and an order to take the bus to the station: both need なら, because 勉強したら and 行ったら would mean once it’s done.

Verb行いった, 見みた行いったら, 見みたら

い-adjective安やすかった安やすかったら
Noun, な-adj.雨あめだった, 暇ひまだった雨あめだったら, 暇ひまだったら

Beyond the lessonThe all-rounder, with a few habits. Lesson 14’s section 6 compared the four conditionals; here’s what たら adds. A Japan Foundation column on giving advice calls 〜たらいい the most conversational of the three advice patterns, beside 〜ばいい and the rather firm 〜といい, and notes that in casual speech it shrinks to 行ったら？, “why not go?”, at some cost in politeness. With a past main clause, たら reports what you noticed when something happened: 電車に乗ったら、ずいぶん混んでいた, “when I got on the train, it was packed”. A grammar module from the Tokyo University of Foreign Studies adds that this “discovery” たら sounds casual, next to と. And across Japan’s dialects, a NINJAL study found more and more areas using たら and similar all-purpose forms, because they come with fewer grammatical limits.

家いえに着ついたら、メールちょうだい。

Send me a message when you get home.

もし明日あした雨あめだったら、試合しあいは中止ちゅうしです。

If it rains tomorrow, the match is off.

分わからなかったら、いつでも聞きいてね。

If there’s anything you don’t understand, ask me any time.

Watch forたら is both “if” and “when”. Put もし in front for an uncertain “if” (もし雨だったら); about something sure to happen, it’s “when”: 二十歳になったら, “when I turn twenty”.

Finding the way

Beyond the lesson: why Hajime gives landmarks, not street names

Beyond the lessonBlocks, not streets. Hajime gives landmarks because street names wouldn’t help: most Japanese streets don’t have one. A 1962 law on addresses allows two systems. One numbers the blocks, 街区, marked out by roads, railways or rivers, and then the buildings in each block: 街区方式. The other names the streets and numbers the buildings along them: 道路方式. The transport ministry notes that block addresses are used almost everywhere in Japan, while street addresses, usual in Europe and America and better for giving directions, are used only in part of Higashine, in Yamagata. So an address like ○○町2丁目3番4号 gives the district, the block and the building, but never the street.

Ask at the 交番. When the landmarks run out, there’s the police box. On 1 April 2026 Japan had 6,132 交番 and 5,718 駐在所, residential police posts, plus about 6,200 civilian counsellors, mostly retired officers, whose work includes giving directions. Tokyo’s first police boxes date from 1874, when the Metropolitan Police was founded.

Why the green light is 青. The traffic law itself says 青色の灯火, the blue light, and a car facing it may go straight on, 直進, or turn. Japan’s first electric traffic lights, imported from America, went up at Hibiya in Tokyo in 1930, and the law called the go light 緑, green, until 1947; but people said 青, and the name stuck. Police pages say lights made since 1973 have been made 青 to match the name. A 2019 vision-science article gives the reason it felt natural: old Japanese used one word, あを, for blue and green together, and the dictionary still defines 青 as once covering blue, green and indigo.

Words for directions

  • 〜を右に曲がる — turn right at: the place takes を, the direction に.
  • 〜が見えるまで — until you see; 〜が見えたら, when you see.
  • 二つ目の角 — the second corner; 〜つ目の信号, the … set of lights.
  • 突き当たり — the end of the road, where you have to turn; 交差点, a junction.
  • 〜の手前 — just before; 〜の先, just past; 〜の向かい, opposite.

二ふたつ目めの信号しんごうを右みぎに曲まがって、突つき当あたりを左ひだりです。

Turn right at the second set of lights, then left at the end of the road.

コンビニの向むかいに交番こうばんがあるから、分わからなかったらそこで聞きいて。

There’s a police box opposite the convenience store, so if you can’t find it, ask there.

7

どうするべ and めんどくさっ

Beyond the lesson: Yasumi’s dialect, and adjectives with the end chopped off

Beyond the lessonべ, from べし. The hosts have it right. べい is a sound change of べき, from classical べし, and since the Middle Ages it has ended sentences in the east of Japan. It lives on, as べい, べ or ぺ, in Kantō and Tōhoku dialects, usually meaning “probably”, “I will” or “let’s”: さあ、行くべい, “come on, let’s go.” It was common enough that Kantō speech was nicknamed べいべい言葉. A 1999 study adds that it’s still widespread in eastern Japan, turns up a little in the west, and means different things from dialect to dialect. Peter heard it in Ibaraki, in Kantō, the prefecture whose accent Tom had in lessons 9 to 13.

めんどくさっ: adjectives with the tail cut off. Drop an adjective’s final い, clip what’s left, and you have an exclamation: 寒っ！, 痛っ！, めんどくさっ！. In a 2010 government survey, 62.8% of people said they say 寒っ themselves and don’t mind hearing it; adding those who don’t say it but don’t mind it, the figure was in the mid-80s for 寒っ, the mid-70s for すごっ and 短っ, and the mid-60s for 長っ and うるさっ. A linguist describes it as the adjective stem said with a sudden stop in the throat: standard Japanese used to allow it only with a few words such as 痛い and 寒い, but now uses it far more widely. A 2003 newspaper column credited Kansai comedians with spreading it among young people in Tokyo, since forms like ああ寒 had always been normal in Kansai. It isn’t new, either: a comic novel of 1809–13 has ヲヲ、さむ, and a children’s song begins おおさむ、こさむ.

もうこんな時間じかんか。そろそろ帰かえるべ。

Is that the time already? Let’s head home. (dialect)

外そとに出でたとたん、「寒さむっ！」と声こえが出でた。

The moment I stepped outside, “It’s freezing!” just came out.

Shops, laundry, phones

Beyond the lesson: what Yasumi walks past, in numbers

Beyond the lessonThe shopping street. In the latest full survey of Japan’s shopping streets, in October 2024, the average street had 51.1 shops, and the vacancy rate had fallen to 11.30%, from 13.59% three years before. Still, a third of streets said they were declining (33.2%) and another 28.3% feared they might be; only 2.7% called themselves prosperous. The biggest problem, for 64.9%, was shop owners growing old with nobody to take over. A street of shuttered shops even has a name, シャッター通り, which one encyclopedia puts down to shrinking local populations and big supermarkets and malls moving out to the suburbs.

Laundromats and dry cleaners. A coin laundry isn’t legally a cleaning business at all, so official counts are rare: the health ministry counted 10,228 in 1996 and 16,693 in 2013. Dry cleaners are shrinking. General cleaning shops fell by 45% in the 25 years to 2015, and at the end of March 2025 there were 69,855 cleaning businesses of all kinds, 4.2% fewer than a year earlier.

From car phone to 携帯. 携帯 first meant carrying something on you, and one dictionary’s earliest example dates from the late 1830s. The phone came much later: a car phone in December 1979, with a radio unit of about 7 kg in the boot; the shoulder phone of 1985, about 3 kg; the first handheld 携帯電話 service in April 1987, at about 900 g; and i-mode, the mobile internet, in February 1999, which docomo switched off on 31 March 2026. In August 2025, 91.8% of households had a smartphone, now more than had a television, and 78.9% of people did.

駅前えきまえの商しょう店てん街がいも、最近さいきんはシャッターが閉しまった店みせが増ふえてきた。

Even the shopping street by the station has more and more shuttered shops these days.

9

Quiz

Pick the word or form that fits. Each answer comes with the reason.

1. 駅えきを＿＿＿、右みぎに曲まがってください。

When you come out of the station, turn right.

A request in the main clause: たら. と can’t end in a request, and nor can ば after an action verb.

2. どこで乗のり換かえ＿＿＿いいですか。

Where should I change trains?

Asking for advice: 〜たらいいですか. 〜てもいいですか asks permission.

3. 仕事しごとが＿＿＿、飲のみに行いかない？

When you’ve finished work, want to go for a drink?

An invitation in the main clause: たら.

4. 家いえに帰かえっ＿＿＿、荷物にもつが届とどいていた。

When I got home, a parcel had arrived.

たら with a past main clause: what you found when it happened.

5. 「日本語にほんごを勉べん強きょうしたいんだ。」「日本語にほんごを＿＿＿、このアプリがいいよ。」

“I want to study Japanese.” “If you’re going to study Japanese, this app is good.”

Advice before the studying starts: なら. 勉強したら would mean once you’ve studied.

6. 暇ひま＿＿＿、ちょっと手伝てつだってくれない？

If you’re free, could you give me a hand?

A request after the condition: たら works (だったら after a noun or な-adjective); と doesn’t.

7. そば屋やが見みえる＿＿＿、まっすぐ進すすんでください。

Go straight on until you see a soba shop.

まで: until, the whole way. までに means “by”, a deadline.

8. 二ふたつ目めの角かど＿＿＿右みぎに曲まがる。

Turn right at the second corner.

The corner you turn at takes を; the direction takes に: 右に.

9. 信号しんごうを＿＿＿すると、左手ひだりてに病びょう院いんがあります。

Go straight on at the lights, and there’s a hospital on your left.

直進する: to go straight on.

10. ＿＿＿に道みちを聞きいたら、親切しんせつに教おしえてくれた。

When I asked a passer-by the way, they told me very kindly.

通行人: a passer-by.

11. 駅前えきまえの＿＿＿には、八百屋やおやや魚屋さかなやが並ならんでいる。

The shopping street by the station is lined with greengrocers and fishmongers.

商店街: a shopping street.

12. 毎日まいにち料理りょうりするのは、正しょう直じき＿＿＿。

Honestly, cooking every day is a pain.

めんどくさい: too much bother.

13. ＿＿＿を家いえに忘わすれて、連絡れんらくできなかった。

I left my phone at home, so I couldn’t get in touch.

携帯: a mobile phone.

14. 終しゅう電でんを逃のがしちゃった。＿＿＿。

I’ve missed the last train. What do I do?

どうしよう: “what shall I do?”
