The dialogue

The whole scene, retold for review

Every line is rephrased, and the last two are new: Yuuko remembers something about Roppongi Hills, and Masami reaches for the grammar point, こそ. The lesson’s exact key lines are in section 3. Scene

Monday lunchtime, a café near the office. Yuuko is glowing; Masami is stirring a coffee that went cold long ago.

Yuuko

映画えいがは正しょう直じきつまらなかったんだけど、デートはすごくよかったのよ。

Honestly, the film was boring, but the date was lovely.

Masami

へえ……。いいわね。幸しあわせそうじゃない。

Oh… lucky you. You look happy.

Yuuko

映画えいがの後あと、たかおさんおすすめのお店みせに行いったんだけど、雰囲気ふんいきがすごくよくてさ。

After the film we went to a place Takao recommended, and it had such a lovely atmosphere.

Yuuko

料理りょうりもおいしいし、お酒さけもおいしいし、もう最高さいこうだったの！

The food was good, the drinks were good; it was just perfect!

Masami

はいはい……。よかったわね、本当ほんとうに。

Yes, yes… Good for you, really.

Yuuko

で、あなたは？あなたもデートだったんでしょ？どうだった？

So what about you? You had a date too, didn’t you? How did it go?

Masami

え？あ、うん……すっごく楽たのしかったよ！六本木ろっぽんぎヒルズの最さい上じょう階かいのレストランで、二人ふたりで食事しょくじしてさ。

Huh? Oh, yeah… it was so much fun! We had dinner, just the two of us, at the restaurant on the top floor of Roppongi Hills.

Masami

眺ながめは最高さいこうだし、彼かれはかっこいいし、あんなデート、生うまれて初はじめて！

The view was amazing, the guy was gorgeous… I’ve never had a date like it!

Yuuko

へえ、すごい！お互たがい、いいデートができてよかったね！

Wow, amazing! I’m so glad we both had good dates!

Masami

うん……そうね……。

Yeah… I suppose…

Yuuko

……あれ？でも、六本木ろっぽんぎヒルズの最さい上じょう階かいって、美術館びじゅつかんじゃなかったっけ？

…Hang on. Isn’t the top floor of Roppongi Hills an art museum?

Masami

……そ、そんなことより、あなたこそ、映画えいががつまらなかったんでしょ！

…N-never mind that! You’re the one whose film was boring!

The scene

Naomi, Yuichi and Peter, before and after the dialogue

The last part of “Going on a Date”: ようやく終わりますね, Naomi says, “finally, the end.” Peter hopes for a happy ending. The grammar point is こそ, a particle that emphasises a word, phrase or clause. Last time the two went to the cinema and then a restaurant in Roppongi; today we find out how it went. The woman seems to be reporting the date to a friend, Naomi says, and since they’re friends, the Japanese is casual.

After the dialogue

Naomi has no particular impression, 特に感想はない, except one thing: the top floor of Roppongi Hills isn’t a restaurant. So Masami was lying, Yuichi concludes? It’s an art museum, Naomi says, the Mori Art Museum. The 52nd floor is an observation deck and the 51st has restaurants, but they’re all members-only, 会員制: you can’t get in unless you know somebody, Peter gathers. How much to join? ¥10,000, Yuichi guesses. Over a million yen, says Naomi, just to join, and that membership covers more than the restaurants, all of Mori Building, Naomi thinks: over a million yen up front, then around ¥200,000 a month. Peter works it out at a hundred yen to the dollar: $10,000 up front and $2,000 a month. Crazy, says Naomi; ¥200,000 a month, Yuichi marvels. Companies are often the members, Naomi has heard, for their clients, Peter guesses; or movie stars, Naomi adds.

Phrases worth taking from the chat:

  • ようやく — at last, finally.
  • 報告する — to report, fill someone in: 友達にデートの話を報告してる.
  • 特に感想はない — “no particular impression”: 感想, back from lesson 19.
  • 会員制 — members-only; 入会金, a joining fee; 月額, a monthly charge.
  • まじですか？ — “seriously?”, Peter’s reply to the price: マジ from lesson 17.

Key lines

The happy report, the question back, and the fib

The lesson’s dialogue is ten lines long, so these three are quoted exactly; section 1 covers the rest in my retelling. The English is my own rendering.

まぁ、映画えいがはつまらなかったけど、いいデートだったのよぉ

Well, the film was boring, but it was a lovely date. — Yuuko

Grammar: まぁ, “well”; つまらなかった, the past of つまらない; けど, “but”; だったのよぉ, the ending のよ, which sounds feminine (lesson 18), stretched out happily.

で、あなたの方ほうはどうだったのよ？あなたもデートだったんでしょ？

So, how did yours go? You had a date too, didn’t you? — Yuuko

Grammar: で, “so”; あなたの方, “you, on your side”, the 方 that picks one of two sides, as in the grammar point (section 5); どうだったのよ？, a question with のよ; 〜んでしょ？, “didn’t you?”, checking what you think you know.

いやー、眺ながめ最高さいこうだったし、彼かれもかっこよかったし、あんな最高さいこうのデート初はじめてよ！

Oh, the view was amazing, and the guy was gorgeous; I’ve never had a date like it! — Masami

Grammar: いやー, “oh, man”; 最高だったし…かっこよかったし, し stacking up reasons (lesson 7); あんな最高のデート, “a date as perfect as that”; 初めてよ, “a first”. The lesson notes give the game away: Masami is lying.

Vocabulary

The hosts’ words, in their order

The hosts’ notes first, then a card for each word, plus more from the dialogue and the chat, each with an example of my own.

  • ふんいき (雰囲気) — atmosphere, mood. How does Yuichi honestly say it? ふいんき. Yuichi swaps the ん and the い, Naomi explains: it’s properly ふんいき, but quite a few people say ふいんき, and lately a lot of people have switched the order, Peter adds. It’s still considered a mistake, Naomi says, but to Japanese ears the two sound almost the same. Peter tries すばらしい雰囲気, and nobody can tell which version Peter said; it got across, though. 雰囲気がいい is a good atmosphere, 雰囲気が悪い a bad one; you also hear 最高な雰囲気, すばらしい雰囲気 and いい雰囲気. Yuichi’s example: この店は店員さんの態度が悪くて、雰囲気が悪い, “the staff here are rude, so the atmosphere is bad.”
  • さいじょうかい (最上階) — the top floor. Yuichi takes the kanji apart: 最, “the most” (もっとも, Naomi adds); 上, upper or top; and 階, a floor. So 最も上の階, the highest floor. Yuichi’s example: いつかホテルの最上階のSuiteに泊まりたい, “someday I’d like to stay in a suite on a hotel’s top floor.” Naomi had no idea Yuichi had that dream; everyone will come and visit. A suite would have a good view, says Peter, which brings us to…
  • ながめ (眺め) — a scene, a view: 最上階からのながめがよかった, “the view from the top floor was great.” The verb is ながめる, to view.
  • びじん (美人) — a beautiful woman; it isn’t usually used for men, Naomi says. For men there’s びだんし, Yuichi says, or びなんし, says Naomi: the same kanji, 美, beautiful, and 男子, men, with the reading sometimes changing. Is Yuichi a 美男子, Peter asks? “Cute”, says Naomi, whose picture of a 美男子 is tall, slim and a little sexy. Yuichi turns the question round: is Naomi a 美女? かわいい, Peter decides; a 美女, Naomi explains, is a tall, slim woman. They’re both just being modest, Peter says: both are beautiful. Naomi isn’t very pleased.
  • おたがい (お互い) — each other: you and me. Peter’s example: お互いを理解し合いましょう, “let’s try to understand each other”, the kind of thing guys hear from women, Peter says. And then おたがいさま, with 様, a polite suffix. Yuichi takes the chance to confess to not having read the script today; Peter hasn’t either: お互い様, we’re both in the same boat. お互い様じゃないよ。来る前に読んでよ, says Naomi, who wrote it: “it’s not お互い様, read it before you come!” はい、わかりました、お母さん, says Peter: “yes, Mum.”

The lesson notes add デート, 幸せ, お勧め, 料理, お酒, うまい, 最高 and 六本木ヒルズ. The cards below cover them all, with more words from the dialogue and the chat.

Beyond the lesson美人 wasn’t always a woman. The dictionaries still list a second sense, a handsome man, with examples from the 1500s and 1600s: a 1591 text calls the biblical Joseph a matchless bijin. Further back, in the Chinese classic 詩経, 美人 could mean a revered ruler or a sage. For men today there’s 美男子, listed under both readings, びなんし and びだんし, and both turn up in print in the early 1890s.

眺め, the view and the daydream. 眺め is looking out over something, or the scenery itself, 眺めが良い; but its second sense is gazing while lost in thought, and in classical poetry it often doubles as 長雨, “long rain”.

おすすめ, three ways. すすめる has three kanji, and the government’s 2014 guide to words that sound the same splits them: 進める moves something forward (時計を進める); 勧める urges someone to do something (入会を勧める, 読書を勧める); 薦める puts forward a person or thing as the right one (候補者として薦める, 良書を薦める, お薦めの銘柄を尋ねる). By that logic a restaurant you vouch for is お薦め; the lesson notes write お勧め, as the dictionary heads it, and kana, おすすめ, avoids the choice.

5

〜こそ

The grammar point: “this one, of all”, and “no, YOU”

Today’s grammar point appears only in the bonus track told from the men’s side, Peter explains. こそ is a particle that emphasises a word, phrase or clause. In that version Takao first talks about the date, then asks a friend, Masashi, how that one went: with お前の方こそ, “you” is stressed as opposed to “me”, so Takao is saying: my story aside, I want to hear about you. Yuichi reads the line: お前の方こそどうだったんだよ, “and what about you?” Piece by piece, Peter says: お前, a very informal “you”, used mainly by guys but for a man or a woman (lesson 17); の方, the possessive の and 方, direction, so “you and things on your side”; こそ, stressing that; and どうだったんだよ, “how were things, what happened”.

It’s the same こそ, Naomi says, as in こちらこそよろしくお願いします, the answer when someone says はじめまして。どうぞよろしくお願いします. Or こちらの方こそ, Yuichi adds, a little more polite, though Peter has never heard it. So they practise: はじめまして。よろしくお願いします; こちらこそよろしくお願いします, or こちらの方こそよろしくお願いいたします, with いたします, the humble form of します, to match. One more: あやまってよ, “apologise!”; そっちこそあやまれよ, “no, YOU apologise!” A bit strong, Peter says: for brothers and sisters, family, good friends.

1

Noun (+ particle) + こそ

今年こそ, 私にこそ. This one, above all: “this year, for sure”, “to me especially”.

2

Turning it back: こちらこそ, そっちこそ

君こそ、あやまれよ. Politely, “it’s me who should say that”; in a fight, “no, YOU”.

3

〜てこそ, 〜からこそ

完走してこそ, 努力しているからこそ. “Only by…”, “precisely because…”.

The lesson notes list where こそ goes: after a noun, 今年こそ, “this year, definitely”; after a noun and particle, 私にこそ, “to me in particular”; after a て-form, 完走してこそ, “only if you finish the race”; and after a clause, usually a reason ending in から, あなたが努力しているからこそ, “precisely because you’re making an effort”. Their examples include 「あやまってよ！」「君こそ、あやまれよ！」 and マラソンは、完走してこそ意義がある, “a marathon only means something if you finish it.”

ExampleMeaning
Noun今年ことしこそthis year, for sure
Replyこちらこそit’s me who should say that
て-form見みてこそonly by seeing it
から好すきだからこそprecisely because you love it

Beyond the lesson“No, YOU.” A Japan Foundation column explains the こそ of 「君が知っているんだろ。」「知らないわよ。あなたこそ白状しなさいよ。」, “You know, don’t you?” “I don’t! YOU confess!”: it picks out a person or thing and points at it hard, “this one, and no other”, so あなたこそ firmly denies being the one. That’s Masami’s あなたこそ in my retelling. And こちらこそ, the dictionary explains, is こそ with the rest of the sentence left unsaid: 「先日はありがとう」「いや、こちらこそ」, with the ありがとう understood. Its other example of that use is ようこそ, “welcome”.

More jobs. A grammar module from the Tokyo University of Foreign Studies describes こそ as pointing at a thing with special force: 山田さんこそ会長にふさわしいです, “Yamada-san, if anyone, is right for chair”; 全力を尽くしてこそいい結果がでます, “only by giving it everything do you get good results.” It can follow ば and から too: あなたのことを思えばこそ（思うからこそ）言うのです, “it’s precisely because I care about you that I’m saying this.” The dictionary adds a quieter use, granting one thing before turning to another: 感謝こそすれ、恨むことはあるまい, “if anything I’d be grateful; I’d hardly hold a grudge.”

「いつもありがとうございます。」「いえいえ、こちらこそ。」

“Thank you, as always.” “Oh no, thank you.”

今度こんどこそ、遅刻ちこくしないで行いくよ。

This time, for sure, I won’t be late.

毎日まいにち練れん習しゅうしたからこそ、上手じょうずになったんだよ。

It’s precisely because you practised every day that you got good.

こそ, old and new

Beyond the lesson: what the dictionaries say, and the grammar rule こそ kept from old Japanese

Beyond the lessonThe rule こそ kept. Classical Japanese had an agreement rule, 係り結び: when ぞ, なむ, や or か appeared in a sentence, the verb at the end took the 連体形, and with こそ it took the 已然形, says one encyclopedia. Another gives 名こそ流れて猶聞えけれ, where けれ is the 已然形, and the dictionary quotes 徒然草: もののあはれは秋こそまされ, “it is autumn, above all, that deepens the feeling for things.” こそ is said to be stronger than ぞ and なむ, and its hold on the verb lasted until the end of the Muromachi period.

Where it survives. In modern Japanese こそ no longer changes the verb, so dictionaries often class it with the 副助詞, which is where the grammar module files it. But the old ending lingers: the conceding use now lives mostly in こそあれ and こそすれ, and こそあれ is こそ plus あり in its 已然形. So does the proverb 身を捨ててこそ浮かぶ瀬もあれ, “only by being ready to sacrifice yourself do you find a way out”, still ending in あれ.

お互い様. お互い is the polite form of 互い, and お互い様 means both sides are in the same position: 困るのはお互い様です, “we’re both in the same boat.” It’s often said to reassure someone, one dictionary adds: since the gain or the burden is the same for both, please don’t worry about it. Peter’s use, sharing the blame for the unread script, is the same idea.

「手伝てつだってくれて、ありがとう。」「いいよ、お互たがい様さまだよ。」

“Thanks for helping.” “No problem, we look out for each other.”

ふんいき or ふいんき?

Beyond the lesson: how many people say ふいんき, why it happens, and sound swaps that stuck

Beyond the lessonHow many say ふいんき? In a survey reported by NHK’s broadcasting research institute in 2018, 22% of people said they (also) say ふいんき, and the share was higher still among people in their forties, so it isn’t simply the young. On air, the article says, ふんいき is preferable. The dictionary notes surveys showing more and more people saying ふいんき, and a veteran dictionary editor has written that it can’t be listed as a reading, since it isn’t a genuine variant.

Why it’s so easy to do. It isn’t a straight swap, NHK explains: it comes about in small steps involving nasal sounds, and ends up sounding as if ン and イ had changed places. The dictionary editor 飯間浩明 makes the same point: the u, n and i there are all nasal, so the two forms come out sounding extremely alike. Naomi said much the same: to Japanese ears they’re almost identical.

Swaps that stuck. Real swaps happen too, the dictionary says, like a child’s とうもころし for とうもろこし; and some stick, like あたらしい, which in Old Japanese was あらたし. An encyclopedia adds 舌鼓, したつづみ, often said したづつみ (another dictionary calls that a mistake), and English “bird”, once “brid”. And 山茶花, さざんか, was first read さんざか; さざんか turns up from the 1600s and eventually took over.

雰囲気ふんいきのいいお店みせを、友達ともだちに薦すすめられた。

A friend recommended a place with a nice atmosphere.

Roppongi Hills

Beyond the lesson: what really is at the top, and the club on 51

Beyond the lessonThe tower. Roppongi Hills, built by Mori Building, opened on 25 April 2003. Its symbol is Mori Tower, an office skyscraper of 54 floors, 238 metres tall.

What really is at the top. Naomi was right: Roppongi Hills’ own site calls the 53rd floor the top floor, and that’s the Mori Art Museum, which opened on 18 October 2003. Below it, on the 52nd, is the observation deck Tokyo City View, 250 metres above sea level; and on the 51st, the Roppongi Hills Club, a members’ club with its own restaurants, open “only to a limited few”. Masami could at least have said the 52nd: a museum café opened there in 2003, and today that floor has a restaurant, and a café for anyone holding a ticket to the deck or the museum.

The price. The club’s fee schedule from April 2008, the year of the lesson, shows ¥1,260,000 to join, a ¥500,000 deposit returned when you leave, and ¥189,000 a year, tax included. So Naomi’s “over a million yen” was right, but the ¥200,000 was a yearly fee, not a monthly one. Membership then was for individuals aged 20 or over; corporate membership appears in a later schedule, from 2010. Today it’s ¥1,760,000 to join and ¥264,000 a year.

ヒルズ族. The building gave Japanese a word: ヒルズ族, the people living in Roppongi Hills’ residential towers, many of them young and rich, from start-up founders to celebrities and models, whose flashy lifestyle drew attention, the dictionary says. It was one of the nominees for the 2005 buzzword awards.

森美術館もりびじゅつかんは、森もりタワーの最さい上じょう階かいにある。

The Mori Art Museum is on the top floor of Mori Tower.

Quiz

Pick the word or form that fits. Each answer comes with the reason.

1. 「はじめまして。よろしくお願ねがいします。」「こちら＿＿＿、よろしくお願ねがいします。」

“Nice to meet you.” “Likewise, nice to meet you.”

こちらこそ: “no, it’s me who should say that”, the standard reply.

2. 「早はやく謝あやまってよ。」「君きみ＿＿＿、先さきに謝あやまれよ。」

“Apologise, will you!” “You’re the one who should apologise first!”

こそ turns it back on the other person: YOU, not me.

3. 今年ことし＿＿＿、絶対ぜったいに合格ごうかくする。

This year, for sure, I’m going to pass.

今年こそ: “this year, of all years”.

4. 毎日まいにち練れん習しゅうした＿＿＿、優ゆう勝しょうできたんだ。

It’s precisely because we practised every day that we won.

〜からこそ: “precisely because”.

5. 旅たびは、自分じぶんの目めで見み＿＿＿意味いみがある。

Travel only means something if you see things with your own eyes.

〜てこそ: “only by doing it does it count”.

6. ＿＿＿、がんばりましょう。

Let’s both do our best.

お互い: each other, both of us.

7. このカフェは、＿＿＿がいいね。

This café has a nice atmosphere.

雰囲気: atmosphere, mood. 天気 is the weather; 元気, being well.

8. ホテルの＿＿＿から、富士山ふじさんが見みえた。

From the hotel’s top floor, we could see Mount Fuji.

最上階: the top floor. 地下 is the basement.

9. ここからの＿＿＿は、最高さいこうだ。

The view from here is amazing.

眺め: a view. 景気 is the economy; 見学, a study visit.

10. この店みせの＿＿＿は何なんですか。

What do you recommend here?

おすすめ: a recommendation. おみやげ is a souvenir; おつり, change.

11. 姉あねは、町まちでいちばんの＿＿＿だった。

My older sister was the most beautiful woman in town.

美人 is used for women; 美男子 is for men, and 美術 means art.

12. 二人ふたりとも、とても＿＿＿そうだった。

They both looked very happy.

幸せそう: looking happy. 幸い means luckily; 幸運, good luck.

13. 料理りょうりもおいしい＿＿、店員てんいんさんも親切しんせつだし、また来きたい。

The food’s good and the staff are kind, so I want to come again.

〜し〜し stacks up reasons, as in lesson 7.

14. 映画えいがは＿＿＿けど、いいデートだった。

The film was boring, but it was a good date.

The past of an い-adjective: つまらない → つまらなかった.
