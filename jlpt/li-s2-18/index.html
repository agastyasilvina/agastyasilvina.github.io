How about a movie, or something?

Part 1 of a new story, “Going on a Date”, told from the woman’s side. Yuuko tries to chat up two men, fails with a stammer and then with a tough-guy act, and finally gets somewhere by talking about a novel. The grammar point is でも in a suggestion: 映画でも観に行かない？, “a movie or something”, which leaves the other person room to say what they’d like. Plus the other jobs of でも, the talk of a pick-up (ナンパ, イケメン, キモッ, 〜かい), and Japan’s 喫茶店.

The dialogue

The whole scene, retold for review

The lesson’s dialogue, retold in my own wording so you can review it in one place. It keeps the same scene, characters, words and grammar, adding いまどき from the word list. Every line is rephrased, and the last two are new: Takao has some advice about Yuuko’s opening line. The lesson’s exact key lines are in section 3. Scene

Outside a bookshop on a Saturday afternoon. Yuuko works up the nerve.

Yuuko

す、す、す、すみません！あ、あの……おひとりですか？もしよかったら、ナ、ナ、ナンパ、させてください！

Ex-ex-excuse me! Um… are you on your own? If you don’t mind, l-l-let me chat you up!

A man

うわっ……キモッ。

Ugh… creepy.

Yuuko

はあ……。あれじゃダメか。よし、次つぎはあの人ひとで行いこう！

Sigh… That was no good. Right, that one next!

Yuuko

よう、そこのイケメン！暇ひまかい？映画えいがでも観みに行いかないかい？チケットならあるぜ。

Yo, handsome! Got a minute? Fancy coming to a movie or something? I’ve got tickets.

Takao

はあ？おめえ、何なにそれ。気取きどってんの？いまどき、そんなナンパ、誰だれもしねえよ。

Huh? What’s that meant to be? Trying to act cool? Nobody chats people up like that these days.

Yuuko

うう、これもダメか……。あれ？その本ほん、もしかして村上春樹むらかみはるきの新作しんさくじゃないですか？

Ugh, that didn’t work either… Oh? That book — is it Murakami Haruki’s new one, by any chance?

Takao

あ、はい。まだ半分はんぶんですけど、今回こんかいのは今いままでで一番いちばんかもしれません。

Oh — yes. I’m only halfway, but this one might be the best yet.

Yuuko

へえ、いいなあ。私わたしも早はやく買かわなくちゃ。

Ooh, lucky you. I’d better hurry up and buy it too.

Takao

村上春樹むらかみはるきの作品さくひん、好すきなんですか？

So you like Murakami Haruki’s books?

Yuuko

好すきなんてもんじゃないですよ。大だいファンです！

Like isn’t the word. I’m a huge fan!

Takao

本当ほんとうですか？俺おれもなんですよ！

Really? So am I!

Yuuko

じゃあ、よかったら、そこの喫茶店きっさてんでケーキでも食たべながら、村上春樹むらかみはるきの話はなしでもしませんか？

Then, if you like, shall we have some cake or something at the café over there, and talk about Murakami?

Takao

いいですね。行いきましょう！

Sounds good. Let’s go!

Takao

……あ、でも、さっきの「暇ひまかい？」は、やめたほうがいいですよ。

…Oh, but I’d drop that “got a minute?” line.

Yuuko

……はい。次つぎからは、普通ふつうに誘さそいます。

…Right. Next time I’ll just ask normally.

2

The scene

Naomi, Yuichi and Peter, before and after the dialogue

A new series, “Going on a Date”. Yuichi’s favourite topic, Naomi teases; very instructive, Yuichi agrees (大変参考になります). The story is told from the woman’s side, Peter explains, and each lesson also has a bonus track retelling it from the man’s, so you can compare male and female speech, a very important part of Japanese. Which is why Naomi and Yuichi are both in the studio; Peter hesitates over Yuichi, Naomi asks whether Yuichi isn’t a man, and Peter insists on having said nothing. The grammar point is でも, as in コーヒーでも飲みましょう, for asking someone to do something. What’s happening? 女の子が男の子を引っかけようとしています, Yuichi says: a girl is trying to pick up a guy. That’s unusual, says Naomi.

After the dialogue

It’s 逆ナンパ, Yuichi says: a reverse pick-up, the opposite of what usually happens, and Peter shrugs, 21世紀だし, “well, it’s the 21st century.” Peter’s English for ナンパ, “to try and pick up girls”, proves Naomi’s point: hence 逆, reverse. According to Wikipedia, Naomi says, katakana ナンパ has two meanings: a man calling out to a woman, and a city in Idaho. It exists, Peter confirms, though it isn’t famous. Japan has a few places with ナンパ in their name too, and the best known is ナンパ橋, the “pick-up bridge” in Osaka’s 道頓堀. Along the way, Peter calls Yuichi an イケメン, and asks Naomi to translate: すごいいい男です.

The Osaka chat-up line, Sachiko has told Naomi, is 「ちゃーしばかへんか？」: Osaka dialect, Yuichi says, for what standard Japanese puts as お茶でもしませんか？ Naomi offers お茶一緒に飲みませんか？, and Peter doubts anyone says that kind of thing now: fifty years ago, maybe, or a hundred, says Naomi. Peter’s Japanese teacher at university, sixty, seventy or eighty at the time, probably still talks like that. Wouldn’t you say お茶飲まない？ or お茶しない？, Naomi asks? Not really, says Yuichi; girls might, but 男の子はお茶をしに行きません, guys don’t go out for tea. That’s the fundamental problem, Naomi concludes. And what does Yuichi say when picking someone up? Yuichi doesn’t, and if Yuichi did, もしもナンパをするならば、考えたことがありません, which, Yuichi admits, makes no sense.

Phrases worth taking from the chat:

  • 大変参考になります — “that’s very instructive”: 参考, reference.
  • 引っかける — literally “to hook”: to pick someone up.
  • 逆ナンパ — a woman chatting up a man: 逆, reverse.
  • 21世紀だし — “it’s the 21st century, after all”: し giving a reason, as in lesson 7.
  • お茶でもしませんか？ — “shall we go for tea or something?”: お茶する, to go for a drink.
  • 根本的な問題 — a fundamental problem.

Key lines

The tough-guy act, the brush-off, and the invitation that works

The lesson’s dialogue is fourteen lines long, but many are a word or two, so these three are quoted exactly; section 1 covers the rest in my retelling. The English is my own rendering.

おい、そこのイケメン、一人ひとりかい？もしよかったら、私わたしと映画えいがでも観みに行いかないかい？

Hey, handsome, all on your own? If you like, why don’t you come and see a movie or something with me? — Yuuko

Grammar: おい, a rough “hey”; イケメン, slang for a good-looking guy (section 7); 一人かい？, with かい, a friendly question ending that sounds like a man, part of Yuuko’s act; もしよかったら, “if you like”; 映画でも, “a movie or something” (section 5); 観に行かないかい, “won’t you go and see”.

はぁ？おめ、何なに気取きどってんだよ？そんなナンパするやつ見みたことねぇよ。

Huh? Who do you think you are? I’ve never seen anyone try to pick someone up like that. — Takao

Grammar: おめ, a rough お前 (lesson 17); 何気取ってんだよ, from 何を気取っているんだよ, with the を dropped and ているんだ squeezed into てんだ; やつ, a rough word for a person; 見たことねぇ, 見たことない with ない turned into ねぇ.

もしよかったら、喫茶店きっさてんでコーヒーを飲のみながら村上春樹むらかみはるきの作品さくひんの話はなしでもしませんか？

If you like, shall we go to a café and talk about Murakami Haruki’s books over a coffee? — Yuuko

Grammar: 喫茶店, a coffee shop (section 8); 〜ながら, “while”; 作品の話, talk about the books; 話でもしませんか, “shall we have a chat or something”, the polite 〜ませんか invitation softened by でも. Back in polite Japanese, Yuuko finally gets a yes.

Vocabulary

The lesson’s word list, in its order

The hosts’ notes first, then a card for each word, plus more from the dialogue and the chat, each with an example of my own.

  • ナンパ — to pick someone up, to pull.
  • だめ (駄目) — no good, hopeless, doesn’t work. It’s a な-adjective: ダメな人, a hopeless person. Then だめでもともと, “there’s nothing to lose”, used like a proverb: もともと is “from the beginning”. Yuichi’s example: だめでもともと、JLPTの1級を受けてみよう, “I’ll probably fail, but I’ll try JLPT Level 1 anyway.” Level 1 is hard, Yuichi says; for foreigners, Peter adds, though Yuichi would be fine. Of course, says Yuichi, and Naomi heads off a misunderstanding: it isn’t that it’s hard for Japanese people. A high-school student could pass it, Yuichi says. A friend of Peter’s, Ryo, once answered a compliment, 「かっこいい！」, with 「え、もともとだよ」, “I’ve always been like this”: since birth, as Yuichi puts it, and a joke, of course. The short form is だめもと: ダメもとでテストを受けてみよう. And the kanji 駄 is a horse (馬) plus “fat” (太): a fat horse is no use, Yuichi reasons, unless you eat it, says Peter. But the word, Naomi says, comes from the game of Go.
  • きどる (気取る) — to put on airs, act superior. 気取った人 and 気取っている人 mean the same: a pompous person, いやな人, Yuichi says. The negative, 気取らない, “unpretentious”, is usually a compliment: Naomiさんは気取らない人で話しやすいです, Naomi’s own example, “Naomi is down to earth and easy to talk to.” Peter tries 気取っている先生, then 気取らない先生, and Naomi says it sounds fake.
  • いまどき — now, these days: いま and とき, the same as 現在 or 最近.
  • もしかして — perhaps, possibly, “by any chance”: もしかして、村上春樹先生ですか？, Yuichi’s example. The lesson notes use もしかして、トムクルーズさんですか, as if from lesson 13.
  • さくひん (作品) — a work, a piece of work: 作 as in 作る, and 品, しな, goods or a product, so “a thing made”. これは誰の作品ですか？, “whose work is this?”; モネの作品です, “it’s a Monet.” In a gallery, the hosts agree, it’s usually written on the wall.
  • きっさてん (喫茶店) — a coffee shop. Yuichi takes the kanji apart: 喫, to eat or drink; 茶, tea, read ちゃ or さ, as in 茶道, the tea ceremony; and 店, a shop. Nagoya’s coffee shops are famous, Naomi says, for “morning” service: order a coffee and toast, an egg, and something sweet like castella come with it, about ¥350 for breakfast. If you’re ever in Nagoya, try one.

The lesson notes add 次, next; 彼女, “she”, and also how you call out to a girl you’re chatting up; 映画, 観に行く, 今回, 大ファン and 話. The cards below cover them all, with more words from the dialogue and the chat.

Beyond the lesson駄目 really is from Go. The dictionary calls the Go sense the original one: a point on the border between the two players’ territory that belongs to neither, so a stone played there gains nothing. One dictionary cites it from 1275. The game also gave 駄目を押す: filling in those neutral points, and so making doubly sure, or, in sport, piling on more points when the game is already won (駄目押し).

The fat horse is a memory aid. 駄 is really a pack horse, or the load it carries (駄賃, 荷駄). Pack horses were seen as low-grade horses, not fit for riding, so 駄 came to mark things of little worth: 駄菓子, cheap sweets; 駄作, a poor piece of work; 駄洒落, a bad pun. As for the 太 inside, a kanji dictionary says the character is built for sound, with 大 giving the reading; its traditional form is 馱.

だめでもともと, says the dictionary, means that if you try and fail, you’re no worse off than if you’d done nothing, and it’s said to urge someone to go for it; 駄目元 is its short form. Yuichi’s 1級 is the old JLPT’s Level 1: since the 2010 revision, the JLPT says, anyone able to pass the old Level 1 can pass today’s N1.

いまどき and 彼女. One dictionary gives いまどき a pointed use: “in this day and age”, for something that seems out of place now, as in Takao’s いまどき、そんなナンパ… in my retelling. The same dictionary lists 彼女 as slang men use to address a young woman they’re talking to, ねえ、彼女, and notes that かのじょ only spread as an everyday pronoun from the Taishō era (1912–26).

観る. The lesson notes write 観に行く, but Japan’s official list of everyday kanji gives 観 only its on-reading, カン; the list’s spelling is 見る. The dictionary accepts 観る as a way to write みる, marked as a reading outside that list.

5

〜でも, “or something”

The grammar point: making an invitation easy to answer

When you invite someone, or ask them to do something, でも makes it polite, Peter explains, because it leaves them other options. 私と映画でも見に行かない？: “would you like to see a movie or something with me?” It doesn’t have to be a movie; let’s do something together. お茶でも doesn’t have to be tea, just something to drink, and コーヒーでも stays in the drinks genre, Peter says: you wouldn’t end up at a 焼き肉 place, Naomi points out, and if Peter suggested お茶でも and took Naomi for yakiniku, that would be strange. Yuichi’s example: 来週の日曜日、動物園でも行きませんか？, “shall we go to the zoo or something next Sunday?”

1

Noun + でも, in a suggestion

映画でも観に行かない？ A movie, or something like it: the other person can suggest something else.

2

It takes the place of が or を

コーヒーを飲む → コーヒーでも飲む. After other particles it follows them: 散歩にでも行こうか.

3

か何か does a similar job

お茶か何か, “tea or something”. Naomi’s addition, with Yuichi’s ビールか何か飲みませんか？

The lesson notes compare it with a plain particle. 映画でも観に行かない？ doesn’t insist on the movie; 映画を観に行かない？ proposes the movie, and nothing else. Leaving the choice open is what makes it polite, and the notes say the same of か何か: トランプか何か、やろうか？, “shall we play cards or something?”

The suggestionWhat it says
を映画えいがを観みに行いかない？let’s see a movie
でも映画えいがでも観みに行いかない？a movie, or something else you’d like
か何か映画えいがか何なにか観みに行いかない？a movie or something, said outright

Watch forお茶か何か、いただけますか？ asks for something for yourself: いただく is the humble “receive”, so it means “could I have some tea or something?”, as the lesson notes put it. The English read in the audio, “would you like tea or something?”, would be an offer: お茶か何か、いかがですか？

Beyond the lessonでも or か何か? A 1998 study by the linguist 森山卓郎 finds them close but not the same. Both offer one example that the listener may swap for something else: 眠気覚ましにコーヒーか何か／でも飲みなさい。いやならほかのものでもいいけど, “have a coffee or something to keep you awake; something else is fine if you’d rather.” Neither works when it has to be exactly that thing: nobody tells you to wash 手でも, “your hands or something”, after using the toilet. The difference is in how the sentence ends. か何か can report what happened, 太郎は買い物か何かにでかけた, “Taro went out shopping or something”, but 彼はコーヒーでも飲んだ doesn’t work. A 2023 paper by 蓮沼昭子 sums up where this でも can go: wishes, intentions, invitations, suggestions, requests and offers, all things not yet done, and sentences whose truth is still open, such as ifs, guesses and questions.

暑あついですね。冷つめたいものでも飲のみませんか。

Hot, isn’t it? Shall we have something cold to drink?

雨あめがやんだら、散歩さんぽにでも行いこうか。

When the rain stops, shall we go for a walk or something?

お腹なかすいた？クッキーか何なにか食たべる？

Hungry? Want a biscuit or something?

The other でもs

Beyond the lesson: what the dictionary says, softer suggestions, and でも’s other jobs

Beyond the lessonWhat the dictionaries say. One describes this でも as naming something without being definite, as just one example: けがでもしたら大変だ, “it’d be awful if you got hurt or something”; 兄にでも相談するか, “maybe I’ll ask my brother or someone.” Another adds that a vague example tends to be something light, so the thing named sounds like a small matter. That suits an invitation: a grammar module from the Tokyo University of Foreign Studies says でも can soften what you say, with the example お茶でも飲みませんか. The grammarian 寺村秀夫 called it 提案のデモ, the “suggestion” でも.

なんか and など. なんか gives an example too, 映画なんかよく行く, “I often go to films and things”, but it can also make light of something: 彼の言うことなんか聞くな, “don’t listen to a word he says.” So 映画なんかどう？ works, but mind the tone. など can soften too, お茶などいかがですか, but the 1998 study finds a difference: グリーンカレーなどを食べませんか suggests other dishes as well, while the でも version carries no hint of more than one.

お茶する is in the dictionary: to have tea, especially to drop into a coffee shop for tea or coffee, 友達とお茶してから帰る; it dates from the 1990s, the dictionary says. Sachiko’s Osaka version, ちゃーしばかへんか, is 茶をしばかへんか: しばく means to whack with a stick, and the phrase itself isn’t in the dictionaries I checked. The same でも does other jobs too:

ExampleMeaning
or somethingお茶ちゃでも飲のもうthis lesson: one example, others welcome
even子こどもでもわかるeven a child understands
any-誰だれでも, 何なんでも, いつでもanyone, anything, any time
butでも、時間じかんがないat the start of a sentence: “but”

この店みせのケーキは、何なんでもおいしい。

Every cake in this shop is good.

そんなこと、子こどもでも知しっているよ。

Even a child knows that.

7

Pick-up talk

Beyond the lesson: ナンパ, イケメン, キモッ, and talking like a man

Beyond the lessonナンパ was a political word. 軟派, the “soft faction”, is a Meiji-era coinage, at first a political term: a group without firm views, the opposite of 硬派, the hard-liners. Newspapers used the pair too, 軟派 for the culture, entertainment and society pages and 硬派 for politics and economics. 軟派 also came to mean a liking for dating and dressing up, which 硬派 scorned as soft, playing up muscle and manliness instead; and by extension, calling out to someone in the street. One dictionary defines that as a man inviting a woman, hence the 逆 when a woman does it; another dates its earliest example to 1984.

The bridge in Dōtonbori is 戎橋, known as ひっかけ橋, “pick-up bridge”, because so many people used to chat others up on it, an Osaka tourism site says. That’s Yuichi’s 引っかける from the introduction, which one dictionary illustrates with 街で若い娘を引っかける. And Naomi’s second meaning is real: kotobank’s dictionary entry for katakana ナンパ is Nampa, Idaho, a city of 100,200 at the 2020 US census, the state’s third largest.

イケメン is いけ, from いけてる, “cool”, plus めん, a pun on 面, “face”, and English “men”. It has never won Japan’s buzzword awards, but in 2010 the top ten included イクメン, men who enjoy raising their children, named as a pun on イケメン. キモい is slang for 気持ち悪い, “creepy”, and キモッ is the same clipped exclamation as 寒っ in lesson 16.

Talking like a man. Yuuko’s act runs on what the linguist 金水敏 calls 役割語, “role language”: ways of talking that go with a type of person, such as their sex, age or job. In it, おい and こら are for men only; men say 行くぜ or 行くぞ where women say 行くわ; and dropping だ turns men’s 今日は雨だよ into women’s 今日は雨よ, the pattern of Yuuko’s own ダメよ. A dictionary agrees that in women’s speech よ often follows a noun or a な-adjective directly, and calls ぜ men’s language, a little rough; a grammar module marks かい as mainly men’s. But these differences took shape in Meiji-era Tokyo, Kinsui writes, and you’ll find them in plenty in fiction, while real men and women today often don’t talk that differently. A review of 中村桃子’s 翻訳がつくる日本語 (2013) opens with heroines in translation, Scarlett O’Hara’s だわ and Ripley’s のよ in Alien, and notes that few real women talk like that: according to the author, some women no longer use あたし, かしら, だわ, わよ or のよ at all, and this “women’s language” lives on in translation.

よう、映画えいがでも観みに行いかないかい？

Yo, fancy a movie or something? (the tough-guy act)

よかったら、映画えいがでも観みに行いきませんか？

If you like, shall we go and see a movie or something? (polite, for anyone)

The 喫茶店

Beyond the lesson: the kanji, the first coffee house, and Nagoya’s mornings

Beyond the lessonThe kanji. 喫 means to eat or drink (喫茶; 喫煙, smoking; 満喫, enjoying to the full), and also to receive, usually something unwelcome: 惨敗を喫する, to suffer a crushing defeat. 茶 is read チャ, or サ in words such as 茶菓 and 喫茶. 茶道 is a curious case: one dictionary heads it ちゃどう and notes that today it’s usually さどう; another says さどう was rare up to the end of the Edo period, when ちゃどう was the normal reading. Japan’s oldest book on tea, 栄西’s 喫茶養生記, was written in 1211 and revised in 1214.

The first coffee house. The name that usually comes up, says the National Diet Library, is 可否茶館, opened in Ueno, Tokyo, in 1888. Its owner, 鄭永慶, had studied in America and wanted a salon for ordinary people and students, at a time when the Rokumeikan was where high society met. Its advertisements promised newspapers, magazines, playing cards, Go, shogi and even cricket, plus a writing room with paper and pens: トランプ and Go, both in this lesson. It was ahead of its time; customers were few, and it soon closed. One encyclopedia names an even earlier coffee shop, 洗愁亭, in 1886.

Counting cafés. Japan’s coffee shops peaked in 1981 at 154,630, according to government surveys compiled by the All Japan Coffee Association; in 2021 there were 58,669. Osaka had the most (6,758), then Aichi (6,171) and Tokyo (6,121). And the hosts were right about Nagoya: in the government’s household survey for 2023–2025, Nagoya households of two or more spent the most, on average, on 喫茶代, non-alcoholic drinks, sweets and fruit eaten out, at ¥16,431 a year, ahead of Tokyo’s wards (¥16,270) and Gifu (¥14,663). The national average was ¥10,261.

The morning. 一宮, in Aichi, calls itself the birthplace of モーニング. In the textile boom of the late 1950s, weavers were in and out of coffee shops day and night, and a kind owner quietly added a boiled egg and peanuts to the coffee, says Ichinomiya’s morning council. 一宮モーニング became a registered regional brand in 2016, and its rules include an egg dish, as at the start. Nobody really knows, though: the council’s own history quotes the head of Aichi’s coffee-shop trade association, “there are many theories, but nothing is certain”, and its hunt for the first shop came up empty. Hiroshima’s ルーエぶらじる and the Toyohashi area claim the title too. In Ichinomiya today, Aichi’s tourism site says, the price of a drink, ¥300 to ¥500 for a coffee, still brings a free set of bread, egg dishes and so on.

And the law. Until June 2021, Japan’s food-sanitation law had a separate business permit called 喫茶店営業; since then it has been folded into 飲食店営業, the restaurant permit. Most of those permits weren’t for cafés at all: of 209,604 in fiscal 2016, 182,032 were for vending machines.

名古屋なごやの喫茶店きっさてんで、モーニングを食たべてみたい。

I’d like to try a morning set at a Nagoya coffee shop.

Quiz

Pick the word or form that fits. Each answer comes with the reason.

1. 疲つかれたね。ちょっとコーヒー＿＿＿飲のもうか。

Tired? Shall we have a coffee or something?

でも: “a coffee or something”, a suggestion that leaves room. しか needs a negative, and さえ means “even”.

2. お茶ちゃ＿＿＿、いただけますか。

Could I have some tea or something?

か何か: “or something”. か誰か is “or someone”, かどこか “or somewhere”.

3. 今度こんどの休やすみ、温泉おんせんに＿＿＿行いかない？

How about going to a hot spring or somewhere next holiday?

After に, でも follows the particle: 温泉にでも, “a hot spring or somewhere”.

4. 今夜こんやは、あのレストラン＿＿＿予約よやくしておいたよ。

I’ve booked that restaurant for tonight.

A plain statement about one thing: を. でも and か何か are for suggestions that leave room.

5. ＿＿＿、山田やまださんじゃありませんか。

Excuse me, are you Yamada-san, by any chance?

もしかして: “by any chance”, for a guess you aren’t sure of.

6. あの人ひとは有名ゆうめいなのに、全然ぜんぜん＿＿＿。

That person is famous, but not stuck-up at all.

全然 wants a negative: 気取らない, “unpretentious”, a compliment.

7. ＿＿＿で、社しゃ長ちょうに直ちょく接せつメールしてみた。

I had nothing to lose, so I tried emailing the company president directly.

だめもと, short for だめでもともと: “nothing to lose”.

8. ＿＿＿、手紙てがみを書かく人ひとは少すくない。

These days, few people write letters.

いまどき: “these days”.

9. これは、ピカソの初期しょきの＿＿＿です。

This is one of Picasso’s early works.

作品: a work. 作者 is the person who made it; 作文, a school essay.

10. 駅前えきまえの＿＿＿で、コーヒーを飲のみながら待まっています。

I’ll be waiting at the coffee shop by the station, having a coffee.

喫茶店: a coffee shop. 喫煙所 is a smoking area, and 美術館 an art gallery.

11. 明日あしたテストだから、今夜こんやは勉べん強きょうし＿＿＿。

There’s a test tomorrow, so I’ve got to study tonight.

〜なくちゃ, short for 〜なくては: “have to”.

12. そんな簡単かんたんなこと、子こども＿＿＿わかるよ。

Something that simple? Even a child would get it.

Noun + でも can also mean “even”: 子どもでも.

13. ＿＿＿でも好すきなものを頼たのんでいいよ。

Order anything you like.

Question word + でも: 何でも, “anything at all”.

14. 祖父そふは、あの歌手かしゅの＿＿ファンです。

My grandfather is a huge fan of that singer.

大ファン: a big fan. 太 is “fat”, the kanji in the hosts’ joke about 駄.
