Made to read? Let them choose.

Part two of lesson 3’s parents’ meeting. This time a parent talks with the teacher: to keep reading from feeling forced, the parent chooses books together with the child, even when the child’s picks are a letdown. The grammar point is the causative-passive, させられる, “to be made to”: 読書させられる, “to be made to read”. It has a gentler use for feelings too: がっかりさせられる, “to be let down”, and 実感させられる, “to have something brought home to you”.

Scene

The same parents’ meeting, now at question time. The principal turns to the room, and one parent puts up a hand.

Principal

では、お子こさんに読書どくしょの習しゅう慣かんをつけさせるために、ご家庭かていでどんな工夫くふうをなさっているか、どなたか聞きかせていただけませんか。

Now, could one of you tell us what you do at home to get your children into the habit of reading?

Parent

はい。うちでは、子こどもが「無理むりやり読よまされている」と感かんじないように、読よむ本ほんは自分じぶんで選えらばせるようにしています。

Yes. At home, so that our child never feels forced to read, we make a point of letting them pick their books themselves.

Principal

それはいいですね。人ひとに言いわれて読よむのではなく、自じ主しゅ的てきに読よむことが何なにより大切たいせつですから。

That’s good. Reading of their own accord, not because someone told them to, matters more than anything.

Parent

ただ、正しょう直じきなところ、子こどもが選えらんでくる本ほんには、がっかりさせられることもあるんです。毎回まいかい、恐きょう竜りゅうの図鑑ずかんばかりで……。

Though to be honest, the books my child comes back with can be a letdown. Every single time, it’s dinosaur encyclopaedias…

Principal

図鑑ずかんでも漫画まんがでも構かまいませんよ。口くちを出ださずに、選えらんだ本ほんをそのまま読よませてあげてください。

Encyclopaedias, comics, it doesn’t matter. Don’t step in: let them read the books they chose, just as they are.

Parent

そうなんですよね。この間あいだも、息子むすこが嬉うれしそうに図鑑ずかんをめくる姿すがたに、やっぱり好すきな本ほんを読よむのがいちばんなんだと実感じっかんさせられました。

That’s just it. Only the other day, the sight of my son happily turning the pages of his encyclopaedia brought it home to me: reading what you love really is what counts.

Principal

ありがとうございます。ほかのご家庭かていは、いかがでしょうか。

Thank you. How about other families?

Back row, thinking

（図鑑ずかんならまだいいわよ。うちの子こなんて、ゲームの攻こう略りゃく本ぼんしか読よまないんだから。）

(Encyclopaedias, I’d take that. Mine reads nothing but game walkthroughs.)

Natsuko, Naomi and Peter, before and after the dialogue

Peter opens with the title, “Making the Rugrats Read, Part 2”: if you missed part one, listen to that first. (That’s lesson 3.) Who’s talking? Since it carries on from last time, Natsuko explains, it’s the teacher and the parents (保護者) again, but today they have a conversation. Last time, Peter sums up, the teacher gave a speech to the parents; this time a parent talks with the teacher. The topic is reading again (読書), and this time both sides use polite Japanese.

After the dialogue

Where do the hosts buy books? Naomi buys them at a bookshop, but gets heavy books and books in English from Amazon. Natsuko uses Amazon a lot too. How many books a month, Peter asks Natsuko? “Don’t ask.” Surely more than ten? Lately, Natsuko says, the plan is not to buy any, which Naomi finds impressive. Naomi buys books in batches, all at once. And because books in Japan aren’t discounted, Naomi also goes to used-book shops (古本屋): clever, says Natsuko. Peter adds that at shops like Book-Off, which sell used books, you can get some very good deals. (Why no discounts? See section 8.)

Phrases worth taking from the chat:

  • 聞かないで下さい — “don’t ask”: 〜ないで下さい, “please don’t…”.
  • 絶対10冊以上でしょ？ — “it’s got to be more than ten, right?”: 絶対, “definitely”, with でしょ checking a guess.
  • 最近買わないようにしてるんです — “lately I’m trying not to buy any”: 〜ないようにする, “to make a point of not doing” (more in section 3).
  • まとめて買います — “I buy them all at once.”
  • 古本屋に行ったりしますね — “I go to used-book shops and so on”: 〜たりする gives one example among others.
  • かしこいですね — “that’s smart.”

Key lines

The parent’s two lines that carry the grammar

The lesson’s dialogue is only six lines long, so just these two are quoted exactly; section 1 covers the rest in my retelling. The English is my own rendering.

子こどもが、無理むりやり読書どくしょさせられたと感かんじないように、子こどもと一緒いっしょに本ほんを選えらぶようにしています。

So that my child never feels forced into reading, I make a point of choosing books together with them. — the parent

Grammar: 読書させられた, the causative-passive of 読書する: “was made to read” (section 5); 無理やり, “against their will”, a word that loves the causative (section 4); 〜と感じないように, “so that they don’t feel that…”, with the ように of “so that”; 〜ようにしています, “I make a point of…”.

Watch forThe English read out in the lesson’s audio has the parent trying “to read to the children”. The Japanese doesn’t say that, and the lesson notes get it right: 本を選ぶ is choosing books, and the parent does it together with the child (子どもと一緒に).

嬉うれしそうに読よむ姿すがたに、やっぱり読よみたい本ほんを読よむのが大切たいせつだと実感じっかんさせられました。

Watching how happily they read brought it home to me that, after all, what matters is reading the books you want to read. — the parent

Grammar: 嬉しそうに, “happily”: 〜そう, “looking…”, used as an adverb; 読む姿, “the sight of them reading” (section 4); 姿に, with に marking what caused the feeling; 実感させられました, the causative-passive used for a feeling (section 7); やっぱり, “after all, just as you’d think”.

Beyond the lessonTwo kinds of ように meet in the first line. 感じないように is “so that they don’t feel”, a purpose. 選ぶようにしています is 〜ようにする, “to make a point of doing”, and Natsuko uses its negative in the chat: 買わないようにしてるんです, “I’m trying not to buy any”. Use 〜ようにしている for a habit you keep up, and 〜ようにする for a resolution:

  • 寝る前にスマホを見ないようにしている。 — I make a point of not looking at my phone before bed.
  • これからは、毎日少しずつ読むようにします。 — From now on, I’ll make sure I read a little every day.
4

Vocabulary

The lesson’s word list, in its order

The hosts’ notes first, then a card for each word, plus a few more from the dialogue and the chat, each with an example of my own.

  • 習慣 (しゅうかん) — custom, habit. Careful, Natsuko warns: it sounds exactly like 週間, a week’s span, as in 1週間, “one week”.
  • 無理やり — forcibly, against someone’s will: 無理やりドアを開けた, “I forced the door open.” It often goes with the causative, Naomi notes, and Natsuko gives two: 無理やり本を読ませる, “to make someone read a book against their will”; 無理やりビールを飲ませる, “to make someone drink beer against their will”. Surely that never happens to Natsuko? Never, Natsuko says: 無理やり飲まされたことはありません。いつも自主的に飲んでます, “I’ve never been made to drink. I always drink of my own free will.” Which, Naomi notes, is the next word.
  • 自主的 — voluntary, of your own accord. 自主的な is the adjective and 自主的に the adverb: 自主的に道をそうじする, “to clean the streets voluntarily”.
  • がっかりする — to be disappointed. Peter files がっかり under onomatopoeia, and Naomi adds that you sometimes hear がっくり instead: both cover being let down, deflated, feeling low. Naomi pictures your head dropping, ガクッ. The example: テストが0点だった。がっかりした, “I got zero on the test. I was so disappointed.” Didn’t they see that coming, Naomi wonders. Naomi also mentions a comedian, やっくん, who was always saying がっかりだよ！, “I’m so disappointed!”
  • 実感する — to feel something for real, to realise. 実 is reality, or fruit, and 感 feeling, so 実感 is a real feeling and 実感する to realise: 病気になって健康の大切さを実感した, “When I got ill, I realised how important health is.” Not a way Naomi would like to learn it.
  • 姿 — figure, appearance. The hosts find two kanji hidden in it: 次, “next”, on top, and 女, “woman”, underneath. So, “the next woman”? Peter offers a cheeky reading (the figure you notice while you’re still with your current partner), Natsuko is lost for words and invites listeners to comment with a better one, and Natsuko’s mnemonic follows: 次の女は姿で見つける, “you find the next woman by her figure”. Peter, playing the purist, announces 私はNatsukoさんとNaomi先生にがっかりしています, “I’m disappointed in Natsuko and Naomi-sensei”, and calls it kanji abuse. Which leads straight to the grammar.

Beyond the lessonThree しゅうかん. 習慣, “habit”, 週間, “a week’s span”, and 週刊, “weekly”, as in 週刊誌, “a weekly magazine”, are all read しゅうかん. Pitch accent won’t tell them apart either: in standard Japanese all three are flat. Only context will. Two of them meet in 読書週間, Book Week, which has run every year since 1948 from 27 October to 9 November, the two weeks around Culture Day. This year’s, the 80th, starts on 27 October, with the slogan 静かだけど、冒険中。, “quiet, but on an adventure”.

無理やり is often written 無理矢理, but its 矢理 is there for the sound only, an ateji. Dictionaries spell the word 無理遣り: the やり is the verb やる, “to do”.

がっかり or がっくり? Dictionaries define がっかり as losing heart when your hopes come to nothing. がっくり starts in the body: something suddenly bending or giving way, as in がっくりとひざをつく, “to sink to your knees”, or a head dropping forward, just as Naomi pictured it. From there it covers a sudden loss of spirit, がっくりくる, and a sharp fall, as in 客足ががっくり減る, “customer numbers fall off sharply”.

Beyond the lessonThe real story of 姿. Sorry, Peter: the 次 is there for its sound. 姿 is a sound-and-meaning character, with 女 for the meaning and 次 for the reading, シ, which the two share. The Shuowen Jiezi, the classic Chinese character dictionary of about 100 AD, defines it as 態, “bearing, manner”. 資, “resources”, and 恣, “self-indulgent”, take their reading シ from 次 the same way. Words to know: 姿勢, “posture”; 容姿, “looks”; and 〜姿 for what someone has on, as in 着物姿, “in a kimono”.

The comedian was Sakurazuka Yakkun (桜塚やっくん). In a sailor-style school uniform and carrying a bamboo sword, Sakurazuka played Sukeban Kyōko (スケバン恐子), a schoolgirl gang leader whose catchphrase was がっかりだよ！, first on the TV show エンタの神様 in 2006. The lesson came out in early 2008, when the line was everywhere. Sakurazuka died in a road accident on an expressway in Yamaguchi in October 2013, aged 37.

5

させられる

The grammar point: the causative-passive, “to be made to”

Today’s grammar point is the causative-passive. Natsuko’s reaction: うわっ, “yikes”. It first appears in the parent’s line about children who might feel they were made to read: 子どもが無理やり読書させられた, “the children were forced to read”. Peter and Naomi build it up in three steps:

1

The causative: “make”

勉強させる is “make the kids study”. For reading, 読書する becomes 読書させる: する turns into させる.

2

Add who is made to do it

The person made to act takes に: 子どもたちに読書をさせる, “make the children read”.

3

Make it passive

Put the children first, as the subject, and add the passive: させる becomes させられる. 子どもが読書させられた: “the children were made to read”.

For the details, Peter says, see the lesson notes (section 6 has them here), but it’s an interesting construction, and Natsuko adds that it’s used quite a lot in Japanese. It often comes with がっかり, Naomi says: がっかりさせられる. When Peter said がっかりしました a moment ago, がっかりさせられました would have worked too. The hosts try it on each other: Naomiにがっかりさせられる, “to be let down by Naomi”, and Peterにがっかりさせられる, “to be let down by Peter” (purely as examples, Natsuko stresses). And one more, Peter remembers, the one you’ll hear most: 勉強させられる, “to be made to study”. Causative plus passive: made to do something.

子こどもの頃ころは、毎日まいにち塾じゅくで勉べん強きょうさせられていた。

As a kid, I was made to study at cram school every day.

罰ばつとして、放課後ほうかごに教きょう室しつを掃除そうじさせられた。

As a punishment, I was made to clean the classroom after school.

新人しんじんのころは、電話でんわ番ばんばかりさせられた。

When I was new, all they had me do was answer the phones.

嫌いやいやながら、弟おとうとにゲームの相手あいてをさせられた。

Much against my will, my little brother roped me into playing his game with him.

Watch forThree look-alike endings: 読ませる, the causative, “make or let someone read”; 読まれる, the passive, “be read”; and 読ませられる, the causative-passive, “be made to read”. With る-verbs, keep 食べさせられる, “be made to eat”, apart from 食べられる, which is “can eat” or “be eaten”.

Forming it

Beyond the lesson: any verb’s causative-passive, and the short form you’ll hear

Beyond the lessonThe hosts leave the details to the lesson notes, so here they are. Start from the causative (lesson 3, section 7). It’s a る-verb itself, so it takes the ordinary passive ending (Upper Beginner S1 #2): 読ませる → 読ませられる.

VerbMade toShort form
読よむ読よませられる読よまされる
飲のむ飲のませられる飲のまされる
待まつ待またせられる待またされる
行いく行いかせられる行いかされる
話はなす話はなさせられる—
食たべる食たべさせられる—
するさせられる—
来くる来こさせられる—
  • The short form. With う-verbs, 〜せられる usually shrinks to 〜される in speech: 読まされる, 飲まされる, 待たされる. Natsuko’s 無理やり飲まされた in section 4 is one. Verbs ending in す keep the full form: 話させられる, never 話さされる. In standard Japanese, る-verbs, する and 来る have only the full form too.
  • Who did what. The person made to act is the subject, with は or が; whoever made them takes に: 私は母に野菜を食べさせられた, “my mother made me eat my vegetables.” From the mother’s side it’s the plain causative: 母は私に野菜を食べさせた.
  • It means you didn’t want to. Made to wait, made to drink, made to apologise: that’s why 無理やり pairs with it so well. If someone let you do something you wanted to do, use 〜させてもらう instead: 父に車を運転させてもらった, “my dad let me drive the car.”
  • The other side of a shop phrase. お待たせしました, “sorry to keep you waiting”, is the causative 待たせる. The customer, meanwhile, 待たされた: was kept waiting.

彼女かのじょはいつも遅おくれてくる。今日きょうも三さん十じゅっ分ぷん待またされた。

She’s always late. Today she kept me waiting half an hour again.

先生せんせいに、みんなの前まえで作文さくぶんを読よまされた。

The teacher made me read my essay out in front of everyone.

日曜日にちようびなのに、上司じょうしに会社かいしゃへ行いかされた。

It was Sunday, but my boss made me go into the office.

初はじめて父ちちに車くるまを運転うんてんさせてもらった。

For the first time, my dad let me drive the car.

7

Made to feel

Beyond the lesson: がっかりさせられる, 実感させられる, 考えさせられる

Beyond the lessonTwo of the dialogue’s three causative-passives have nothing to do with being forced. With a verb of feeling or thinking, 〜させられる says the feeling came from outside: something made you feel it. Whatever caused it takes に: 子どもが選ぶ本にがっかりさせられる, “to be let down by the books the children choose”, and 嬉しそうに読む姿に…実感させられました, “the sight of them reading happily brought it home to me”.

The lesson itself uses three forms of がっかり. Peter: 私はNatsukoさんとNaomi先生にがっかりしています. Naomi: Peter could have said がっかりさせられました. And Natsuko, offering to give up on the lesson: がっかりされちゃったから, “now that Peter’s disappointed in us”. Here is the whole family, with the causative that links them:

FormMeaning
がっかりするto be disappointed
がっかりさせるto disappoint someone, let them down
がっかりさせられるto be let down by something or someone
がっかりされるto have someone be disappointed in you

So which is it, がっかりした or がっかりさせられた? Both describe the same disappointment. がっかりした simply reports how you felt; がっかりさせられた points at whatever caused it, with a hint that it was done to you. The same goes for a handful of other verbs, which turn up constantly in reviews and comment sections:

この本ほんには、考かんがえさせられることが多おおかった。

This book gave me a lot to think about.

考えさせられる: “it makes you think”. Almost a set phrase.

小ちいさな子こどもの集しゅう中ちゅう力りょくには、いつも感心かんしんさせられる。

Small children’s powers of concentration never fail to impress me.

感心させられる: to be impressed, to have to admire.

彼かれの日本語にほんごのうまさには、驚おどろかされた。

I was amazed at how good his Japanese was.

驚かされる: to be taken by surprise, the short form for 驚く.

試合しあいに負まけて、自分じぶんの実じつ力りょく不足ぶそくを思おもい知しらされた。

Losing the match made me painfully aware of how far I still have to go.

思い知らされる: to be made to see something, the hard way.

8

Books at a fixed price

Beyond the lesson: why Naomi hunts for used books

Beyond the lessonA price the publisher sets. Naomi is right: new books in Japan aren’t discounted, and the law allows it. The Antimonopoly Act normally stops a maker from fixing what shops charge, but article 23 makes an exception for copyrighted works (著作物): books, magazines, newspapers, records, music tapes and music CDs. Under this resale price maintenance, 再販制度 for short, a new book costs the same in every shop: its 定価, the list price printed on it. In March 2001 the Fair Trade Commission decided to keep the system “for the time being”. Competition policy pointed to scrapping it, the Commission said, but there was no national consensus, and public comments had run 28,048 to keep it against 338 to scrap it. That “for the time being” still stands.

What it doesn’t cover. E-books, for one: the exception is for goods, and the Commission treats e-books as information, so their prices can be cut. Used books, for another, and that’s Naomi’s way round it.

Book-Off. BOOKOFF opened its first shop in Sagamihara, Kanagawa, in May 1990, and made its name with a new kind of shop, the 新古書店: fairly recent books, in nearly new condition, sold cheaply. In July 2026 the group ran about 700 shops in Japan and about 830 worldwide. The old-style 古本屋 lives on in Tokyo’s Jimbocho, where about 130 used-book shops line the streets.

And Amazon? Amazon.co.jp opened on 1 November 2000, selling books. Points can work like a discount, and that has caused friction: Amazon Student, launched in 2012, gave 10% back in points on books, and in May 2014 three small publishers stopped supplying Amazon for six months, saying the points broke their fixed-price agreements.

Meanwhile, bookshops are disappearing. At the end of March 2026 Japan had 9,993, fewer than 10,000 for the first time, and 510 municipalities, 29.3% of them, had none at all, up from 26.2% in 2022. The Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry set up a project team to support bookshops in March 2024, and published a plan to revive them in June 2025.

Quiz

Pick the word or form that fits. Each answer comes with the reason.

1. 子こどもの頃ころ、毎日まいにちピアノを練れん習しゅう＿＿＿。

As a child, I was made to practise the piano every day.

練習させられた: was made to practise. 練習させた would mean I made someone else practise.

2. 会社かいしゃの飲のみ会かいで、先輩せんぱいにお酒さけを無理むりやり＿＿＿。

At the office party, a senior colleague forced me to drink.

飲まされた: the spoken short form of 飲ませられた, “was made to drink”.

3. 病びょう院いんで二時間にじかんも＿＿＿。

I was kept waiting at the hospital for two whole hours.

待たされる: to be kept waiting. お待たせしました, “sorry to keep you waiting”, is the causative 待たせる.

4. 会議かいぎで、急きゅうに英語えいごで＿＿＿。

At the meeting, I was suddenly made to speak in English.

Verbs ending in す keep the full form: 話させられる. There is no short form 話さされる.

5. 嫌きらいなピーマンを、母ははに全部ぜんぶ＿＿＿。

My mother made me eat every one of the green peppers I hate.

食べさせられた: was made to eat. 食べられた would be “could eat” or “was eaten”.

6. この映画えいがには、いろいろと＿＿＿。

This film really made me think.

考えさせられる: something made you think. The cause, この映画, takes に.

7. 楽たのしみにしていた新作しんさくだったが、内容ないようには＿＿＿。

I’d been looking forward to the new release, but the story was a letdown.

がっかりさせられた: I was let down by it. がっかりされた would mean someone was disappointed in me.

8. 嫌いやがる子こどもに、＿＿＿薬くすりを飲のませた。

I made my child take the medicine, though they hated it.

無理やり: by force, against someone’s will. It goes naturally with the causative.

9. 誰だれにも言いわれていないのに、彼かれは＿＿＿ごみを拾ひろっている。

No one asked him to, but he picks up litter of his own accord.

自主的に: of your own accord, voluntarily.

10. 毎朝まいあさ六時ろくじに起おきる＿＿＿をつけたい。

I want to get into the habit of getting up at six every morning.

習慣をつける: to form a habit. 週間 is a week’s span; 週刊, “weekly”, as in a weekly magazine.

11. 合格ごうかくしたけど、まだ＿＿＿がわかない。

I passed, but it still hasn’t sunk in.

実感がわかない: it doesn’t feel real yet.

12. 久ひさしぶりに、元気げんきな祖母そぼの＿＿＿を見みて安心あんしんした。

It was a relief to see my grandmother looking well after so long.

姿: someone’s figure, how they look; the sight of them.
