The dialogue

The whole scene, retold for review

The lesson’s dialogue, retold in my own wording so you can review it in one place. It keeps the same scene, characters, words and grammar, and carries on from my retelling of lesson 7. The lesson’s stage directions are read by a narrator, and the ending back at the sushi bar is new: it ties up a loose end from lesson 5, where Wasabi said she hated wasabi. The lesson’s exact key lines are in section 3. Hide the English with the button at the top to test yourself.

Scene

The wasabi field at dusk, where lesson 7 left off. The principal steps forward.

Principal

悪わるいが、わさび君くん、君きみにはここで消きえてもらうよ！とりゃあーっ！

Sorry, Wasabi, but you’ll have to disappear, right here! Hiyaaah!

Wasabi

しょうがない……。こうなったら、あれを使つかうしかない！

It can’t be helped… If it’s come to this, I’ll have to use that!

Narrator

わさびちゃんは、畑はたけのわさびを一本いっぽん引ひっこ抜ぬくと、バリバリとかじった。

Wasabi yanked a root of wasabi out of the bed and crunched into it.

Narrator

激はげしい戦たたかいの末すえ、校こう長ちょうはあっという間まにやられてしまった。

A fierce fight followed, and in no time the principal was beaten.

Principal

いててて……。なぜ君きみはそんなに強つよいんだ？空手からてか何なにかでも習ならっているのかね？

Ow, ow, ow… Why are you so strong? Do you take karate lessons or something?

Wasabi

いいえ、校こう長ちょう先生せんせい。わさびです。わさびを食たべると、体からだの奥おくから特別とくべつな力ちからが湧わいてくるんです。

No, Principal. It’s wasabi. When I eat wasabi, a special strength wells up from deep inside me.

Wasabi

だから私わたしは、こう呼よばれています。「わさびくノ一いち」と。

That’s why they call me… “the Wasabi Kunoichi”.

Principal

な、何なんだって！？君きみがあの噂うわさの「わさびくノ一いち」だったとは……。相手あいてが悪わるかった……。ぐふっ。

Wh-what?! So you’re the Wasabi Kunoichi everyone talks about… I picked the wrong opponent… Urgh.

Scene

Back at the sushi bar, that night.

Wasabi

大たい将しょう、ただいま！伊豆いずのわさび、取とり返かえしてきました！

Boss, I’m back! I’ve got Izu’s wasabi back!

Boss

おかえり！……おっ、本ほんわさびのいい香かおりがしてきたぞ！

Welcome back! …Oh, I can smell good, real wasabi!

Boss

でも、わさびちゃん、わさびは大嫌だいきらいじゃなかったのか？

But Wasabi, didn’t you hate wasabi?

Wasabi

食たべるのは、今いまでも大嫌だいきらいです。でも、食たべないと力ちからが出でないんです。

I still hate eating it. But if I don’t, my strength won’t come.

Boss

そうかそうか！よし、今夜こんやは本ほんわさびをたっぷり使つかって握にぎるぞ！

Is that so! Right, tonight I’m making sushi with plenty of real wasabi!

2

The scene

Natsuko, Naomi and Peter, before and after the dialogue

Natsuko is back after about two weeks: お久しぶりです, ごぶさたしてました. Without Natsuko, Naomi says, who knows how far Peter would wander off track (脱線). Was it that bad? Peter missed Natsuko: someone has to keep Peter in line. Is this the last Wasabi episode, Natsuko asks? It is. But Natsuko has forgotten the story. Just listen to the earlier lessons, says Peter (cold, says Natsuko), but listeners may have forgotten too, so Naomi sums up: one day all the wasabi at the sushi bar turned into mustard; the boss appeared; the culprit was, of all people, the principal of Wasabi’s own school; Wasabi caught the principal at it; and last week ended with 君を生かして返すわけにはいかない. A real “after the break” moment, Natsuko says: stay tuned. And the grammar point is 〜てくる.

After the dialogue

So how was the long-awaited Wasabi story? Natsuko had no idea wasabi had that kind of power. Like Popeye, Naomi says, and Peter agrees: Popeye, but a woman, with wasabi for spinach. And a ninja, Naomi adds. Wasabi’s sting might really feel powerful, Natsuko thinks: it would be good when you’re sleepy. Peter could use some, Naomi suggests, being short on sleep (寝不足) lately. Would wasabi work? Natsuko thinks so: do try it. Now Peter is the one being picked on (いじめられてる). While Natsuko was away, Peter confesses, Peter and the new “alpha male”, Yuichi, picked on Naomi. So why isn’t the new alpha male here, Natsuko asks? Rather cool, that one, says Naomi. Peter takes the hint: why Peter instead? Natsuko never said that. Good to have you back, says Peter.

Phrases worth taking from the chat:

  • ごぶさたしてました — “I’ve been out of touch”: the polite way to say you haven’t been in contact for a while.
  • どうにかしなきゃね — “we’ll have to do something about that”.
  • 前のレッスン聞けばいいんじゃないですか — “why not just listen to the earlier lessons?”: 〜ばいいんじゃない, a suggestion.
  • 続きは次回 — “to be continued”.
  • 眠い時とか効きそうですよね — “it looks like it would work when you’re sleepy”: 効く, “to work, take effect”.
  • 初耳です — “that’s news to me”, Peter on where karaoke got its name (section 4).

Key lines

Wasabi’s reveal, and the principal’s last words

The lesson’s dialogue is only six lines long, so just these two are quoted exactly; section 1 covers the rest in my retelling. The English is my own rendering.

いいえ、校こう長ちょう。ワサビです。わさびを食たべると、特別とくべつなパワーがわいてくるのです。

No, Principal. It’s wasabi. When I eat wasabi, a special power wells up inside me. — Wasabi

Grammar: いいえ、校長, still without 先生, which the hosts frowned on in lesson 6; わさびを食べると, “whenever I eat wasabi”: と for something that always follows; わいてくる, today’s 〜てくる, a feeling or force rising up in you (section 5); のです, explaining (lesson 7).

な、なんだって！！君きみが噂うわさの「わさびくノ一いち」……相手あいてが悪わるかった……グフっ……

Wh-what?! You’re the Wasabi Kunoichi I’ve heard about… I picked the wrong opponent… Urgh… — the principal

Grammar: なんだって, “what did you say?!”, shock; 噂の, “the one people talk about”; 相手が悪かった, “it was the wrong opponent”: the fight was lost the moment it began; グフっ, a gasp, as in manga (all in section 7).

Watch forThe lesson’s stage directions are Japanese too, and full of this lesson’s words: ワサビを引っこ抜いて、バリバリ食べる, “pulls up a wasabi root and crunches it down”; 戦闘シーン, “battle scene”; 校長やられる, “the principal is beaten”.

4

Vocabulary

The lesson’s word list, in its order

The hosts’ notes first, then a card for each word, plus a few more from the lesson notes and the chat, each with an example of my own.

  • しょうがない — it can’t be helped. It’s a shortened しようがない, Naomi explains: there’s no means or method, no alternative. 仕様 is a manner, a means, a measure. Natsuko adds 仕方がない, said the same way, and often without the が: しかたない. A former colleague of Naomi’s remembered it as “no ginger”: しょうが is ginger, and ない, there isn’t any. Not very funny, Naomi thought. But today’s topic is wasabi, Natsuko reminds everyone, so don’t go saying わさびない.
  • 引っこ抜く (ひっこぬく) — to uproot. It started as 引き抜く, Natsuko explains: 引く, to pull, plus 抜く, to pick or remove, so to pull something out. 引っこ抜く is the more casual form, and stronger, Naomi adds: you’re pulling hard. The example: 雷が鳴っているので、急いでコンセントを引っこ抜いた, “the thunder was rumbling, so I hurried to unplug the cable.” 引き抜いた would do too, but 引っこ抜いた feels hurried, a hard yank.
  • やられる — to be beaten, overcome, killed; to suffer damage. やる is, put simply, to do, Naomi says, so やられる is to be done, and English has the same idea, Peter notes: “I’m done for”. In games you say ボスキャラにやられた, “the boss got me”, Natsuko says; and in real life, Naomi’s あ！財布がない！やられた！, “my wallet’s gone! I’ve been robbed!”
  • 空手 (からて) — karate, already an English word, though the pronunciation differs. から means empty and て hand: fighting without weapons, nothing in your hands. Karaoke comes from the same から, Naomi adds: an “empty orchestra”, empty of vocals. Peter has never heard that before: 初耳. (Peter thanks Natsuko for it, though it was Naomi’s explanation: see, Naomi says, I’ve been picked on like this all along.)
  • わく (湧く) — to well up, arise: to come out naturally. The idea for the Wasabi story, Naomi says, welled up in the writer’s mind on seeing wasabi at a soba shop: アイデアがわいた. You also say it of water springing up, 水がわく, Natsuko adds, which may be where the word began: its kanji has the water radical on the left.
  • うわさ (噂) — rumour. An interesting kanji, Natsuko says: 口, mouth, on the left, and 尊, valuable, esteemed, as in 尊敬, on the right. Respect with your words only, Peter suggests, while your actions say otherwise. Naomi’s expression: 噂が噂を呼ぶ, “one rumour breeds another”. It isn’t only for bad things, though: you add 悪い for that, 悪い噂を聞くよ, or say いい噂は聞かないね; and あそこのお店、おいしいっていう噂だよ is good news.

The lesson notes list a few more: 死ぬ, to die; こうなったら, now that it’s come to this; バリバリ食べる, to crunch away at; 戦闘シーン, a battle scene; 特別な, special; 呼ぶ, to call; なんだって, “what?!”; and 相手, opponent. The cards below cover most of them.

Beyond the lessonしょうがない sits in dictionaries under its older form, 仕様がない: “there’s no good way”, or “it’s hopeless”, with a note that today most people say しょうがない. Its twin 仕方がない means the same.

湧く or 沸く? Both are わく. The government’s guide to kanji that share a reading gives 湧く to things that spring up: hot springs and oil, and also courage, doubts, ideas, interest, clouds, applause and cheers (勇気が湧く, アイデアが湧く). 沸く is for heat and excitement: 風呂が沸く, the bath is hot; 場内が沸く, the whole hall goes wild.

引っこ抜く is 引き抜く done with force: the dictionary’s example is pulling up a daikon. It can also mean poaching staff from another company: 人材を引っこ抜く.

The real story of 噂. Natsuko’s puzzle has an answer: 口 gives the meaning, and 尊 is only there for its sound. The oldest Chinese character dictionary defines the character as people gathering to talk. And when the person you’ve been talking about walks in: 噂をすれば影がさす, often just 噂をすれば, “speak of the devil”.

しょうがないい-adjective

it can’t be helped, there’s nothing for it — from しようがない; also 仕方がない, 仕方ない

雨あめなら、試合しあいが中止ちゅうしになってもしょうがない。

If it rains, there’s nothing to be done if the match is called off.

こうなったらphrase

now that it’s come to this — often before しかない, “there’s nothing for it but…”

こうなったら、最後さいごまでやるしかない。

Now that it’s come to this, we’ll just have to see it through.

引ひっこ抜ぬくう-verb

to yank out, uproot — a stronger, more casual 引き抜く

庭にわの雑ざっ草そうを、根ねっこから引ひっこ抜ぬいた。

I yanked the weeds in the garden out by the roots.

バリバリadverb

crunch, crunch; tearing; also working flat out — バリバリ働く

弟おとうとは、せんべいをバリバリ食たべている。

My little brother is crunching away at rice crackers.

やられるる-verb

to be beaten, done for; to be hurt or ruined; to be outdone — やられた!, “they got me!”

雨あめに降ふられて、新あたらしい靴くつがやられた。

I got caught in the rain, and my new shoes are ruined.

空手からてnoun

karate: 空, empty + 手, hand

週しゅうに二回にかい、空手からての道どう場じょうに通かよっている。

I go to a karate dojo twice a week.

特別とくべつな-adjective · noun

special — 特別な日, 特別に

誕生日たんじょうびだけは、特別とくべつにケーキを二ふたつ食たべてもいい。

On your birthday, and only then, you’re allowed two pieces of cake.

湧わくう-verb

to well up, spring — water, ideas, feelings; not 沸く, to boil

話はなしを聞きいているうちに、勇気ゆうきが湧わいてきた。

As I listened, I felt courage welling up.

呼よぶう-verb

to call; to name; to invite — 〜と呼ばれる, “to be called …”

友達ともだちからは、「ケン」と呼よばれている。

My friends call me Ken.

噂うわさnoun · する-verb

rumour, talk — not always bad: いい噂, 悪い噂

あの二人ふたりが付つき合あっているという噂うわさを聞きいた。

I heard a rumour that those two are going out.

相手あいてnoun

the other party: opponent, partner — 相手が悪かった, “picked the wrong opponent”

決けっ勝しょうの相手あいては、去年きょねんの優ゆう勝しょうチームだ。

Our opponents in the final are last year’s champions.

寝不足ねぶそくnoun

lack of sleep — what Naomi suspects of Peter

寝不足ねぶそくで、授じゅ業ぎょう中ちゅうに何度なんどもあくびをした。

Short on sleep, I kept yawning in class.

脱線だっせんnoun · する-verb

derailment; going off topic — what Peter does without Natsuko

先生せんせいの話はなしは、よく脱線だっせんする。

Our teacher’s talks often wander off topic.

初耳はつみみnoun

He’s married? That’s news to me.

5

〜てくる, senses and feelings

The grammar point: something reaching your senses, or rising up inside you

This time, Peter explains, 〜てくる is for the senses, like a smell or a sound reaching you, and for a change in your inner state. Naomi’s example: アイディアが浮かんでくる, “an idea comes to me”. And a simpler one: 車が見えてくる, “the car comes into sight”. Taken apart:

1

アイディア + が

The idea, marked as the subject with が.

2

浮かんで

The て-form of 浮かぶ, to rise up, float up.

3

くる

Rising to the surface: an idea deep in your brain comes up to where you can use it, Peter says. In the same way, 見えて, from 見える, “to be visible”, + くる: the car comes into view.

Peter and Naomi act it out, waiting for someone to arrive by car: 今見えますか？ 見えない。 A minute later: あー、見えてきた見えてきた。あ、見えた見えた見えた。 It feels like something gradually arriving, Naomi says, and Natsuko sums up: it shows a state that is changing.

The lesson notes add a list: 考えが浮かんでくる, an idea comes; においがしてくる, a smell reaches you; 音が聞こえてくる, a sound reaches you; joy, sorrow or interest wells up, 喜び・悲しみ・興味が湧いてくる; and 眠気が襲ってくる, sleepiness creeps over you. The dialogue’s own example is 特別なパワーがわいてくる.

ていく and てくる, all together

Beyond the lesson: the four Wasabi lessons, side by side

Beyond the lessonWith the story over, here is every use the four Wasabi lessons taught. One idea holds them together: ていく points away from the speaker, or on from now; てくる points towards the speaker, or up to now.

Lesson and useExamples
5 · ていく: do it, then go; move away試食ししょくしていく, have a taste before you go; 走はしっていく, run off
6 · てくる: go, do it, come back出でかけてくる, pop out; 買かってくる, go and buy
6 · てくる: towards me, or aimed at me走はしってくる, come running; 送おくってくる, send to me
7 · てきた: a change up to now寒さむくなってきた, it’s been getting colder
7 · ていく: a change from now on上手じょうずになっていく, keep getting better
8 · てくる: senses and feelings reaching you見みえてくる, come into view; 湧わいてくる, well up

The time uses and the feelings use can meet in one sentence: だんだん日本語が分かってきた。これからもっと上手になっていくだろう, “Japanese has started to make sense to me, and I’ll keep on getting better.”

7

Fighting words

Beyond the lesson: the language of the final battle

Beyond the lessonThe showdown is short, but every line is a set phrase from manga, anime and samurai drama:

  • 君には死んでもらう — the villain’s classic: 〜てもらう, “have someone do something”, turned into an order. The retelling’s 消えてもらう, “you’ll have to disappear”, works the same way.
  • でやぁー！, とりゃあ！ — battle cries, the sounds of putting your strength into a blow.
  • しょうがない……こうなったら — resignation, then resolve: “there’s nothing for it… now that it’s come to this”.
  • いたたた — “ow, ow, ow”, from 痛い; the retelling’s いててて is a gruffer version.
  • 空手か何かでも — “karate or something”: か何か for a vague guess, and でも for “say”.
  • な、なんだって！ — “Wh-what did you say?!”: the stammer shows the shock, and って quotes what was just said.
  • 相手が悪かった — “I picked the wrong opponent”: said by a loser who never stood a chance.
  • グフっ — a gasp or a cough of defeat, straight from manga.

Beyond the lessonバリバリ, in the dictionary, is the sound of biting something hard, たくあんをバリバリかむ, or of tearing paper. It also describes working flat out, バリバリ仕事をこなす, and as a noun, someone at their peak: 第一線でバリバリの社員. やられる covers being hurt or damaged and being outdone, and even being smitten: あの笑顔にやられた, “that smile got me”.

8

Empty hands, empty orchestra

Beyond the lesson: karate, karaoke, and wasabi that wakes you up

Beyond the lessonFrom 唐手 to 空手. The hosts’ “empty hand” is the modern spelling. The art grew up in Okinawa, where it was first called simply ティ, and later written 唐手, “Tang hand”, a nod to China. Funakoshi Gichin demonstrated it in Tokyo in 1922. In the early Shōwa years, Funakoshi and Keio University’s club began writing 空 instead of 唐 (the club’s diary records the switch in April 1929), and on 25 October 1936 a meeting of masters in Okinawa made 空手 the official spelling. Since 2005, 25 October has been Karate Day in Okinawa Prefecture. Karate made its Olympic debut at the Tokyo Games, at the Nippon Budokan in August 2021, but it wasn’t in Paris in 2024, and it isn’t among the extra sports for Los Angeles in 2028.

The empty orchestra. Karaoke began as music-industry slang for a backing track with no vocals, used for rehearsal and recording. Machines for singing along in bars appeared in Kobe in 1971. Who invented them is disputed: Inoue Daisuke won the 2004 Ig Nobel Peace Prize for it, “thereby providing an entirely new way for people to learn to tolerate each other”, while others credit Negishi Shigeichi’s machine of 1967.

Wasabi that wakes you up. Natsuko’s hunch has a prize behind it. In 2011 a Japanese team including Imai Makoto won the Ig Nobel Prize in Chemistry for finding the ideal density of airborne wasabi to wake sleeping people in a fire or other emergency, and for turning it into a wasabi smoke alarm, designed for people who are deaf or hard of hearing.

And Popeye, the sailor whose strength comes from a can of spinach, first appeared in E. C. Segar’s newspaper strip Thimble Theatre in January 1929.

Quiz

Pick the word or form that fits. Each answer comes with the reason.

1. キッチンから、カレーのにおいが＿＿＿。

The smell of curry drifted in from the kitchen.

A smell reaching you: においがしてくる.

2. 隣となりの部屋へやから、ピアノの音おとが＿＿＿。

I can hear a piano from the next room.

A sound reaching you: 聞こえてくる. 聞いてくる is going to ask, and coming back.

3. しばらく歩あるくと、海うみが＿＿＿。

After walking for a while, the sea came into view.

Coming into sight: 見えてくる. 見てきた is “I went and saw it”.

4. シャワーを浴あびていると、いいアイディアが＿＿＿。

Good ideas come to me in the shower.

An idea rising to mind: 浮かんでくる.

5. 応援おうえんしてもらうと、元気げんきが＿＿＿。

When people cheer me on, I feel the energy welling up.

Feelings and energy well up: 湧く. 沸く is for boiling water, or a crowd going wild.

6. お風呂ふろが＿＿＿よ。

The bath’s ready.

Water heating up: 沸く.

7. 電車でんしゃが止とまっているなら、遅おくれても＿＿＿ね。

If the trains have stopped, it can’t be helped if we’re late.

しょうがない: there’s nothing to be done about it.

8. ＿＿＿、自分じぶんでやるしかない。

Now that it’s come to this, I’ll just have to do it myself.

こうなったら: now that things have come to this.

9. 犬いぬがテレビのプラグを＿＿＿しまった。

The dog yanked the TV plug out.

引っこ抜く: to yank out. 引っ越す is to move house; 引っかける, to hook or catch on something.

10. この問題もんだい、ひっかけだったのか。＿＿＿！

So this question was a trick. They got me!

やられた: you’ve been beaten, or caught out.

11. ＿＿＿をすれば影かげがさす、って本当ほんとうだね。今いま、本人ほんにんが来きたよ。

Speak of the devil: here they come now.

噂をすれば影がさす: talk about someone, and they appear.

12. 相談そうだんできる＿＿＿がいなくて、困こまっている。

I’m stuck, with no one to talk it over with.

相手: the person on the other side: a partner, or an opponent.

13. 子こどもの頃ころは、「たっくん」と＿＿＿いた。

As a kid, I was called “Takkun”.

Being called something: the passive 呼ばれる, as in Wasabi’s 「わさびくノ一」と呼ばれる.

14. 「来月らいげつ、あの店みせが閉しまるらしいよ。」「えっ、それは＿＿＿だ。」

“Apparently that shop’s closing next month.” “Really? That’s news to me.”

初耳: the first you’ve heard of it.
