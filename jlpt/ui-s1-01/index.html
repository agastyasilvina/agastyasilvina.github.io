The person in charge is out. Shall we call you back?

“We Need a Translator”, part 1, and the start of a new series: business Japanese on the phone. Scene

An interpreting and translation agency in Tokyo. The phone rings three times.

Yagami

お待またせいたしました。人事課じんじかの矢神やがみでございます。

Sorry to keep you waiting. Yagami, personnel section, speaking.

Matsudaira

私わたくし、札幌堂さっぽろどう総務課そうむかの松まつ平だいらと申もうします。

My name is Matsudaira, from the general affairs section at Sapporo-dō.

Matsudaira

I understand your company sends interpreters to symposiums and international conferences, so I’m calling about that.

Matsudaira

お忙いそがしいところ恐おそれ入いりますが、ご担当たんとうの方かたとお話はなししできますでしょうか。

I’m sorry to trouble you when you’re busy, but might I speak to the person in charge?

Yagami

Mr or Ms Matsudaira of Sapporo-dō. I’ll put you through to the person in charge now, so please hold for a moment.

2

The scene

Natsuko, Naomi and Peter, before and after the dialogue

“We Need a Translator”: but is that 通訳者 or 翻訳者, Naomi asks? English covers both, Natsuko says; Peter renders 通訳者 as interpreter and 翻訳者 as translator. In Japanese, Natsuko explains, 翻訳 nearly always means written translation, and 通訳 the spoken kind. And at last, a lesson on business Japanese: ビジネスマンの方、おまたせいたしました, Naomi says, “to all you business people, thank you for waiting.” The expressions are very useful in business, and you can use them just as they are, Natsuko adds. The situation: 松平さんという人が通訳、翻訳会社に電話をしているようです, Matsudaira calling an interpreting and translation company. Business Japanese on the phone, Peter notes, which makes it even harder: みなさん、頑張ってください.

After the dialogue

Natsuko tells a story. An Australian friend studied Japanese in Australia, then did a homestay in Japan, and used keigo with the host family, only for the host mother to say 敬語なんて使わなくていいよ, “you don’t need to use keigo”. The friend was quite cross, and declared that keigo wasn’t needed, so why study it? Peter understands the feeling. So does Natsuko, but unfortunately keigo really is necessary: there are times you don’t need it, of course, and times when you absolutely must use it. Business, says Naomi.

Take ちょっと待って, “just a moment”, Naomi says: that’s for friends, family, people you’re very close to. Add ください, ちょっと待ってください, and it’s a bit politer, for co-workers and colleagues. For your boss or a customer, it’s 少々お待ちください. Changing your politeness to suit the person you’re talking to is very important, Natsuko thinks, and when you’re not sure which level to use, the politer one is safer. Whenever in doubt, Peter sums up, stick with polite Japanese: not super-polite, Naomi says, just normal polite, which is about right, Natsuko agrees. Then the levels turn into a menu: casual polite and super polite, like the sizes at McDonald’s, then “mega polite”, after the Mega Mac, and “ultra polite”. And today, says Naomi, is ultra polite.

Phrases worth taking from the chat:

  • 敬語なんて使わなくていいよ — you don’t need to bother with keigo: なんて from LI lesson 23.
  • その気持ち、よくわかります — I know exactly how that feels.
  • 使わないと絶対にダメな場面 — situations where you absolutely must use it.
  • 丁寧な方を使ったほうがセーフ — it’s safer to use the politer one.

Key lines

The request, the call-back, and the message taken

The lesson’s dialogue has twelve spoken lines, so these three are quoted exactly; section 1 covers the rest in my retelling. The English is my own rendering.

恐おそれ入いりますが、ご担当たんとうの方かたにおつなぎいただけないでしょうか。

I’m sorry to trouble you, but could you possibly put me through to the person in charge? — Matsudaira

Grammar: 恐れ入りますが, the polite cushion (section 5); ご担当の方, the person in charge on the other side; おつなぎいただけないでしょうか, お + ます-stem + いただく, “have you connect me”, as a negative question: “couldn’t you possibly…?”

では、お手数てすうですが折おり返かえしお電話でんわをいただけますでしょうか。

Then, sorry to put you to the trouble, but could I have a call back? — Matsudaira

Grammar: お手数ですが, “sorry to trouble you”, from LI lesson 24; 折り返し, calling back; いただけますでしょうか, the potential いただける plus でしょうか, a very soft request.

確たしかに申もうし伝つたえます。私わたくし、人事課じんじかの矢神やがみが承うけたまわりました。

I’ll be sure to pass that on. This was Yagami, personnel section, who took your message. — Yagami

Grammar: 確かに, “without fail”; 申し伝えます, the humble “pass on”; 〜が承りました, “… received it”, giving your name so the caller knows who is responsible (section 5).

Vocabulary

The hosts’ words, in their order

The hosts’ notes first, then a card for each word, plus more from the dialogue, each with an example of my own.

  • おんしゃ (御社) — your company, a very polite way of saying あなたの会社: 御社のパンフレットを頂けますか, “could I have your company’s brochure?” And your own company? 弊社 or 当社, Naomi says. Even a word like “company” is hard in Japanese, Natsuko says.
  • こくさいがっかい (国際学会) — an international conference: 国際, international, and 学会, an academic meeting.
  • おそれいる (恐れ入る) — to be filled with awe, amazed, grateful, apologetic; basically for apologies and thanks, Peter says. It mostly stands in for ありがとうございます, すみません or “I was surprised”, Natsuko says. Peter knows the first two from shops: 恐れ入りますが〜ないです, “I’m sorry, but we don’t have…”. And once in a restaurant a waiter dropped a fork, Peter picked it up, and the waiter said 恐れ入ります: “thank you for doing that for me.” In today’s lesson it opens a request: 恐れ入りますが、お水を頂けますか, “excuse me, but could I trouble you for some water?” With it your Japanese becomes really polite, Natsuko says, but with friends it’s too much, says Naomi: think of it as すいません or ありがとうございます.
  • たんとう (担当) — in charge; 担当の人, the person in charge. For someone at another company, say 担当の方, since 方 is politer than 人; for someone at your own company, 担当の者. 担当の方いらっしゃいますか, “is the person in charge there?”; 申し訳ございません。担当の者は今外出中です, “I’m very sorry, the person in charge is out.” Watch which is which, Naomi says.
  • さしあげる (差し上げる) — a very polite あげる, to give: プレゼントを差し上げます instead of プレゼントをあげます. In business it goes with calls and contacts: またお電話を差し上げます, “I’ll call you again”; またご連絡差し上げます, “I’ll be in touch again”.
  • あいにく — unfortunately, a very polite expression: 社長はあいにく出張中で今日の会議には出られません, “unfortunately the president is away on business and can’t attend today’s meeting.”
  • おりかえし (折り返し) — by return; the verb is 折り返す, from 折る, to fold, and 返す, to return. It’s also used for going back, but on the phone it usually means calling back: すみません今電車の中ですので、すぐ折り返します, “sorry, I’m on the train, I’ll call you right back.” People say that a lot on trains, Natsuko says. But you’re not supposed to take calls on the train, Peter objects: who wrote this example? すいません、私が作りました, Naomi admits. Still, answering once, saying no and hanging up quickly is less annoying than letting it ring and ring, Natsuko thinks.

Three set phrases

The grammar points: opening politely, asking politely, taking a message

お忙しいところ失礼いたします

“I’m sorry to bother you when you’re busy.” It’s hard to say, Naomi admits; simpler versions are お忙しいところ失礼します or お忙しいところすみません. Adding お忙しいところ gives a polite “I know you’re busy, but…”, Natsuko says, and you add it as a matter of manners, Naomi says, even if the person isn’t busy at all. The lesson notes say it didn’t come up in this call but easily could have: use it to open a business call, or face to face, for instance before speaking to your boss at their desk. Their example: お忙しいところ失礼いたします。ご相談があるのですが……, “sorry to disturb you; I have something to ask your advice on.”

恐れ入りますが / お手数ですが + 〜ていただけないでしょうか

Two requests, both used a lot, and almost the same, Naomi says: 恐れ入りますが通訳をして頂けないでしょうか and お手数ですが通訳をして頂けないでしょうか, “sorry to ask, but could you interpret for me?” It’s actually a negative, Peter points out, closer to “wouldn’t you be able to…?”, which makes it even more polite than asking straight out. Simpler, Naomi says: 通訳をお願いします. What’s the difference? して頂けないでしょうか leaves the other person room to say no, Natsuko explains, whereas お願いします is more “please do it”, harder to turn down: the first is a question, Naomi says, so the listener has a chance to refuse. Or try 通訳をお願いできますか, Natsuko adds.

The notes give the shapes: 恐れ入りますが or お手数ですが, then either the て-form + いただけないでしょうか / いただけますか, or a noun + をお願いできますでしょうか / お願いできますか. 恐れ入ります literally means “that makes me feel very humble”, they say, and お手数 “troubles to you”, from LI lesson 24.

確かに申し伝えます。私、〜が承りました。

The notes’ third phrase is the set reply when you take a message on a business call. 申し伝える is the humble form of 言い伝える, to tell or convey; 〜が承りました gives your name, since 承る is the humble 聞く. With it, you take responsibility for getting the message through. LI lesson 12 has more on taking messages, and on ウチ and ソト.

The keigo guide on asking

Beyond the lesson: what the government’s keigo guide says about requests, and which humble forms are which

Beyond the lessonAsking, by the book. The government’s 2007 keigo guide, from LI lesson 22, has a section on making requests. Asking someone to do something puts a burden on them, it says, and just a preface such as すみませんが or 忙しいところ申し訳ないけど changes the impression a great deal. Indirect forms like これ、お願いできますか or お願いしてもいいですか show consideration more surely than お願いします, which can sound as if the request is taken for granted. And if you’re not the one in charge, a question that leaves the decision to the other person, この仕事をしていただけますか, is better than この仕事をしていただきます, which sounds as if the call were yours. That’s Natsuko’s point about room to say no. But the guide also warns that where a simple instruction or request will do, such roundabout forms are best avoided.

Two principles. Keigo should rest on mutual respect, 相互尊重, the guide says, and that runs both ways: not only juniors towards seniors and staff towards bosses, but seniors, bosses and teachers understanding and considering the position of the people below them. And using keigo is, in the end, self-expression, 自己表現: each time, you choose for yourself, based on how you feel about the relationship and the situation. Hence Natsuko’s politeness levels: the right one depends on who you’re talking to, and where.

Which kind of humble? In the guide’s five-way scheme, 差し上げる and 申し上げる are 謙譲語Ⅰ, raising the person the action is directed at. 申し伝える, built on 申す, is 謙譲語Ⅱ, courteous to the listener. Its Q&A has a section chief tell the company president はい、そのように申し伝えておきます: polite to the president, without raising the staff who will hear the message, and so perfectly correct. 弊社, with its humble 弊, is 謙譲語Ⅱ too, and almost always written, the guide says. 承る isn’t in the guide at all; the dictionaries call it the humble 受ける and 聞く, from 受け賜る, “receive from a superior”.

Watering the plants. The guide’s own example of keigo shifting: in a February 2006 survey, 30–40% of men in their teens and twenties said 植木に水をあげる, against 5–10% of men in their fifties and over, who said やる. By the old rules that あげる is a mistake, the guide says, but its drift from a humble word towards a merely refined one is under way and taking hold.

すみませんが、この資料しりょう、コピーをお願ねがいしてもいいですか。

Sorry, could I ask you to copy these papers? — a request with room to say no

御社, 恐れ入る, あいにく

Beyond the lesson: the call’s words in the dictionary

Beyond the lesson御社 or 貴社? Both mean “your company”, but in writing 貴社 is usual, 大辞泉 says. 御社 came into use mainly in speech in place of 貴社, which has too many confusing homophones, perhaps from around the early 1990s, the dictionary adds, hedging. Your own company is 弊社, humbly, or plain 当社, “this company”, which isn’t humble at all.

担当の者. 者 is simply “person”, but one used when humbling or making light of someone, or in formal speech, 大辞泉 says: 店の者に言いつけてください, “just tell one of the shop staff”. The dictionary’s example for 申し伝える is 係の者に申し伝えます, “I’ll pass it on to the staff in charge.”

恐れ入る, four ways. 大辞泉 lists being grateful, ご厚情のほど恐れ入ります; being apologetic, the sense that opens a request or a question as 恐れ入りますが; being amazed at someone’s skill, 恐れ入った腕前だ; and being appalled, あれで秀才とは恐れ入るよ, “and that’s supposed to be a genius? Spare me.” Peter’s sarcastic use has its own dictionary sense.

あいにく is a sound change of the older あやにく, the dictionaries say, though one notes a rival theory. あいにく came into use alongside it from the late Edo period through Meiji, and took over after the Taishō era. 大辞泉’s examples: 生憎ですが、もう売り切れました, “I’m afraid they’re sold out”.

And the rest. As an adverb, 折り返し is responding to a letter or question without delay: 折り返し電話します. 通訳 replaced the older words 通事 and 通弁, mainly from the Meiji era, while 翻訳 is re-expressing a written text in another language. A 学会 is a body run mainly by researchers in a field, or its meeting. 総務 handles business that concerns the whole organisation, and 人事 covers people’s posts, status and abilities.

8

Phones and interpreters

Beyond the lesson: three rings, phones on trains, an impossible number, and the interpreting trade

Beyond the lessonThree rings. City courtesy manuals spell out the custom. Koriyama’s says to answer within three rings; at four, say お待たせいたしました, and at five or more, 大変お待たせいたしました, so the dialogue’s 大変 after three rings errs on the polite side. Shiojiri’s adds お待たせいたしました once it has rung three times, Kazo’s from four. Then you read back, 復唱, the caller’s name and details, and end with your own name: 私、××課の○○と申します。確かに承りました, in Koriyama’s version. Its list of cushion words, クッション言葉, includes 恐縮ですが, お手数をお掛けしますが and 失礼とは存じますが.

On the train. When this lesson came out, the rule on Kantō trains was phones off near the priority seats, silent mode elsewhere, and no calls. From 1 October 2015, 37 rail operators in Tōhoku, Kantō and Kōshin’etsu changed the priority-seat rule to “switch off when it’s crowded”, after the communications ministry’s 2013 revision of its guideline on implanted medical devices cut the recommended distance from 22 cm to 15 cm, and as smartphones spread. Calls in the carriage are still to be avoided however empty the train is, the joint announcement said. Peter was right, and still is.

03-1…? The 03 area switched all its local exchange codes to four digits at once on 1 January 1991, NTT East records. And an exchange code can’t begin with 0 or 1, the communications ministry explains: 0 is the prefix that signals a domestic call, and numbers beginning with 1 are kept for emergency and public services such as 110 and 119. So the lesson’s 03-1868-… can never ring anywhere, and nor can my retelling’s.

Two kinds of interpreting. Simultaneous interpreting, 同時通訳, runs almost in parallel with the speaker: at international conferences, interpreters for each language sit in a separate room, and listeners pick their language on earphones. Consecutive interpreting, 逐次通訳, renders the speech in chunks at natural breaks. Kunihiro Masao co-founded the agency Simul International in 1965, and made their name in 1969 with the simultaneous interpretation of the TV coverage of the Apollo 11 moon landing.

Licensed guides. Since 4 January 2018, anyone may guide foreign visitors in a foreign language for pay; only the title, 全国通訳案内士, National Government Licensed Guide Interpreter, is reserved for those who passed the national exam and registered with a prefecture. On 1 April 2025, registrations, counted once per language, stood at 27,950, across ten languages, though the tourism agency estimates that only about 3,000–4,000 guides make their living mainly from guiding.

Conferences. JNTO counted 1,702 international conferences in Japan in 2024, meetings with at least 50 participants from at least three countries: up 23.7% on 2023, but still only 47% of 2019’s figure. Tokyo’s 23 wards led with 351, ahead of Kyoto with 215 and Fukuoka with 131, and Sapporo, home of the dialogue’s Sapporo-dō, had 47, tenth. And the scholars of the trade: 日本通訳学会, founded on 23 September 2000, renamed itself 日本通訳翻訳学会 in September 2008, the year of this lesson.

Quiz

Pick the word or form that fits. Each answer comes with the reason.

1. ＿＿＿失礼しつれいいたします。ちょっとご相談そうだんがあるのですが。

Sorry to disturb you when you’re busy. I have something to ask you about.

お忙しいところ: “at a busy time for you”, the set opener.

2. ＿＿＿、お名前なまえをもう一度いちどお願ねがいできますか。

I’m sorry, could I have your name again?

恐れ入りますが: a very polite “excuse me, but”.

3. 恐おそれ入いりますが、こちらに記入きにゅうして＿＿＿。

I’m sorry to ask, but could you possibly fill this in?

〜ていただけないでしょうか: a negative question, the politest request.

4. ＿＿＿、折おり返かえしお電話でんわをいただけますでしょうか。

Sorry to trouble you, but could I ask for a call back?

お手数ですが: “sorry to put you to the trouble”.

5. （社外しゃがいの人ひとに）あいにく、担当たんとうの＿＿＿は外がい出しゅつしております。

(To an outsider) I’m afraid the person in charge is out.

Your own colleague: 担当の者.

6. （相手あいての会社かいしゃに）ご担当たんとうの＿＿＿をお願ねがいできますか。

(To another company) Could I speak to the person in charge?

Their side: ご担当の方.

7. （相手あいての会社かいしゃについて）＿＿＿のパンフレットをいただけますか。

Could I have your company’s brochure?

御社: the other person’s company.

8. （自分じぶんの会社かいしゃについて）＿＿＿の新製品しんせいひんをご紹しょう介かいいたします。

Let me introduce our new product.

弊社: your own company, humbly.

9. 確たしかに、担当たんとうの者ものに＿＿＿。

I’ll be sure to pass that on to the person in charge.

申し伝える: the humble “pass on”.

10. 私わたくし、人事課じんじかの山田やまだが＿＿＿。

This was Yamada from personnel, who took your message.

承る: the humble “hear, receive”.

11. 戻もどりましたら、こちらからお電話でんわを＿＿＿。

When the person is back, we’ll call you.

Giving, from your side: 差し上げる.

12. 戻もどり次第しだい、＿＿＿お電話でんわいたします。

We’ll call you back as soon as the person returns.

折り返し: calling back.

13. ＿＿＿、社しゃ長ちょうは出しゅっ張ちょう中ちゅうでございます。

Unfortunately, the president is away on business.

あいにく: “unfortunately”, for news the listener won’t like.

14. 会議かいぎで、日本語にほんごを英語えいごに＿＿＿してもらった。

At the meeting, someone interpreted from Japanese into English for us.

Spoken: 通訳. Written: 翻訳.
