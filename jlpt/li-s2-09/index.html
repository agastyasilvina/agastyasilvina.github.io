One day, no subtitles.

A new story begins, in a café in America. Tom, a university student majoring in Japanese, walks up to Junko and asks her to be his friend: he wants to become able to read manga, catch song lyrics, watch films without subtitles, and chat up Japanese girls. The grammar point is 〜ようになる, “to come to, to get to the point where”, usually after a potential form: 話せるようになりたい. And Tom, by the way, speaks Japanese with an Ibaraki or Tochigi accent.

The dialogue

The whole scene, retold for review

The lesson’s dialogue, retold in my own wording so you can review it in one place. It keeps the same scene, characters, words and grammar, but every line is rephrased, Tom’s long wish list is split in two, and Junko gets a last thought of her own. The lesson’s exact key lines are in section 3. Hide the English with the button at the top to test yourself.

Scene

A café near a university in the United States. Tom, an American student, notices Junko reading a Japanese paperback.

Tom

あのう、すみません。失礼しつれいですが、もしかして日本にほんの方かたですか。

Um, excuse me. Sorry to bother you, but are you Japanese, by any chance?

Junko

え？ええ、そうですけど……。

Huh? Yes, I am…

Tom

やっぱり！僕ぼく、この大学だいがくで日本語にほんごを専攻せんこうしているトムといいます。

I knew it! I’m Tom. I’m majoring in Japanese at the university here.

Tom

もっと日本語にほんごが話はなせるようになりたいんです。よかったら、友達ともだちになってもらえませんか。

I want to get to the point where I can really speak Japanese. Would you be my friend, if you don’t mind?

Junko

えっ、友達ともだち……？ごめんなさい、最近さいきんちょっと忙いそがしくて……。

Huh, friends…? I’m sorry, I’ve been a bit busy lately…

Tom

そこを何なんとか、お願ねがいします！漫画まんがが辞書じしょなしで読よめるようになりたいし、アニメの歌うたも聞きき取とれるようになりたいんです。

Please, I’m begging you! I want to be able to read manga without a dictionary, and to catch the words of anime songs.

Tom

日本にほんの映画えいがも字幕じまくなしで見みられるようになりたいし……それに、日本人にほんじんの女おんなの子こをナンパできるようにもなりたいんです！

And I want to be able to watch Japanese films without subtitles… and I also want to be able to chat up Japanese girls!

Junko

……そうですか。でも、日本語にほんごはもう十じゅう分ぶんお上手じょうずだと思おもいますよ。

…I see. But I think your Japanese is plenty good already.

Junko, thinking

（最後さいごのは、聞きかなかったことにしよう……。）

(I’ll pretend I didn’t hear that last one…)

2

The scene

Naomi and Peter, before and after the dialogue

Just Naomi and Peter this time, with a message from the writer: today’s conversation is between Tom and Junko, and Tom speaks Japanese with an Ibaraki or Tochigi accent. Not いばらぎ, Peter interrupts: いばらき, please. Nobody cares, Naomi teases, except the people of Ibaraki; but Peter lived there for more than two years, and the people of Ibaraki don’t like hearing it said wrong. Tom is American, so instead of an American accent, the voice actor gave him a north-Kanto one. The grammar point is 〜ようになる. The scene is a café in America, and since Tom and Junko aren’t friends yet, they speak quite politely. (If Tom’s accent isn’t for you, the lesson also comes in standard Japanese.)

Beyond the lessonいばらき, not いばらぎ. Peter is right: dictionaries list the prefecture as いばらきけん. The people of Ibaraki have been correcting outsiders since the prefecture was founded in 1871, and in 2022 the local newspaper, the Ibaraki Shimbun, went as far as tying the offending dakuten to a balloon and “launching it into space”, above 30,000 metres, for Prefecture Day on 13 November. Part of the confusion may be the local accent itself, which voices k and t sounds, so a local saying いばらき can sound to outsiders like いばらぎ.

After the dialogue

What would Naomi do in Junko’s place? Maybe talk a little more; it’s a tough question. And when a stranger approaches Naomi? “I’d definitely ignore them,” Naomi says: “I’m busy, sorry.” But that’s already answering, Peter points out. In Japan, Naomi insists, it’s 無視 every time, though a real question, like asking the way, gets an answer. Peter talks and answers, both; Naomi is a little shy. Has anyone asked Naomi 日本の方ですか? Not in Japan, but abroad, yes, and Naomi answers in a friendly way, except at markets, where saying yes means being sold things at a high price. 鴨がねぎを背負ってやってくる, says Peter: the duck that turns up carrying the leeks for its own hotpot. So at markets Naomi says “I’m not Japanese”, in Japanese, which gives the game away every time.

Phrases worth taking from the chat:

  • こういうふうに声かけられて — “being approached like this”: 声をかける, “to speak to”, in the passive.
  • 私絶対無視します — “I’d ignore them, absolutely”.
  • 自分の世界に入っています — “you’re in your own world”, Peter on Naomi.
  • 高い物売られるもん — “they’d sell me expensive stuff”: もん, a casual “because”, from もの.
  • て言ってばれます — “I say it, and I’m found out”: ばれる, “to be found out”.

Key lines

Tom’s opening, and his request

The lesson’s dialogue is only six lines long, so just these two are quoted exactly; section 1 covers the rest in my retelling, including Tom’s long wish list. The English is my own rendering.

すみません、あの、失礼しつれいですけど、日本にほんの方かたですか。

Excuse me, um, sorry to ask, but are you Japanese? — Tom

Grammar: すみません, to get attention; あの, hesitating; 失礼ですけど, a cushion before a personal question, “sorry to ask, but”; 日本の方, with 方, the polite word for a person (section 7).

私わたし、今いま大学だいがくで日本語にほんごを専攻せんこうしているんですけど。日本語にほんごが話はなせるようになりたいんです。私わたしと友達ともだちになってくれませんか。

I’m majoring in Japanese at university at the moment. I want to be able to speak Japanese. Would you be my friend? — Tom

Grammar: 専攻しているんですけど, a current state, explained with んです and left open with けど to lead into the request; 話せるようになりたい, the potential 話せる + ようになる + たい (section 5); 〜てくれませんか, “won’t you…?”, a request.

Vocabulary

The lesson’s word list, in its order

The hosts’ notes first, then a card for each word, plus a few more from the dialogue and the chat, each with an example of my own.

  • 失礼 (しつれい) — impolite. 失 means missing and 礼 manners: manners missing. Tom’s 失礼ですけど could equally be 失礼ですが, used like “excuse me, but”, as in “excuse me, but I have a favour to ask”. Peter’s try, 失礼ですが、私の足を踏んでいます, “excuse me, you’re standing on my foot”, gets the reply that you don’t need the 失礼ですが there.
  • 専攻 (せんこう) — major, field of study: 専攻は何ですか, “what’s your major?”; to someone who has graduated, 専攻は何でしたか. As a verb, Tom’s 日本語を専攻しているんです: for the current state you can’t say 専攻します, Naomi notes, only 専攻しています. 専攻します is for plans: 来年日本語を専攻します.
  • 漫画 (まんが) — manga, comics, written in katakana, hiragana or kanji. 漫画本 is a comic book and 漫画家 a manga artist. At a 漫画喫茶, a manga café, you can read manga, use a computer or watch DVDs, paying by the hour: handy if you miss the last train, Naomi says. Has Naomi ever stayed the night in one? Of course not. Then why recommend it, Peter asks? It would suit Peter perfectly, Naomi replies.
  • 字幕 (じまく) — subtitles: 字, letters, and 幕, a curtain. Japanese DVDs let you switch between Japanese subtitles, English subtitles and none. Naomi watches Western films (洋画) with Japanese subtitles, and Hollywood films sometimes with English ones. Watching films without subtitles was a long-held dream, though now Naomi just reads the Japanese ones. It’s tiring, Peter agrees: Peter used to read every subtitle, but now looks at them for only the first ten minutes or so.
  • ナンパ — hitting on someone, trying to pick someone up: ナンパする, and in the passive, ナンパされる, “to be picked up”. It’s very casual, Naomi warns, so keep it out of formal situations; to Naomi it sounds a bit sleazy (やらしい), and a bit young. When were you last chatted up, Peter asks? Not lately. And the best pick-up line? Ask Peter, the specialist, Naomi says: あれ？こないだ会ったよね？久しぶり！ No good, says Peter, since Naomi would just answer 人違いです, “wrong person”. Good lines welcome, the hosts say.
  • 日本の方ですか — a really good expression, Naomi says, extremely polite. 日本人ですか is fine, but 日本の方ですか is better: less direct, and 方 is a very polite way to refer to someone (though never to yourself). Peter would be asked アメリカの方ですか. To ask where someone’s from, どこの方ですか, or politer still, どちらの方ですか. Peter’s trouble: asked どちらの方ですか in Tokyo, Japanese people answer 日本人です. They’re teasing you, Naomi suggests; better to ask 東京の方ですか or 大阪の方ですか.

Beyond the lessonPeter’s proverb. 鴨が葱を背負って来る: the duck turns up carrying the leeks too, so duck hotpot is ready at once. Dictionaries give it two shades: things falling into place nicely, or a soft touch walking in with something you can profit from, which is how Naomi means it at the market. The slangy short form is かもねぎ: あの客はかもねぎだ, “that customer is an easy mark”.

5

〜ようになる

The grammar point: coming to be able to, getting to the point where

Today’s use of 〜ようになる, Peter explains, expresses a change: someone or something comes to be in a different state from before. The dialogue’s examples show it best:

1

日本語 + が + 話せる

Japanese, the subject marker が, and 話せる, the potential form of 話す: can speak.

2

ようになりたい

ようになる, “get to that point”, with たい on なる: I want to get to where I can speak Japanese.

3

日本の漫画が読めるようになりたい

The same shape with 読める. A potential form very often comes before ようになる, Peter notes, and the whole thing is sometimes translated “so that”: so that I can read Japanese manga.

Naomi’s own example: 私は映画を字幕なしで見れるようになりたかったです, “I wanted to get to the point where I could watch films without subtitles.” And when Peter started Japanese? 食べたいものを、注文ができるようになりたかったですね: to be able to order what Peter wanted to eat. Which means Peter couldn’t, Naomi says, and acts it out: すいません。そば頼んだんですけど、なんでカレーが来てるんですか？ Naomi has been there too: in America, a vanilla shake order once brought a banana shake. バニラシェイクが頼めるようになりたかったです.

Beyond the lesson〜ようになる describes a change that happened, often gradually, rather than one you decided on. Three common shapes:

ShapeExample (mine)
potential + ようになる: an ability泳およげるようになった, I’ve learned to swim
dictionary form + ようになる: a habit毎朝まいあさ走はしるようになった, I’ve taken up running every morning
〜なくなる: losing one夜よる眠ねむれなくなった, I can’t sleep at night any more

毎日まいにち練れん習しゅうして、やっとひらがなが全部ぜんぶ書かけるようになった。

I practised every day, and at last I can write all the hiragana.

日本にほんに来きてから、納豆なっとうが食たべられるようになった。

Since coming to Japan, I’ve learned to eat natto.

子こどもが生うまれてから、夫おっとは早はやく帰かえってくるようになった。

Since our child was born, my husband has started coming home early.

目めが悪わるくなって、遠とおくの字じが読よめなくなった。

My eyes have got worse, and I can’t read things far away any more.

Watch forようになる is a change that happened; ようにする, in lesson 10, is a change you work at. And after a noun or adjective, plain になる does the job: 上手になる, 寒くなる. ようになる follows verbs.

Can-do forms

Beyond the lesson: the potential forms before ようになる, and Naomi’s 見れる

Beyond the lessonTom’s wish list runs on potential forms: 話せる, 読める, 聞き取れる, 見られる, できる. Here is how each kind of verb makes one.

VerbCan doSpoken
話はなす話はなせる—
読よむ読よめる—
聞きき取とる聞きき取とれる—
見みる見みられる見みれる
食たべる食たべられる食たべれる
来くる来こられる来これる
するできる—
  • う-verbs change the final u to e and add る: 話す → 話せる. る-verbs take られる, the same shape as the passive, so context decides: 見られる can be “can see” or “be seen”. する becomes できる, which is why Tom says ナンパできる and 日本語ができる.
  • が or を? With a potential, the thing you can do is traditionally marked with が, as in most of Tom’s lines: 日本語が話せる, 漫画が読める. を is common too, and Tom uses it twice: 映画を字幕なしで見られる, 女の子をナンパできる.
  • Dropping the ら. Tom says 見られる; Naomi, talking freely, says 見れる. Leaving out the ら of る-verb potentials is called ら抜き言葉, “ra-less words”.

Beyond the lessonHow common is 見れる? The Agency for Cultural Affairs asks every few years. In its 2015 survey, 見れた overtook 見られた for the first time, and in the latest, published on 2 October 2026, it led 53.8% to 44.5%; 来れますか led 来られますか 53.3% to 45.1%, and 出れる led too. Age decides a lot: 81.3% of 16- to 19-year-olds chose 見れた, while 64.4% of people over 70 chose 見られた. And it depends on the verb: 食べられない still beat 食べれない 63.0% to 35.5%, and 考えられない beat 考えれない 92.2% to 6.2%.

The agency’s view: 見られる is the original form; 見れる grew up on the pattern of potentials like 読める, and is held to be out of place in formal settings, and rare in newspapers. Back in 1995, its advisory council also noted the argument in its favour: 見れる can only mean “can see”, while 見られる can also be passive or honorific. So: say what you like with friends, but write 見られる, as Tom does.

Polite with strangers

Beyond the lesson: 〜の方, 失礼ですが, and asking where someone is from

  • 方 for people. Read かた, 方 is the respectful word for a person: あの方, “that person”; 日本の方, “a Japanese person”. It’s for other people only, never yourself. Its plural is 方々, かたがた.
  • Asking where someone is from. どこの方ですか, and politer, どちらの方ですか, or ご出身はどちらですか. Naomi’s tip for Peter’s problem in Tokyo: name a guess, 東京の方ですか, so the answer can’t be 日本人です.
  • Softening the question. Tom’s 失礼ですけど is a cushion phrase, said before a request or a personal question to make it gentler.

Beyond the lessonThe dictionary defines 方 as a respectful way to refer to a person, as in 女の方, and 失礼ですが as a set greeting for when you ask someone something or ask a favour: 失礼ですが、山本さんでいらっしゃいますか, “excuse me, but would you be Mr or Ms Yamamoto?”. The Agency for Cultural Affairs’ guide to keigo calls these lead-ins 前置きの表現, with すみませんが as its example, and business manuals call them “cushion words” (クッション言葉): 失礼ですが, すみませんが, 恐れ入りますが, お手数ですが, “sorry to trouble you, but”.

失礼しつれいですが、どちらの方かたですか。

Excuse me for asking, but where are you from?

恐おそれ入いりますが、もう一度いちどお名前なまえをお願ねがいできますか。

I’m very sorry, but could I have your name once more?

すみませんが、ちょっと写真しゃしんを撮とっていただけませんか。

Excuse me, could you take a photo for us?

8

Accents, subtitles, manga cafés

Beyond the lesson: Ibaraki’s name and accent, the subtitle rule, and the café you can sleep in

Beyond the lessonTom’s accent. North Kanto is part of an accentless belt that runs from Ibaraki up into southern Tohoku: speakers make no pitch difference between 箸, chopsticks, and 橋, bridge, which standard Japanese tells apart only by pitch. Dictionaries also note the rising intonation of Tochigi and Ibaraki speech, and the sentence ending べ or っぺ, used across Kanto and Tohoku dialects for guesses, intentions and invitations: 行くべ, “let’s go”; そうだっぺ, “isn’t it?”. In northern Tochigi, says the prefecture’s own newsletter, people sound more like Tohoku, where イ and ウ can merge, so that 地図, map, and 知事, governor, both come out as ツズ.

Four characters a second. Naomi’s dream of watching films without subtitles makes sense once you know how tight Japanese subtitles are: the industry rule of thumb is about four characters per second of speech, with a limit of about thirteen characters a line and two lines on screen. The veteran translator Toda Natsuko puts it at three or four a second, since reading is so much slower than listening, which is why subtitles are mostly free translations rather than word-for-word ones. Viewers are split: in a 2024 survey of 600 people, 43.0% preferred subtitles for foreign films and 36.7% dubbing (吹き替え), while about one in five didn’t watch foreign films at all.

The café you can sleep in. Manga cafés and internet cafés offer private booths, shelves of manga, computers, drinks and, at many, showers and cheap overnight packs, which is why they are a fallback after the last train. They have a harder side too: a Tokyo Metropolitan Government survey, published in 2018, estimated that about 4,000 people without a home of their own spend each night in Tokyo’s net cafés and similar places, most of them in unstable work.

Quiz

Pick the word or form that fits. Each answer comes with the reason.

1. 練れん習しゅうして、自じ転てん車しゃに乗のれる＿＿＿。

With practice, I learned to ride a bike.

A change you arrived at: 〜ようになった. ようにした is a deliberate effort; ことにした, a decision.

2. 日本語にほんごの新聞しんぶんが＿＿＿ようになりたい。

I want to be able to read Japanese newspapers.

Ability: the potential 読める, then ようになる.

3. 最近さいきん、夜よるにコーヒーを飲のむと眠ねむれ＿＿＿。

Lately, if I drink coffee at night, I can’t sleep.

Losing an ability is usually 〜なくなった. ないようにした would mean “made a point of not”.

4. 子こどもが、自分じぶんから野菜やさいを食たべる＿＿＿。

My child has started eating vegetables without being told.

A new habit you’ve noticed: dictionary form + ようになった.

5. 字幕じまくなしで映画えいがが＿＿＿ようになりたい。

I want to be able to watch films without subtitles.

The standard potential of 見る is 見られる; in speech many people say 見れる.

6. 明日あしたのパーティー、＿＿＿？

Can you make it to the party tomorrow?

The potential of 来る: 来られる, or 来れる in casual speech.

7. （丁寧ていねいに）日本にほんの＿＿＿ですか。

(Politely) Are you from Japan?

方, かた, is the polite way to say person. 者 is humble, and 奴 rough.

8. （出しゅっ身しんを丁寧ていねいに聞きく）＿＿＿の方かたですか。

(Asking politely where someone is from) Where are you from?

どちらの方ですか: “where are you from?”, more polite than どこの方.

9. ＿＿＿、駅えきはどちらでしょうか。

Excuse me, but which way is the station?

失礼ですが cushions a question to a stranger. 失礼しました apologises; 失礼します is said on entering or leaving.

10. 大学だいがくでの専攻せんこうは＿＿＿か。

What was your major at university?

For someone who has graduated, put it in the past: 専攻は何でしたか.

11. 今いま、大学だいがくで日本語にほんごを＿＿＿。

I’m majoring in Japanese at university at the moment.

A current state: 専攻している. 専攻します is a plan: 来年日本語を専攻します.

12. この映画えいがは英語えいごだけど、日本語にほんごの＿＿＿があるから大丈夫だいじょうぶだ。

The film is in English, but there are Japanese subtitles, so it’s fine.

字幕: subtitles.

13. 電話でんわの声こえが小ちいさくて、よく＿＿＿。

The voice on the phone was so quiet I couldn’t make it out.

聞き取れない: can’t catch what’s said. 聞かなかった is “didn’t listen”.

14. 友達ともだちだと思おもって肩かたをたたいたら、＿＿＿だった。

I tapped someone on the shoulder, thinking it was a friend, but it was the wrong person.

人違い: mistaking someone for someone else, Naomi’s reply to Peter’s pick-up line.
