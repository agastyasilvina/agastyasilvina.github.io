Tough meat, a soft exit — idioms with teeth.

Nishimura, from lesson 1, is at home with his wife, complaining that the meat is tough — when the TV news announces that a star footballer playing in Italy has suddenly retired. The dialogue

The whole scene, retold for review

The lesson’s dialogue, retold in my own wording so you can review it in one place: the same scene, idioms and vocabulary, with the names, the dinner, the player and some lines changed. (Yoshida is the retold Nishimura from lesson 1.) The lesson’s exact key lines are in section 3. Hide the English with the button at the top to test yourself.

Scene

Dinner at the Yoshidas’, at home.

Yoshida

うーん、このイカ、硬かたいなあ。また特売品とくばいひんを買かってきたんだろ？

Hmm, this squid is tough. You bought the cut-price stuff again, didn’t you?

Wife

あら、歯はごたえがあって、おいしいわよ。

Oh, it’s nice and chewy. It’s delicious.

Yoshida

どこが。噛かんでも噛かんでも、味あじがしないよ。……あ、ニュースの時間じかんだ。テレビつけるぞ。

Delicious? I chew and chew and it tastes of nothing. … Oh, it’s time for the news. I’m putting the TV on.

Sound

The TV comes on: the sports news.

Newsreader

サッカーのスペインリーグで活躍かつやくしていた森田もりた選手せんしゅが、きょう、突然とつぜん引退いんたいを発はっ表ぴょうしました。記者きしゃ会見かいけんの様子ようすをご覧らんください。

Morita, who had been doing well in Spain’s football league, today suddenly announced his retirement. Here is the press conference.

Yoshida

えっ、森田もりたが引退いんたい？冗じょう談だんだろ？

What? Morita, retiring? You’re joking.

Yoshida

たった三さんか月げつ前まえに「サッカーの本場ほんばで勝しょう負ぶしたい」って言いって、スペインのチームに移籍いせきしたばかりじゃないか。

Only three months ago he moved to a Spanish club, saying he wanted to take on the best in the home of football!

Wife

実際じっさいに行いってみたら、今いまの実じつ力りょくじゃ全然ぜんぜん歯はが立たたなかったんじゃない？

Maybe once he actually got there, he was no match for them at his level?

Yoshida

そんな情なさけない理由りゆうで辞やめるなよ！歯はを食くいしばって、もうちょっと頑張がんばれよ！

Don’t quit over something that pathetic! Grit your teeth and hang in there a bit longer!

Yoshida

しかも、何なんなんだよ、この会見かいけんは。歯切はぎれが悪わるくて、辞やめる理由りゆうがちっとも伝つたわってこないよ。

And what’s with this press conference? He’s so evasive, his reason for quitting isn’t coming across at all.

Wife

確たしかに、いつもの彼かれらしくないわね。

True, that’s not like him at all.

Wife

日本にほんのチームにいたころは、歯はに衣きぬ着きせぬ物言ものいいで有名ゆうめいだったじゃない。チームメートのプレーを平気へいきで批判ひはんするし、監督かんとくにまで文句もんくを言いってたのに。

On his Japanese team he was famous for never mincing his words. He’d criticise his teammates’ play without a second thought, and even complain to the manager.

Yoshida

まったくだ。おい森田もりた、しっかりしろよ。こんなにあっさりあきらめるなんて……見みていて歯はがゆいよ。

Exactly. Come on, Morita, pull yourself together. Giving up as easily as this… it’s so frustrating to watch.

2

The scene

Dinner, the news, and a husband who takes football personally

The lesson’s theme is idioms using 歯, “tooth”. The conversation is between Nishimura — the man in lesson 1 with the quick-witted assistant he owes too much to argue with (頭が上がらない) — and his wife, at home, so of course it’s casual Japanese.

Over dinner Nishimura complains that the meat is tough: she’s bought cheap meat again. His wife says it’s chewy, and tasty. He puts the TV on, and the news announces that Oohara, a footballer who had been playing well in the Italian league, has suddenly retired. Nishimura can’t believe it: only six months ago Oohara moved to an Italian club to test himself in the home of football. Maybe, his wife suggests, he found he was simply no match for them. Nishimura is disgusted — quitting for a reason like that? Grit your teeth and try harder! And the press conference is so evasive you can’t tell why he’s quitting. His wife agrees it’s not like him: in Japan he never minced his words, criticising other players and even complaining to the manager. Giving up so easily, Nishimura says — it’s infuriating.

After the dialogue

Nishimura is clearly a big football fan, Natsuko says; he got very 熱くなる — worked up — about the news. Naomi admits to having no interest in football at all; Natsuko used to watch but has stopped, so doesn’t know the players. Chris, though, likes football, even if American football and basketball are more popular in the US.

Football in Japan, Natsuko says, took off after the professional J.League started in 1993. But the best players head off to European clubs, Naomi adds; Oohara, Natsuko points out, was one of them. Why, Chris asks: the money? Partly, the hosts laugh; but the European leagues are also said to be at a higher level, so players may want to sharpen their skills there.

Key lines

The lesson’s own lines that carry its idioms

Five lines from the lesson’s dialogue, quoted exactly for study. The English is my own rendering.

あら、歯はごたえがあって、美味おいしいじゃない。

Oh, but it’s nice and chewy — it’s delicious. — his wife

Grammar: 歯ごたえがある, “chewy, with bite”; 〜じゃない isn’t a negative here: said with a falling tone, it means “but it’s…!”, gently contradicting him.

行いってみたら、自分じぶんの実じつ力りょくじゃ、まったく歯はが立たたないってことがわかったんじゃないの？

Maybe when he got there he found that, at his level, he was no match for them at all? — his wife

Grammar: 行ってみたら, “when he went and tried”; 実力じゃ = 実力では; 歯が立たない (section 6); 〜ってこと = 〜ということ; 〜んじゃないの？, “isn’t it that…?”

そんな情なさけない理由りゆうでやめる気きか？！男おとこなら、歯はを食くいしばって、もっと努力どりょくしてみろよ！

He’s going to quit for a pathetic reason like that?! If he’s a man, he should grit his teeth and try harder! — Nishimura

Grammar: やめる気か, “does he mean to quit?”; 歯を食いしばる, “grit your teeth”; してみろ, the blunt imperative of してみる. 男なら, “if you’re a man”, is Nishimura’s old-fashioned appeal, not a phrase to copy.

それに、なんだよ、この記者きしゃ会見かいけん！歯切はぎれが悪わるくて、何なにが理由りゆうなのかさっぱりわからないよ。

And what is this press conference?! He’s so evasive, I can’t tell at all what his reason is. — Nishimura

Grammar: なんだよ, “what is this?” (exasperated); 歯切れが悪い, “evasive, unclear”; さっぱりわからない, “not the faintest idea”.

日本にほんにいたときは、歯はに衣きぬ着きせぬ言いい方かたで、他ほかの選手せんしゅのプレーを批判ひはんしたり、監督かんとくにも文句もんくを言いっていたのにね。

When he was in Japan he never minced his words — criticising other players’ play, even complaining to the manager. — his wife

Grammar: 歯に衣着せぬ, “outspoken” (section 6, and the ぬ in section 7); 〜たり〜たり, “things like… and…”; 〜のにね, “and yet…”.

Vocabulary

The dialogue’s sports-news and argument words, each with a new example

引退いんたいnoun · する-verb

retirement: giving up active play, a post, or a line of work — 現役引退, “retiring from playing”

膝ひざのけがが治なおらず、三十さんじゅっ歳さいで引退いんたいを決きめた。

His knee injury never healed, and he decided to retire at thirty.

移籍いせきnoun · する-verb

a transfer to another team — moving your 籍, your “registration”

彼かれは来季らいきから大阪おおさかのチームに移籍いせきする。

He’s moving to an Osaka team from next season.

記者きしゃ会見かいけんnoun

a press conference — often just 会見; 会見を開く, “hold a press conference”

社長しゃちょうが記者きしゃ会見かいけんを開ひらいて、謝罪しゃざいした。

The company president held a press conference and apologised.

活躍かつやくnoun · する-verb

doing well, playing an active part — 活躍中, “currently doing great things”

卒そつ業ぎょう生せいたちは、世界せかい中じゅうで活躍かつやくしている。

Our graduates are doing great things all over the world.

実じつ力りょくnoun

real ability, what you can actually do — 実力不足, “not good enough”

今いまの実じつ力りょくでは、一いっ回かい戦せんを勝かつのも難むずかしい。

At our current level, even winning the first round will be hard.

本場ほんばnoun

the home of something, where it began or is done best — 本場の味, “the real taste”

本場ほんばのイタリアで、ピザの作つくり方かたを学まなびたい。

I want to learn to make pizza in Italy, where it comes from.

情なさけないい-adjective

pathetic, shameful, miserable

こんな簡単かんたんな問題もんだいを間違まちがえるなんて、情なさけない。

To get such an easy question wrong — how pathetic.

批判ひはんnoun · する-verb

criticism, criticising

新あたらしい法律ほうりつは、多おおくの専門家せんもんかから批判ひはんされた。

The new law was criticised by many experts.

監督かんとくnoun

manager, head coach (in sport); director — 映画監督, “film director”

試合しあいの後あと、監督かんとくは選手せんしゅたちを褒ほめた。

After the match the manager praised the players.

文句もんくnoun

a complaint — 文句を言う, “to complain”; 文句なし, “faultless”

弟おとうとは、私わたしが何なにを作つくっても文句もんくを言いう。

Whatever I cook, my younger brother complains.

熱あつくなるphrase

to get worked up, heated — excited, or angry

野球やきゅうの話はなしになると、父ちちはすぐ熱あつくなる。

Whenever baseball comes up, my father gets all worked up.

〜らしくないsuffix

not like (someone), out of character — 〜らしい, “just like, typical of”

遅刻ちこくするなんて、真面目まじめな君きみらしくないね。

Late? That’s not like you — you’re always so conscientious.

引退, 退職 and 移籍

The lesson’s word notes: two kinds of retirement, and a transfer

引退 or 退職?

English uses “retirement” for both, but Japanese splits them. 引退 is what an athlete does on giving up playing — and more generally, stepping down from a position or title, or giving up your line of work altogether. 退職 is leaving a company. Naomi’s example uses Natsuko, a translator: leaving the company Natsuko works for would be 退職; giving up translating altogether would be 引退.

祖父そふは六十五歳ろくじゅうごさいで会社かいしゃを退たい職しょくした。

My grandfather left his company at 65.

人気にんき歌手かしゅが、今年ことし限かぎりでの引退いんたいを発はっ表ぴょうした。

A popular singer has announced retirement at the end of this year.

移籍 — moving your registration

移籍 is written “move” + 籍, and 籍 — “register” — means being officially registered as something. 国籍, nationality, means being registered as a citizen of a country. In the dialogue, the player was registered as a member of one team, and 移籍 means moving that registration to another team: a transfer. Both 引退 and 移籍 come up constantly in professional sports news, so they’re worth having ready.

国籍こくせきを問とわず、誰だれでも応募おうぼできます。

Anyone can apply, whatever their nationality.

The 歯 idioms

The lesson’s focus: expressions with “tooth”

歯が立たない — the opponent is far too strong for you to win, or you lose badly. Natsuko’s picture: food so hard you can’t chew it, or even get your teeth into it (your teeth would crack, Naomi adds). In other words, much stronger than your teeth. Chris’s English: you can’t “sink your teeth into” it. It works for tests and problems too, not just people. The lesson’s own example: 昨日のテストは難しすぎて、歯が立たなかった, “yesterday’s test was so hard I couldn’t get anywhere with it”.

歯に衣着せぬ — saying exactly what you think, without worrying about how others will feel: speaking frankly, bluntly. Broken down: 歯 is teeth; 衣, read きぬ here, is clothing; and 着せぬ is the same as 着せない, because ぬ is a negative. So: “not putting clothes on your teeth” — Chris’s “not dressing your teeth”. It describes a person’s character, so you wouldn’t say it about yourself, or ask someone to do it. The lesson’s example: 父は、歯に衣着せぬ言い方をするので、よく人と言い争いになる, “my father says whatever he thinks, so he often ends up in arguments”.

And English’s own tooth idiom, from Chris: long in the tooth — old, and with plenty of experience. Natsuko’s guess at the logic: you’ve chewed a lot.

All six of the dialogue’s tooth idioms, with examples of my own:

歯はごたえがあるidiom

chewy, with some bite — and of a task or an opponent, challenging, worth taking on

今度こんどの相手あいては強つよいから、歯はごたえがありそうだ。

Our next opponent is strong, so it looks like a real test.

歯はが立たたないidiom

to be no match for; can’t make a dent in (a problem) — literally, can’t get your teeth into it

兄あにとチェスをしても、まったく歯はが立たたない。

When I play chess with my older brother, I don’t stand a chance.

歯はを食くいしばるidiom

to grit your teeth: to bear pain or hardship and keep going

足あしが痛いたかったが、歯はを食くいしばって最後さいごまで走はしった。

My legs hurt, but I gritted my teeth and ran to the end.

歯切はぎれが悪わるいidiom

evasive, hesitant, not clear-cut — 歯切れがいい, “crisp, clear, decisive”. 歯切れ is how cleanly a bite goes through

理由りゆうを聞きいても、彼女かのじょの返事へんじはどうも歯切はぎれが悪わるかった。

When I asked why, her answer was strangely evasive.

歯はに衣きぬ着きせぬidiom

outspoken, not mincing words — usually 歯に衣着せぬ物言い or 言い方; said of someone else

あの評ひょう論ろん家かは、歯はに衣きぬ着きせぬ批評ひひょうで人気にんきがある。

That critic is popular for reviews that don’t mince words.

歯はがゆいい-adjective

frustrating, exasperating — watching things go wrong when you can’t fix them yourself; also written 歯痒い

娘むすめがなかなか本気ほんきを出ださないので、見みていて歯はがゆい。

My daughter just won’t give it her all, and it’s so frustrating to watch.

Watch forTwo slips to avoid. Because 衣 is read きぬ here, the idiom sometimes gets written 歯に絹着せぬ, with 絹, “silk”; dictionaries count that as a mistake. And long in the tooth is less kind than it sounds here: dictionaries give it as “too old”, usually said humorously, not as praise for experience. The picture is a horse’s: its gums recede with age, so its teeth look longer. Natsuko’s “you’ve chewed a lot” is a lovely guess, though.

Beyond the lessonFour more teeth worth knowing, all common in writing and the news:

  • 奥歯に物が挟まったよう — “as if something were stuck in your back teeth”: talking around something instead of saying it. A close cousin of 歯切れが悪い.
  • 歯が浮く — “your teeth float”: flattery or sweet talk so overdone it makes you cringe, usually 歯の浮くようなお世辞.
  • 歯止めをかける — to put a brake on something getting worse. A 歯止め is a brake or a wheel chock; the news uses it constantly, as in 少子化に歯止めをかける, “halt the falling birth rate”.
  • 目には目を、歯には歯を — “an eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth”, from the Code of Hammurabi and the Bible.

彼かれの話はなしは奥歯おくばに物ものが挟はさまったようで、本音ほんねがわからない。

He talks around everything; you can’t tell what he really thinks.

物価ぶっかの上昇じょうしょうに、なかなか歯止はどめがかからない。

There’s no stopping the rise in prices.

The old negative ぬ

Beyond the lesson: the ぬ in 歯に衣着せぬ, and where else it lives

Beyond the lessonNaomi’s note on 着せぬ (“ぬ is a negative, so it means 着せない”) is worth following up. ぬ is a form of the classical negative ず, and the pair are still very much alive: in set phrases and proverbs, in writing and the news, and in a few everyday patterns. They attach exactly where ない does:

With ないWith ぬ and ず
着きせない着きせぬ・着きせず
知しらない知しらぬ・知しらず
食たべない食たべぬ・食たべず
しないせぬ・せず, never しぬ or しず
来こない来こぬ・来こず

ぬ before a noun

Before a noun, ぬ lives on in fixed phrases, some of them everyday words:

  • 見知らぬ — unknown, strange: 見知らぬ人, “a stranger”; 見知らぬ町, “a town you’ve never been to”.
  • 思わぬ — unexpected: 思わぬ事故; 思わぬところで, “where you’d least expect it”.
  • 予期せぬ — unforeseen, a news favourite, again with せ for する.
  • 知らぬ間に — before you knew it, without noticing.
  • And in proverbs: 知らぬが仏, “not knowing is Buddha”, that is, ignorance is bliss; 言わぬが花, “not saying is the flower”: some things are better left unsaid.

見知みしらぬ人ひとから、突然とつぜんメールが届とどいた。

An email suddenly arrived from someone I’d never heard of.

思おもわぬところで、昔むかしの友達ともだちに会あった。

I ran into an old friend where I’d least have expected it.

予期よきせぬトラブルで、電車でんしゃが一時間いちじかん止とまった。

An unforeseen problem stopped the trains for an hour.

ず in everyday patterns

  • 〜ずに = 〜ないで, “without doing”, common in speech too.
  • 〜ず、 links clauses in writing: “didn’t…, and so…”.
  • 〜ずにはいられない, “can’t help doing”: an N2 pattern.
  • 〜ざるを得ない, “have no choice but to”. ざる is yet another form of ず; for する, it’s せざるを得ない.

傘かさを持もたずに出でかけて、雨あめに降ふられた。

I went out without an umbrella and got caught in the rain.

薬くすりを飲のんでも熱ねつが下さがらず、病院びょういんに行いった。

The medicine didn’t bring my fever down, so I went to the hospital.

あの映画えいがの最後さいごは、泣なかずにはいられない。

You can’t watch the end of that film without crying.

雨あめが強つよすぎて、試合しあいは中止ちゅうしせざるを得えなかった。

The rain was so heavy that the match had to be called off.

One more survivor: the ん of わからん and 知らん, everyday in western Japan and the stock speech of old men in films and manga, is this same ぬ, worn down.

Watch forNot every ぬ is a negative. Classical Japanese had a second ぬ, meaning “has happened”, which attaches to a different form of the verb: 立ちぬ, “has risen”, against 立たぬ, “does not rise”. So 風立ちぬ means “the wind has risen”. Hori Tatsuo used it as a novel’s title, taken from Paul Valéry’s line “Le vent se lève ! il faut tenter de vivre !”, and Miyazaki Hayao’s 2013 film The Wind Rises drew on that novel. With a verb like 立つ, the sound before ぬ tells you which one you have: an a (立た) for the negative, an i (立ち) for the old “has happened”.

Football in Japan

Beyond the lesson: the J.League, and the road to Europe

Beyond the lessonNatsuko’s date is right. The J.League was set up in 1991, and its first match was on 15 May 1993 at the National Stadium in Tokyo: Verdy Kawasaki 1, Yokohama Marinos 2, in front of almost 60,000 people. It had ten clubs then. Now it has 60: twenty in each of three divisions, J1, J2 and J3.

It has also just moved to a European calendar. From 2026–27 the season starts in August (this one opened on 7 August 2026) and ends the following May, or early June for J1. To bridge the gap, all 60 clubs played a one-off half season from February to June 2026, the 明治安田Ｊリーグ百年構想リーグ, the “100 Year Vision League”, with no relegation and drawn games settled by penalties. Vissel Kobe won the J1 section.

Naomi’s point has only grown truer. Nobody publishes a reliable count for Europe alone, but the CIES Football Observatory counted 242 Japanese players at clubs abroad on 1 May 2026, in 135 leagues around the world, 100 more than in 2021. Belgium was the most common destination.

Oohara’s story even has a real-life echo. Nakata Hidetoshi was Japan’s first big star in Italy: a move to Perugia in 1998, then a Serie A title with Roma in 2000–01, the first for a Japanese player. On 3 July 2006, straight after Japan went out of the World Cup in the group stage, Nakata suddenly announced a retirement from football on the player’s own website, at just 29.

The first Japanese player in Serie A, though, was Miura Kazuyoshi, “King Kazu”, at Genoa in 1994. Born in February 1967, Kazu is still a professional: at 59, playing in J3 for Fukushima United, on loan from Yokohama FC until June 2027. Long in the tooth, perhaps, but not yet 引退.

Sports news has a vocabulary of its own. These come up in almost every transfer story:

WordMeaning
現役げんえきactive, still playing: 現役選手; 現役を引退する, “retire from playing”
退団たいだんleaving a club, but carrying on playing elsewhere, unlike 引退
完全かんぜん移籍いせきa permanent transfer
期限付きげんつき移籍いせきa loan: “a transfer with a time limit”
移籍金いせききんa transfer fee
契約けいやく満了まんりょうthe end of a contract
戦力外せんりょくがい通告つうこくbeing told you’re no longer in the club’s plans: released
古巣ふるすyour old club (“old nest”): 古巣に復帰する, “go back to your old club”
日本にほん代表だいひょうthe national team; 代表に選ばれる, “be picked for Japan”

期限付きげんつき移籍いせきを終おえて、彼かれは古巣ふるすに復帰ふっきした。

His loan over, he went back to his old club.

Quiz

Pick the phrase that fits. Each answer comes with the reason.

1. 相手あいては世界せかいチャンピオンだから、僕ぼくなんかじゃ＿＿＿よ。

They’re the world champion. Someone like me wouldn’t stand a chance.

歯が立たない: far too strong to beat. 歯ごたえがある would make them a good challenge, not a hopeless one.

2. 苦くるしい練れん習しゅうにも、彼かれは＿＿＿耐たえた。

He gritted his teeth and got through even the hardest training.

歯を食いしばる: grit your teeth and bear it.

3. 大臣だいじんの説明せつめいは＿＿＿、何なにが言いいたいのかわからなかった。

The minister’s explanation was so evasive, I couldn’t tell what they meant.

歯切れが悪い: evasive, unclear. Its opposite is 歯切れがいい.

4. 祖母そぼは＿＿＿人ひとで、思おもったことは何なんでも言いう。

My grandmother doesn’t mince words: she says whatever she thinks.

歯に衣着せぬ describes how someone talks: frankly, bluntly.

5. 後輩こうはいが同おなじミスを繰くり返かえすのを見みていると、＿＿＿。

Watching my junior make the same mistake again and again is so frustrating.

歯がゆい: the frustration of watching something go wrong that you can’t fix yourself.

6. もう少すこし＿＿＿仕事しごとを任まかせてほしい。

I’d like to be given work with a bit more of a challenge to it.

歯ごたえがある: chewy, and figuratively, challenging. Before a noun, が often becomes の.

7. あの選手せんしゅは、今季こんき限かぎりで現役げんえきを＿＿＿する。

That player will retire from playing at the end of this season.

An athlete giving up playing: 引退. 退職 is leaving a company.

8. 父ちちは三十年さんじゅうねん勤つとめた銀行ぎんこうを、来月らいげつ定年ていねん＿＿＿する。

Next month my father retires from the bank where he has worked for thirty years.

Leaving a company, here at the retirement age, is 退職: 定年退職.

9. 彼かれはドイツのチームに＿＿＿することが決きまった。

It’s been decided that he’ll transfer to a German team.

移籍: moving your registration (籍) to another team, a transfer.

10. 全然ぜんぜん勉べん強きょう＿＿＿試験しけんを受うけてしまった。

I ended up taking the exam without studying at all.

〜ずに = 〜ないで, “without doing”. する becomes せ before ず and ぬ: せずに, 予期せぬ (section 7).
