A lost-child announcement. And a boy who follows pretty girls.

“Cheerleaders and Lost Kids”, a flashback to a young Taiga. At a Chiba Lotte Marines game, a crying four-year-old is taken in by a cheerleader, a lost-child announcement goes out, and the boy’s mother arrives with a scolding. The grammar points are the humble いたします and 〜ております, the language of every announcement, and the vocabulary is the verbs for wearing things. Plus the keigo guide on いたす and おる, why a grown-up calls a little boy 僕, how Edo found its lost children, and the Marines’ windy ballpark by the sea.

The scene

Naomi, Yuichi and Peter, before and after the dialogue

“Cheerleaders and Lost Kids”, チアリーダーと迷子, and the kanji for 迷子 are interesting, Naomi says: who’s the one who’s lost? Yuichi sets the scene: 千葉ロッテマリーンズの球場です, the Chiba Lotte Marines’ ballpark, and ある男の子が迷子になっているみたいです, a boy seems to be lost. The grammar points are いたします and ております.

After the dialogue

Is this Taiga the same Taiga as before, Peter asks: the host? Naomi wrote it with that in mind, as a story outside the bank series, imagining what kind of kid Taiga was. Yuichi points to the mother’s last line, すぐきれいなお姉さんについていくんだから: Taiga hasn’t changed as a grown-up, says Naomi, 女好き, a ladies’ man, says Yuichi. And the announcement is very typical, Naomi says: you hear it in department stores and stadiums all the time.

Has Yuichi ever been lost? Probably, as a small child. Following pretty girls, Naomi teases? Yuichi doesn’t remember: ま、昔のことです. Then Yuichi has a discovery to share: the cheerleader calls the child ぼく. Isn’t 僕 about yourself? Usually, Peter says: it’s a masculine first-person pronoun, but here it refers to the child. The cheerleader is just trying to be friendly, Naomi thinks, and to make the boy comfortable, Peter adds: we sometimes call little boys ぼく. And little girls? おじょうちゃん, say Naomi and Yuichi, with Naomi adding の私, “like me”, as a joke. So 僕 is just for little boys.

Phrases worth taking from the chat:

  • そのつもりで書きました — I wrote it with that in mind.
  • 大人になっても変わらなかった — didn’t change even as an adult.
  • 昔のことです — that was a long time ago.
  • おもしろいことを発見したんですけど — I noticed something interesting.

Key lines

The announcement, and the mother’s verdict

The lesson’s dialogue is eleven lines long, so these three are quoted exactly; section 1 covers the rest in my retelling. The English is my own rendering.

迷子まいごのお知しらせをいたします。

This is a lost-child announcement. — Announcement

Grammar: お知らせ, a notice, with the polite お; いたします, the grammar point: the humble します, for the stadium’s own action (section 5).

ロッテマリーンズのキャップをかぶり、サングラスをかけ、ドラゴンボールのビーチサンダルを履はき、マリーンズのユニフォームを着きた4歳さい位くらいの男おとこの子こをチアリーダー控ひかえ室しつで保護ほごしております。

We are looking after a boy of about four, in a Lotte Marines cap, sunglasses, Dragon Ball beach sandals and a Marines uniform, in the cheerleaders’ waiting room. — Announcement

Grammar: かぶり、かけ、履き、着た, one wearing verb per item, chained with the ます-stem, all describing 男の子 (section 4); 4歳位, “about four”; 保護しております, the second grammar point: the humble しています.

すぐ、きれいなお姉ねえさんに付ついて行いくんだから。

You always go running after a pretty young lady. — Mother

Grammar: すぐ, “at the drop of a hat”; 付いて行く, to follow; 〜んだから at the end, an exasperated “honestly, you always…”.

Vocabulary

The hosts’ words, in their order

Japanese uses several verbs for wearing or putting things on, depending on where they go, and the hosts start with those. Then the hosts’ other words, a card for each, and more from the dialogue, each with an example of my own.

  • かぶる — to wear on your head: from the top, Naomi says, with a pulling-down motion, Peter adds, above the shoulders. 帽子をかぶる, to wear a hat.
  • かける — to wear glasses. かける feels like “to hang”, Naomi says: the glasses hang on your ears, Peter says. 眼鏡をかける.
  • きる (着る) — to wear, for the upper body; the most common one, Naomi says: ドレスを着る, to wear a dress.
  • はく (履く) — to wear on the lower body, with a kind of pulling motion, Peter says: ズボンをはく, trousers; 靴下をはく, socks; サンダルをはく, sandals.
  • つける — to wear an accessory, and with accessories する works too: ネックレスをつける or ネックレスをする. A necktie? しめる, to fasten, Yuichi says, though ネクタイをする is fine too.

Now the announcement: ロッテマリーンズのキャップをかぶり、サングラスをかけ、ドラゴンボールのビーチサンダルを履き、マリーンズのユニフォームを着た4歳位の男の子, one long relative clause describing the boy, Naomi says. The interesting part, Peter says, is how it’s joined up: each wearing verb is linked to the next with its ます-stem, かぶり, かけ, 履き, until the last, 着た. In English you say “wearing” once and list the things; in Japanese each item needs its own verb.

  • ひかえしつ (控え室) — a waiting room: 控える, to wait or to refrain, and 室, room, so a room where someone waits or stands by.
  • ほごする (保護する) — to protect, to look after, to take in. 保護者, also in the dialogue, is 保護をする人, Yuichi says, 子供を守る人, Naomi says: a guardian.
  • インフォメーションセンターまで、至急お越し下さい — please come to the information centre as soon as possible. 至急 means 急いで, in a hurry, and お越し下さい is the polite 来て下さい, Naomi explains. In an announcement or a very polite situation, you say 至急お越し下さい rather than 急いで来て下さい: 至急入り口にお越し下さい, Yuichi offers, “please come to the entrance at once”.

The lesson notes add 迷子 (a lost child), お知らせ (a notice) and 付いて行く (to follow). お越しください appeared in lesson 21 too.

Beyond the lessonWhat the dictionaries say. 控える is to wait close by, ready for a task or your turn, and a 控室 is a room for doing just that. 保護 is protecting someone from harm, and also, in 大辞泉’s second sense, holding someone who needs urgent care at a police station or similar place, with the example 迷子を保護する. A 保護者 is someone obliged to look after a minor, especially the parent, or whoever stands in for one. As for wearing, 大辞泉’s note on 着る says it once covered putting on almost anything, from hakama and helmets to futons and swords; today it’s mainly for the whole body or the upper body, and the verb depends on the body part: はく for hakama and trousers, かぶる for hats, おびる for swords. The 日本国語大辞典 dates the change: from the Muromachi to the Edo period, かぶる, かづく and はく gradually took over 着る’s ground, and by the Meiji era hats were mostly かぶる and hakama はく. はく even has two kanji, 穿く for trousers and 履く for footwear. Glasses have been かける since at least Natsume Sōseki’s 永日小品 (1909): 近眼の所為か眼鏡を掛けて. And a tie is 締める, tied firmly, or simply する, as in ネクタイをする or マスクをする. And the notes’ two other samples: ふらりと means setting off or turning up casually, without warning or purpose, and 首, also written 馘, is dismissal: 首になる, to be fired.

いたします and ております

The grammar points: lowering yourself, politely

いたします is the ます form of the humble verb いたす, the humble する, Peter explains: speaking about your own action, you lower your position and so show more respect. It sounds difficult, Naomi says, but you’ll hear it in どうぞよろしくおねがいいたします, a politer version of the phrase everyone knows, どうぞよろしくおねがいします: take out します and put in いたします. A practice round: あした、でんわします, “I’ll call tomorrow”, becomes 明日電話いたします, which you could translate, Peter says, as “I will humbly call”.

The second point is ております, the humble form of the progressive ています, from another humble verb, おる: an ongoing action or state, spoken of with the speaker lowered. わたしは東京にすんでいます, “I live in Tokyo”, becomes 私は東京に住んでおります. Naomi asks Yuichi how long Yuichi has worked at JapanesePod101: 去年から働いております, Yuichi replies, to show respect to Naomi Sensei. This is rare ね, says Naomi.

The lesson notes add that いたす works on its own or in compounds like 運転いたします, that it belongs to formal speech, and that it keeps company with honorific お and ご words, as in 迷子のお知らせをいたします. Their examples include 私たちは、来月結婚いたします and 明日、お電話いたします, and for ております, 佐藤は、ただ今外出しております: Sato is a colleague, so on the phone to an outsider, Sato counts as your side, the ウチ and ソト of lesson 22. One warning from the notes: in the plain form, 〜ておる sounds arrogant, as in そんなことは分かっておる！, “I know that perfectly well!”, the voice of a domineering spouse or boss.

おる, おられる and 早急

Beyond the lesson: what the keigo guide says about いたす and おる, おる’s two faces, and how 早急 is read

Beyond the lessonPolite to the listener. In the government’s 2007 keigo guide, from lesson 22, いたす and おる are 謙譲語Ⅱ, or 丁重語: words that describe your own side’s actions courteously to the person you’re talking to, alongside 参る and 申す. Because they work toward the listener, the guide says, they can even be used for third parties or things you needn’t raise, as in あ、バスが参りました, “ah, here comes the bus”, and they generally come with ます: hence いたします and おります. And since they’re for your own side, using them for the listener’s side or someone you should raise, as in （あなたは）どちらから参りましたか or 先生は来週海外へ参ります, is inappropriate.

おる’s two faces. 大辞泉 lists plain おる as an old-fashioned or arrogant way of saying いる, with a dialectal ring, and 〜ておる likewise, そこに控えておれ, “wait there!”; but おります, said about yourself or your own side, is the polite いる: ただ今、外出しております. The 日本国語大辞典 traces the split: in modern Japanese, plain おる came to show the speaker’s arrogance, 知っておる, while おります became an extra-polite います. That’s the notes’ そんなことは分かっておる！ いたす has the same dark side: 大辞泉 lists a pompous いたす for someone else’s action, 何をいたしておる。早くいたせ, “what are you doing? Get on with it!”

East and west. In much of western Japan, though, おる is neither humble nor haughty. The 1967 Linguistic Atlas of Japan shows イル for a person being somewhere in eastern Japan and オル in the west, a paper in a Kansai University journal notes. And 大辞泉 even lists おられる and 〜ておられる, おる with the respectful れる, as respectful forms.

さっきゅう or そうきゅう? The notes’ sample sentence, この書類を早急に出さなければ、あなたはクビです, reads 早急 as そうきゅう. The dictionaries give さっきゅう as the main reading, with 早 read さっ by custom, first found in 東海道中膝栗毛 (1814), and そうきゅう as an alternative, found from 1871. In 文化庁’s survey of early 2004, 74.5% said they read it そうきゅう and 21.2% さっきゅう. Its near-synonym 至急, “in great haste”, is the announcement word.

父ちちは、ただ今いま出でかけております。戻もどりましたら、お電話でんわいたします。

My father is out at the moment. We’ll call you back once Dad is home.

僕 and 迷子

Beyond the lesson: why you call a little boy 僕, and how Edo found its lost children

Beyond the lesson僕 for a little boy. 大辞泉’s second sense of 僕 is a word for calling to a small boy, with the example 僕のお名前は, almost the cheerleader’s line. The 日本国語大辞典 calls a mother’s ボク to a small child a word for “I” turned round to address the listener. Linguists since Suzuki Takao’s ことばと文化 (1973) have explained it from the child’s side: a small child may struggle with a second-person word like きみ, which means switching viewpoint, so the adult borrows the boy’s own word for “I”. That, a 2025 paper notes, is why it’s used for boys and not girls.

From servant to schoolboy. 僕 first meant a male servant. Read やつがれ, it was a humble way of referring to yourself; as ぼく, it spread among students and young men, from Edo-period kanbun according to the 日本国語大辞典, whose first example dates from 1763–69, or from the Meiji era according to 大辞泉. Today it’s above all the word boys use for “I”. For a little girl, it’s お嬢ちゃん: お嬢さん, from lesson 21, is a less formal お嬢様, also used to call to a young woman, while お嬢ちゃん is for little girls, 大辞泉 says.

Lost children in Edo. 迷子 is a sound change of まよいご. In the Edo period, when a child went missing, the neighbourhood went out together, banging gongs and drums and calling the child’s name: a 迷子捜. One encyclopedia records the cries, まいごのまいごの○○やーい in Kanto and かやせ、もどせ, “give back, return”, in Kansai. Children wore 迷子札, tags with their name and address, still fairly common until around the start of Japan’s postwar boom.

And by Ichikokubashi bridge in Tokyo stands the 一石橋迷子しらせ石標, put up in February 1857 by the local neighbourhood after a petition to the magistrate’s office. Parents posted a description of the missing child on one side, and anyone who knew something posted a note on the other: “an Edo noticeboard”, Tokyo’s education board calls it. Similar stones stood at Yushima Tenjin and Sensōji, and this one is now a Tokyo-designated cultural property.

お嬢じょうちゃん、ひとり？お母かあさんは？

Are you on your own, sweetheart? Where’s your mum?

The Marines and their ballpark

Beyond the lesson: the ballpark by the sea, the team, its cheerleaders, and the sandals

Beyond the lessonThe ballpark by the sea. Chiba City built Chiba Marine Stadium in the prefectural Makuhari seaside park; it was completed in February 1990, opened that April, and the city still owns it. The Marines have played there since 1992, and it holds 29,645. QVC Japan bought the naming rights from 1 March 2011, and Start Today from 1 December 2016, giving today’s ZOZOマリンスタジアム, a name renewed in 2026 until November 2033, at ¥400 million a year. The sea is part of the experience: the scoreboard has wind gauges for speeds high up and at ground level, and the sea breeze rusts the seats, the city’s redevelopment plan notes. That plan aims for a new stadium where you can feel Makuhari’s “sea, wind and sky”, 海・風・空, though in November 2025, at the Marines’ request, the city agreed to look again at a dome.

The team. On 19 November 1991 the Lotte Orions, moving to Chiba, took the name Marines, chosen from public entries. They started 1992 in new pink uniforms, and their strong first half was nicknamed the “pink whirlwind”, ピンク旋風. They won the Japan Series in 2005, sweeping Hanshin 4–0 for their first title in 31 years, and in 2010, the first team ever to do it after finishing third. A giant 26 flag went up in the right-field stands in 2003, and the fan club, TEAM26, turned 20 in 2026: Lotte treats 26 as the fans’ number, the sports site SPAIA explains, the 26th player after the 25 on the bench. Apt, for lesson 26.

The cheerleaders. M☆Splash!!, the club’s official cheer team, was formed in 2004, the club says, to dance on the field and on stage at home games and help with fan events. One member’s party trick, by the team page, is an impression of the mascot balloon, Chance-kun, struggling to inflate in the strong wind. And on pro baseball days the stadium’s information booths, inside and out, still list 迷子対応, help with lost children, among their services, next to lost property.

The sandals. Dragon Ball ran in Weekly Shōnen Jump from issue 51 of 1984 to issue 25 of 1995, filling 43 volumes. Its creator, Toriyama Akira, died on 1 March 2024, aged 68, the studio announced on 8 March, in a notice that closes with this lesson’s grammar: ここに謹んでお知らせいたします, “we humbly inform you.”

試合しあいのあと、海風うみかぜに吹ふかれながら駅えきまで歩あるいた。

After the game, we walked to the station in the sea breeze.

お待ちしております: the shop’s own waiting, so humble.

5. 帽子ぼうしを＿＿＿。

To put on a hat.

Hats go on the head: かぶる.

6. 眼鏡めがねを＿＿＿。

To put on glasses.

Glasses hang on your ears: かける.

7. 靴下くつしたを＿＿＿。

To put on socks.

Feet and legs: はく.

8. セーターを＿＿＿。

To put on a sweater.

The upper body: 着る.

9. ネクタイを＿＿＿。

To put on a tie.

A tie is fastened: 締める, or simply する.

10. 遊園地ゆうえんちで、子こどもが＿＿＿になった。

A child got lost at the amusement park.

迷子になる: to get lost (of a child).

11. ＿＿＿の方かたは、受付うけつけまでお越こしください。

Would the parent or guardian please come to reception.

保護者: the person responsible for a child.

12. ＿＿＿、入いり口ぐちまでお越こしください。

Please come to the entrance as soon as possible.

至急: urgently, the announcement word for 急いで.

13. 知しらない人ひとに＿＿＿行いってはいけません。

Don’t go off with strangers.

付いて行く: to follow, go along with.

14. 迷子まいごの＿＿＿をいたします。

This is a lost-child announcement.

お知らせ: a notice, an announcement.
