…Oh well. At least I know the shortcut home now.

Seriously!? Yes, please!

The scene

Naomi, Yuichi and Peter, before and after the dialogue

The last part of “Party in the ’Burbs”. Yuichi recaps the story so far: Hajime and Yasumi are friends; Yasumi was invited to Hajime’s house, but last time got lost on the way (道に迷ってしまいました); today, it seems, Yasumi arrives. The grammar point is ば, and the conversation is super casual, Yuichi says: two guys.

After the dialogue

The language is quite something today, Naomi says, and Yuichi supplies the word: rough. Naomi doesn’t talk like that; does Yuichi? Sometimes, Yuichi admits. Very polite in the studio, Peter teases, and rough the minute Yuichi walks out; Naomi pictures Yuichi’s dark side, out of the office. Not dark, Yuichi protests: very friendly. Naomi does an impression, まったく、Peterのやつよー！, “honestly, that Peter!”, and when Yuichi admits to talking like this with friends, Peterのやつよー、まじむかつくんだよ, “that Peter really winds me up”. Guys in their twenties talk like this, Yuichi says; women don’t, Naomi thinks, and Yuichi agrees.

お前 is a pretty rude word, Peter says, really strong. Does Yuichi use it with friends? Not much; it’s very rude. Surely sometimes, Peter presses; sometimes, Yuichi allows. Some men call their girlfriends お前, Naomi says, and what if a boyfriend did that, Peter asks? Naomi has said it before: being called お前 is not welcome. Probably wasn’t listening, Peter admits; more like not interested, Naomi suggests. Peter once asked listeners how the Looney Tunes catchphrases go in Japanese: the Road Runner’s is ミミ！, Naomi remembers, and Daffy Duck’s “you’re despicable” became お前って最低. Yuichi, 21, has never seen Looney Tunes: a generation gap, which in Japanese is simply ジェネレーションギャップ, or in conversation あ、若いな, “ah, so young”.

Phrases worth taking from the chat:

  • 今までのストーリーを教えて下さい — “tell us the story so far”: 今まで, until now.
  • 道に迷ってしまいました — “got lost on the way”: 道に迷う.
  • Peterのやつ — “that Peter”: やつ, a rough word for a person, or a thing.
  • むかつく — “to be annoyed, to be wound up”: slang.
  • たぶん聞いてなかったですね — “I probably wasn’t listening”: 聞いてなかった, short for 聞いていなかった.
  • がんばってくだせー！ — Yuichi’s sign-off, back again.

Key lines

The complaint, the bad news, and making the best of it

The lesson’s dialogue is nine lines long, so these three are quoted exactly; section 1 covers the rest in my retelling. The English is my own rendering.

お前まえの教おしえてくれた道みち、、かなりややこしかったぞ。。まぁ着つけたけどさ。

The way you told me to come was seriously confusing. Well, I made it, but still. — Yasumi

Grammar: お前, a rough “you” (section 7); 教えてくれた道, “the way you told me”; ややこしかった, the past of ややこしい; ぞ, a rough, emphatic ending; 着けた, the potential, “managed to get here”; けどさ, “but, y’know”.

もう少すこし早はやく着ついてれば、食たべられたのにな。みんな食たべちゃったよ。わりぃね

If you’d got here a bit earlier, you could have had some. We’ve eaten it all. Sorry. — Hajime

Grammar: 着いてれば, 着いていれば with the い dropped: the ば conditional (section 5); 食べられたのに, “could have eaten, but…”, a regret; 食べちゃった, short for 食べてしまった; わりぃね, a rough 悪いね, “sorry”.

せっかく来きたのに。。。。まぁ、帰かえりの道みちの近道ちかみちは少すくなくとも分わかったから良いいか。。

And after I came all this way… Oh well, at least I know a shortcut for the way home, so that’ll do. — Yasumi

Grammar: せっかく来たのに, “after I made the effort to come, and…”; 帰りの道の近道, the shortcut on the way home; 少なくとも, at least; 良いか, “I suppose that’ll do”.

Vocabulary

The lesson’s word list, in its order

The hosts’ notes first, then a card for each word, plus more from the dialogue and the chat, each with an example of my own.

  • かるく (軽く) — lightly. 軽い is an い-adjective: 軽い食事をする, to have a light meal; 軽く is the adverb: 軽く食事をする, to have a quick meal. Yuichi’s example, 軽く食事をしてから帰ろう, “let’s grab something quick before we go home”: McDonald’s, say.
  • ぼうけん (冒険) — adventure. There’s also 冒険家 or 冒険者, an adventurer, Yuichi says, and 冒険小説, an adventure novel, Naomi adds: ロビンソン・クルーソーは有名な冒険小説です, “Robinson Crusoe is a famous adventure novel.”
  • ややこしい — complicated, puzzling, back from lesson 14: ややこしい問題, 男女関係はややこしい. Yuichi uses it a lot.
  • まじ — serious: short for まじめ, Yuichi says; just drop the め, Naomi suggests. Yuichi often says まじで, “really? are you serious?”, the same as 本当に？
  • ありえない — impossible. Yuichi uses this a lot too: もうあり得ない！, when something shocks you. The dialogue’s ありえねー is a very casual form: ない becomes ねー, so わからない is わからねー. Naomi remembers the comedian with 「そんなの関係ねー」: 小島よしお, Yuichi supplies, who performs in swimming trunks. Adjectives do it too: こわい becomes こえー. Peter’s rule, in romaji: the last three letters change, so nai becomes nee and wai becomes wee. すげー, Naomi says; うめー, Peter adds; but not in business or formal situations, please.
  • ちかみち (近道) — a shortcut: 近い, near, and 道, a road. 近道をする is to take a shortcut: 駅まで近道をした, “I took a shortcut to the station.” Yuichi’s 人生に近道はない, “there are no shortcuts in life”, leaves Naomi and Peter unconvinced. The opposite is とおまわり (遠回り), 遠い + 回る: 駅まで遠回りをする, and Naomi’s own invention, 人生に遠回りはない, “nothing in life is a waste”. かっこいいですね, says Yuichi: かっけー、かっけー.
  • どうとかこうとか — something or other; どうこう for short. Naomi’s example: 昨日のNewsで、ガソリンの値段がどうとかこうとか言ってた, “they were saying something or other about petrol prices on the news yesterday”; handy when you’re not sure of the details, or it’s complicated. なんたらかんたら, Peter offers: that’s なんとかかんとか, Yuichi says: yada yada yada.

The lesson notes add やっと, at last, and ピザ. The cards below cover them all, with more words from the dialogue and the chat.

Beyond the lessonせっかく〜のに. Besides “specially”, the dictionary notes, せっかく can mourn an effort that didn’t bring the hoped-for result: せっかく努力したのに不合格だった, “I worked so hard, and still failed.” Yasumi’s せっかく来たのに is exactly that.

悪い, as “sorry”. Besides “bad”, 悪い apologises or thanks: 心配をかけて、悪いね, “sorry to worry you”; 悪いけれど先に帰るよ, “sorry, but I’ll head off first.” Hajime’s わりぃ is a rough way of saying it.

ジェネレーションギャップ is in the dictionary just as Naomi says it, from the English, and also as ゼネレーションギャップ: a difference between generations, the gulf between them that comes from different values.

〜ば…のに

The grammar point: “if only”, about something that didn’t happen

The last of the four conditionals is ば, and this lesson uses it counterfactually, Peter explains: we talk about a past event that, if it could have been different, would have led to something else, contrary to the facts. Yuichi reads the dialogue’s line, もう少し早く着いてれば、食べられたのにな, “if you’d got here a little earlier, you could have had some.” Too late now, Peter says. They didn’t arrive in time, Naomi says, so they couldn’t eat; and watch the tense of the main clause, Peter adds: 食べられた, past.

The pattern always reminds Naomi of the film Sliding Doors: もし私が駅まで走れば、電車に乗れたのに, “if I’d run to the station, I’d have caught the train”; もし私が電車に乗れたら、会議に間にあったのに, “if I’d caught the train, I’d have been in time for the meeting”; もし会議に間にあったら、あなたとは出会わなかったのに, “if I’d been in time for the meeting, I’d never have met you.” Then Peter begins a sentence, もし, and stops, and Naomi’s 何？もし何？私が何よ？, “what? if what? what about me?”, earns praise for its perfect rising intonation.

1

The ば form, then a past

走れば、間に合った; 安ければ、買った. What would have happened, if only.

2

ていれば, or in speech てれば

着いていれば → 着いてれば. The い of ている drops in casual speech.

3

のに for the regret

食べられたのに, “could have, but didn’t”. It closes the sentence with a sigh.

The lesson notes put it plainly: if the first situation had been different, a different result would have followed, but it didn’t. In the dialogue, the fact is that Yasumi was too late to eat; もう少し早く着いてれば imagines the opposite. And のに at the end voices the dissatisfaction or regret.

Beyond the lessonば isn’t alone. A poster handout from a Linguistic Society of Japan meeting, comparing Japanese and Marathi conditionals, notes that Japanese counterfactuals are made mainly with たら, ば and なら, with a past verb plus のに or だろう in the main clause: このボタンを押せば、水が出たのに, “if you’d pressed this button, water would have come out.” So Naomi’s たら lines in the Sliding Doors chain are fine too. The dictionary’s term for the idea is 反実仮想: supposing the opposite of the facts, as in もし〜だったら…だろうに.

のに, the sigh. At the end of a sentence, のに voices discontent, a grudge or a reproach: これで幸せになれると思ったのに, “and I thought this would make me happy.” The word itself has been used since the Edo period, and came into wide use only in modern times. As for てる, the dictionaries list it simply as a sound change of ている, used in speech; casual speech squeezes すれば too, into すりゃ: こうすりゃよかった, “I should have done it this way.”

傘かさを持もってくれば、濡ぬれなかったのに。

If I’d brought an umbrella, I wouldn’t have got wet.

もう少すこし安やすければ、買かったのになあ。

If it had been a little cheaper, I’d have bought it.

あの時とき、正しょう直じきに言いっていれば、こんなことにはならなかった。

If I’d been honest back then, it wouldn’t have come to this.

ば, the fourth “if”

Beyond the lesson: what else ば does, and the four conditionals side by side

Beyond the lessonWhat else ば does. A grammar module from the Tokyo University of Foreign Studies says ば basically states relations that always hold, and it’s a favourite for general truths, proverbs above all (塵も積もれば山となる, section 8). But it also works for a one-off condition: あした雨が降れば、運動会は中止です, “if it rains tomorrow, sports day is off.” Where ば is the best choice is when a result is wanted and the point is what it takes: どうすれば、漢字を覚えることができますか, “what do I have to do to learn kanji?”

〜ばいい and 〜ば〜ほど. The same ば makes 〜ばいい, which a Japan Foundation column says carries a sense of “do just that, and it’s enough”, so it can sound helpful or curt depending on the context; the bare 〜ば？, as in 自分でやれば？, “do it yourself, then?”, tends to sound cold. And 〜ば〜ほど means “the more…, the more…”: 見れば見るほど美しい, “the more you look, the more beautiful it is.”

The rule to watch. The University of Texas’s notes put it this way: normally, after an action verb, ば can’t lead into a command, request, invitation, wish or intention. Not 食べれば、ねてください, but 食べたら、ねてください, “once you’ve eaten, go to bed.” After an adjective or a verb of state, it can: 大きければ、買うつもりです, “if it’s big, I plan to buy it”; 時間があれば、勉強しませんか, “if you have time, shall we study?”

The more you study Japanese, the more interesting it gets.

7

Rough talk

Beyond the lesson: お前, 俺, マジ, and why ない turns into ねー

Beyond the lessonお前 used to be polite. It began as a respectful word for the space in front of a god or a noble, and as a pronoun it was once a respectful word for superiors, used by men and women alike. Only around the early 1800s did it slide to equals and inferiors, where it has stayed: a word for someone close, or someone you look down on a little. 俺 has narrowed too: once used by men and women of every rank, to superiors and inferiors alike, it’s now what men say, in casual settings, to equals or juniors.

マジ is older than it sounds. The dictionaries agree it’s short for まじめ, and one cites it from a 1781 novel of the pleasure quarters. An article by a former dictionary editor notes that まじめ and マジ both seem to have come into use in the Edo period, though today’s マジ is used a little differently, so it may not have been in continuous use since.

Why ない becomes ねー. Turning ai or oi into a long e is a feature of old Tokyo’s 下町 speech, which carries on the speech of Edo’s craftsmen and shopkeepers: アケー for 赤い, ホセー for 細い. The rough talk of craftsmen was called べらんめえ言葉, and in Edo speech, a dictionary notes, ない often came out as ねえ. Today’s すげー and ありえねー carry the same merger.

そんなの関係ねぇ. 小島よしお’s catchphrase, chanted in nothing but swimming trunks, made the top ten of Japan’s 2007 buzzword awards.

マジで？全然ぜんぜん知しらなかった。

Seriously? I had no idea.

急がば回れ

Beyond the lesson: shortcuts, detours, and proverbs built on ば

Beyond the lesson急がば回れ. “If you’re in a hurry, go round”: if you want to get there quickly, a safe, sure way, even a long one, gets you there sooner than a risky shortcut. 急がば is the classical form: in the old language, ば after the 未然形 made a hypothetical “if”, where modern Japanese would say 急げば.

A collection of comic tales completed in 1623, the 醒睡笑, quotes a poem it credits to the linked-verse poet 宗長 (1448–1532): 武士のやばせの船は早くともいそがば廻れ瀬田の長橋, “the warriors’ boat from Yabase may be quick, but if you’re in a hurry, go round by the long bridge at Seta.” The poem is often called the proverb’s source, but a proverb dictionary thinks it was the other way round: the saying was already current in the 16th century, and the poem worked it in.

Why the boat was the risk. Kusatsu’s tourist board, which claims the proverb’s roots for the town, explains: from Kusatsu to Ōtsu, you could cross the Seta bridge and go round the southern end of Lake Biwa by land, about 12 km, or take the 矢橋 ferry across the lake, about 6 km. The boat was quicker, but winter to spring is the season of the 比叡おろし, the wind that sweeps down off Mount Hiei on the far shore, so walking the long way round was the surer bet. The ferry still thrived in the Edo period, as a shortcut on the 東海道.

More proverbs built on ば.

  • 塵も積もれば山となる — “even dust, piled up, becomes a mountain”: small things add up. The 塵 isn’t rubbish but the tiniest speck, a proverb dictionary notes. The saying goes back to a Buddhist treatise, the 大智度論, and it’s one of the cards in the Edo いろはかるた.
  • 住めば都 — “live somewhere, and it becomes the capital”: anywhere feels like home once you’re used to it. It doesn’t mean “if you’re going to live somewhere, make it the city”, the dictionary warns. A proverb dictionary adds that this 住めば is the old 已然形 plus ば: not a plain “if”, but a settled condition, “once you’ve lived there”.
  • 噂をすれば影がさす — “talk about someone, and their figure appears”: speak of the devil. The 影 is a person’s silhouette, and 影がさす means catching a glimpse of them; in conversation it’s usually cut short, to 噂をすれば影, or blurred into 噂をすれば何とやら.
  • 犬も歩けば棒に当たる — “even a dog that wanders about runs into a stick”: the first card of the Edo いろはかるた, read two opposite ways since Edo times. Go out and you’ll meet trouble, or go out and you’ll meet luck; taken literally it’s the trouble, but most recorded uses are the lucky one.

Watch forFiguratively, the dictionary’s 近道 is a quick way to a goal (出世の近道, the fast track to promotion), and 遠回り is trouble you needn’t have taken (遠回りな方法, a roundabout method). Yuichi’s 人生に近道はない uses the first; Naomi’s 人生に遠回りはない turns the second on its head, and 急がば回れ is on Naomi’s side.

1. もう少すこし早はやく家いえを＿＿＿、間まに合あったのに。

If I’d left home a little earlier, I’d have made it.

Looking back on what didn’t happen: 〜ば…たのに. と and なら can’t do that.

2. もっと安やす＿＿＿、買かったのに。

If it had been cheaper, I’d have bought it.

An い-adjective takes ければ: 安い → 安ければ.

3. 雨あめ＿＿＿、試合しあいは中止ちゅうしです。

If it rains, the match is off.

A noun can’t take ば directly: 雨なら, or more formally 雨であれば.

4. 宿しゅく題だいが＿＿＿、遊あそびに行いけるのに。

If I didn’t have homework, I could go out.

ない becomes なければ.

5. せっかく作つくった＿＿＿、誰だれも食たべてくれなかった。

I went to the trouble of making it, and nobody ate any.

Things didn’t go as they should have: のに, often after せっかく.

6. 駅えきまでは、どう行いけ＿＿＿いいですか。

How should I get to the station?

Asking what to do: 〜ばいいですか.

7. 練れん習しゅうすれ＿＿＿するほど、上手じょうずになる。

The more you practise, the better you get.

〜ば〜ほど: the more…, the more….

8. もっと勉べん強きょうし＿＿＿、合格ごうかくできたのに。

If I’d studied more, I could have passed.

していれば, with the い dropped as in speech: してれば.

9. 「明日あした、試験しけんだって。」「＿＿＿で？全然ぜんぜん勉べん強きょうしてないよ。」

“Apparently the exam’s tomorrow.” “Seriously? I haven’t studied at all.”

マジで？: “seriously?”, slang from まじめ.

10. 「わからない」の、ぞんざいな言いい方かた：＿＿＿

The rough way to say わからない:

ない becomes ねー in rough speech: ありえねー, わからねー.

11. この路地ろじを通とおると、駅えきへの＿＿＿になる。

Going down this alley is a shortcut to the station.

近道: a shortcut.

12. 道みちを間違まちがえて、ずいぶん＿＿＿してしまった。

I took a wrong turn and ended up going a long way round.

遠回り: a detour, the long way round.

13. 三時間さんじかん待まって、＿＿＿順じゅん番ばんが来きた。

After three hours’ wait, my turn finally came.

やっと: at last, after a struggle.

14. ニュースで、天気てんきがどう＿＿＿こうとか言いっていた。

On the news they were saying something or other about the weather.

どうとかこうとか: something or other.
