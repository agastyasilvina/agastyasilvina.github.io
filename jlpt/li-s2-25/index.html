A time deposit would have been better. Still, you’ve grown up.

Every line is rephrased, and the last two are new: Taiga voices a regret, and Seiya disagrees. The lesson’s exact key lines are in section 3. Scene

Outside the bank. Seiya runs into Taiga coming out.

Seiya

お、大河たいがじゃん！銀行ぎんこうで何なにしてたの？もう早速さっそくお金かねおろしたとか？

Hey, Taiga! What were you doing in the bank? Taking money out already?

Taiga

ああ、聖夜せいや。いや、お金かねを引ひき出だしに来きたわけじゃないんだ。

Oh, Seiya. No, I wasn’t here to take money out.

Seiya

え？お金持かねもちの弁護士べんごしのハツコ先生せんせいから、振ふり込こみがあったんじゃないの？

What? Didn’t that rich lawyer, Hatsuko, send you a transfer?

Taiga

それがさ……急きゅう用ようができたって、ドタキャンされちゃったんだ。

Well, the thing is… something urgent came up, and I got cancelled on at the last minute.

Seiya

じゃあ、口座こうざはまだ0円えんのまま？

So your account’s still at zero?

Taiga

俺おれ、貯金ちょきんはゼロだけど、1円玉えんだまと5円玉えんだまと10円玉えんだまだけは、いつも貯金箱ちょきんばこに入いれてるんだよ。

I’ve got no savings, but I always drop my 1-, 5- and 10-yen coins in a money box.

Seiya

うん、それで？

Right, and?

Taiga

それがけっこう貯たまったから、今日きょう、銀行ぎんこうに持もってきたわけ。全部ぜんぶで6万まん5332円えん！

Well, it had really built up, so I brought it to the bank today. ¥65,332 in all!

Seiya

マジで？すげえ。じゃあ、貯蓄用ちょちくようの口座こうざも作つくったの？

Seriously? That’s amazing. So did you open a savings account too?

Taiga

それは次回じかいのお楽たのしみ。

That’s a treat for next time.

Taiga

今回こんかいは、半額はんがくを普通ふつう預金よきんに入いれて、もう半額はんがくの3万まん2666円えんは現金げんきんでもらってきた。

This time, I paid half into my ordinary account and took the other half, ¥32,666, in cash.

Taiga

そのうち半分はんぶんは生活費せいかつひ、残のこりの半分はんぶんは小遣こづかいにするつもり。

Half of that is for living expenses, and the other half is my spending money.

Seiya

どうせなら、普通ふつう預金よきんより定期預金ていきよきんに入いれればよかったのに。

If you were going to do it, you should have put it in a time deposit, not an ordinary account.

Seiya

まあ、それでも大河たいが……ずいぶん成せい長ちょうしたじゃん。なんか、うれしくて泣なけてくる。

Still, Taiga… you’ve really grown up. I’m so happy I could cry.

Taiga

ハツコさんにも、ちゃんと貯金ちょきんしてるって言いえばよかったなあ。

I should have told Hatsuko I’m a saver too.

Seiya

……いや、それは言いわなくてよかったと思おもうよ。

…No, I think it’s just as well you didn’t.

2

The scene

Naomi, Yuichi and Peter, before and after the dialogue

“More Money, More Problems”, part 5: the last one, Peter suggests; そうですかね, says Naomi, is it? Yuichi gives the recap. The conversation is between Taiga and Seiya, who are friends, so it’s casual, and 銀行の前で、偶然TaigaさんとSeiyaさんが会ったみたいです: they seem to have bumped into each other in front of the bank. In the previous lesson, Naomi reminds us, Taiga was changing coins into notes, and there was quite a lot of money, a few tens of thousands of yen, Naomi thinks. 私は覚えてないです, Peter admits. The grammar point, Yuichi says, is ば、よかった: “it would have been good if I or someone had done something”, a conditional with a little remorse or wishfulness about what should have been done, Peter explains. Naomi uses it a lot.

After the dialogue

Taiga changed coins into notes, Naomi says, but sometimes Naomi changes notes into notes, for the same amount: going to a wedding in Japan, you have to bring new notes as a money gift. Ordinary notes are old by then, Yuichi supposes. And some amounts are no good, Peter says: the number has to be odd, Naomi agrees, never even. So usually ¥30,000, Peter says. But if you want to give ¥20,000, Naomi says, one 10,000-yen note and two 5,000-yen notes is fine. うそー！, says Peter: 前そのことを知っていればよかったのに, “if only I’d known that before”, the grammar point in the wild. Yuichi has never been to a wedding, and during the talk of odd numbers, the others report, Yuichi’s face said “really?” and Yuichi started staring at the ceiling. 勉強になりました, Yuichi says: that was educational.

Are there money gifts in America? There are, Peter says, but not as large as in Japan, and you can give a present instead, though some people give both. And if you’re invited to an American wedding, you can bring someone along. In Japan? 絶対だめだめだめ, says Naomi: there’s no seat for them, says Yuichi. Naomi has heard of an Asian country where inviting one person brings the whole family. At funerals, Naomi adds, the notes don’t have to be new, though there is a custom of giving money.

Naomi’s grandmother died recently, Naomi mentions, and Peter asks: すいません, what should you say in a case like this? あ、そうですか…, the others suggest, said quietly and looking a little down. Not すみませんでした, Naomi warns: that sounds as if you killed my grandmother. Peter’s すいません was just a natural reaction, “sorry I asked”. A dictionary will offer お気の毒ですね or ご愁傷様です, Naomi says, but we don’t say that: ご愁傷様 has a bad connotation, Yuichi says, because people use it as a joke far too often, Naomi says. It still has its meaning, but you have to be very careful. The best way is not to say much: look a little down, nod your head a little, and あー、そうですか…

Phrases worth taking from the chat:

  • 前そのことを知っていればよかったのに — if only I’d known that before.
  • 勉強になりました — that was educational, I learned something.
  • 絶対だめ — absolutely not.
  • あ、そうですか… — said softly, the safest response to news of a death.

Key lines

A cancelled date, a money box, and a regret on Taiga’s behalf

The lesson’s dialogue is fourteen lines long, so these three are quoted exactly; section 1 covers the rest in my retelling. The English is my own rendering.

いや…。それが、急きゅう用ようができたからって、キャンセルされちゃって。

Well… the thing is, I got cancelled on, because something urgent came up. — Taiga

Grammar: それが, “the thing is”, warning that the news isn’t what was expected; 急用ができたからって, reporting the reason someone gave, with って for quoting; キャンセルされちゃって, the passive される plus ちゃう, “got cancelled on, unfortunately”, trailing off with て.

俺おれ、貯金ちょきんはないけど、いつも、一円玉いちえんだまと五円玉ごえんだまと十じゅう円えん玉だまは貯金箱ちょきんばこに入いれるんだ。

I don’t have any savings, but I always put my 1-, 5- and 10-yen coins in a money box. — Taiga

Grammar: 貯金はないけど, は for contrast plus けど, “savings, no, but…”; いつも〜入れるんだ, a habit, explained with んだ; 一円玉, the everyday word for a coin, from lesson 24.

普通ふつう預金よきんじゃなくて、定期預金ていきよきんにすれば、もっとよかったな。

A time deposit instead of an ordinary account would have been even better. — Seiya

Grammar: AじゃなくてB, “not A but B”; 定期預金にすれば、もっとよかったな, the grammar point: する → すれば + よかった, with もっと and an exclaiming な (section 5).

Vocabulary

The hosts’ words, in their order

The hosts’ notes first, then a card for each word, plus more from the dialogue, each with an example of my own.

  • さっそく (早速) — immediately, promptly: the same as すぐに, Yuichi says. The first kanji is “early”, 早い, and the second means “fast”, so early and fast: right away.
  • きゅうよう (急用) — urgent business. The original is 急な用事, Naomi says, shortened to 急用: 急な, urgent or sudden, and 用事, things to do, an errand. Japanese people love shortening things, Peter says. Americans do too, says Naomi; not as much as the Japanese, says Peter, who make everything compact: スタバ, for one, and バイト, Yuichi adds.
  • ちょちく (貯蓄) — savings; the verb is 貯蓄する, to save. ためる works too, Naomi says, and it’s transitive, so it needs を: お金を貯める, “I save money”. The intransitive partner is たまる, which takes が: お金が貯まる, “money builds up”.
  • せいかつひ (生活費) — living expenses: 生活, living, and 費, expenses, as in 費用. 生活に使うお金, the money you use for living. In the same way, transportation costs are こうつうひ (交通費), and food costs しょくひ (食費).
  • こづかい (小遣い) — an allowance, spending money, pocket money. In Japan you get it until about university, Naomi says. Yuichi works and gets none from home, but got ¥5,000 a month in high school. In America it depends on the family, Peter says, maybe $20 a week, about $80 a month; and these days parents often pay for the mobile phone, which isn’t handed over directly but is a kind of allowance. Married husbands in Japan get お小遣い too, Naomi says, from their wives: the salary is paid into an account, Peter explains, the wife keeps the passbook and manages all the money, and gives the husband an allowance. That may be changing, Naomi says, as more and more married women work outside the home. Does Peter get an allowance? いや、違います.

The lesson notes add 貯金箱 (a money box), 成長する (to grow up), 次回 (next time) and 今回 (this time), and 弁護士 from lesson 23. The cards below cover them, with more words from the dialogue and the chat.

Beyond the lessonWhat the dictionaries say. 貯める is transitive, to build up savings, and 貯まる intransitive, savings piling up; the dictionaries file both under 溜める and 溜まる, noting that for money they’re also written with 貯. A 定期預金 is money deposited for a set term that, in principle, can’t be paid out before the term ends, though, as lesson 23 found, banks let you break one early at a lower rate. And 小遣い is short for 小遣い銭, “small spending money”.

5

〜ばよかった

The grammar point: “I wish I had…”

〜ばよかった expresses the speaker’s regret, Peter says: “I wish I, or someone, had done something”, or literally, “it would have been good if…”. Naomi’s example: 子供の時、もっと勉強すればよかったなぁ, “I wish I’d studied more as a child”, for when the regret runs deep. Another: あのbag、売り切れてた, “that bag was sold out”, and Yuichi’s reply, もっと早く買えばよかったね, “we should have bought it sooner.”

Several endings can follow, Peter says. なぁ emphasizes the regret, and so does ね. A common one is のに, Naomi says: もっと早く買えばよかったのに, which really emphasizes the regret. The lesson notes add that な gives an exclamatory tone, as in Seiya’s line, and that when you’re talking about someone else’s action, ね invites the listener to agree: 君も昨日のパーティに来ればよかったね, “you should have come to the party yesterday too.”

The ば form, as the notes explain, changes the final -u of the dictionary form to -e and adds ば; the full set is in lesson 17, where 〜ば…のに also turned up as a regret.

Verbば form“I wish I had…”
行いく行いけば行いけばよかった
休やすむ休やすめば休やすめばよかった
食たべる食たべれば食たべればよかった
来くる来くれば来くればよかった
するすればすればよかった
1

The shape

ば form + よかった. “It would have been good if…”: so, it didn’t happen.

2

The endings

〜ばよかったな, a sigh to yourself; 〜ばよかったね, sympathy about someone else; 〜ばよかったのに, frustration. のに is the strongest.

3

The opposite

〜なければよかった, “I wish I hadn’t”. And 〜なくてよかった, “good thing I didn’t”.

傘かさを持もってくればよかった。雨あめが降ふってきた。

I should have brought an umbrella. It’s started to rain.

あんなに食たべなければよかった。おなかが痛いたい。

I wish I hadn’t eaten so much. My stomach hurts.

言いってくれれば、手伝てつだったのに。相談そうだんすればよかったのに。

If you’d told me, I’d have helped. You should have asked me.

Regret and sympathy

Beyond the lesson: ばよかった and のに in the dictionary, ご愁傷様, and shortened words

Beyond the lessonばよかった in the dictionary. The 日本国語大辞典 files it under よい: in forms like …がよい and …ばよい, it marks something as the right thing to do, or recommends it. Its first example is already a regret, from Natsume Sōseki’s 坊っちゃん (1906): そんな大病なら、もう少し大人しくすればよかった, “if it was that serious an illness, I should have behaved myself better.” Sentence-final のに carries complaint, dissatisfaction, resentment or reproach, 大辞泉 says: いいかげんにすればいいのに, “you could just give it a rest.” The Japan Foundation’s Bangkok office, in its teacher’s guide to the textbook あきこと友だち, teaches 〜ばよかった alongside 〜なければよかった for regret and disappointment.

ご愁傷様. The dictionaries are less wary than the hosts. 大辞泉 gives it first as a condolence to someone who has lost a family member, このたびはご愁傷様でございます, and second as a play on that, with light teasing: 休日にも出勤とはご愁傷様, “working on your day off, you poor thing.” The 日本国語大辞典 calls it a greeting at funerals that’s also used for light sarcasm when someone’s hopes fall through. So the formal sense is alive and well; it’s the joking one that makes it risky in casual talk. お気の毒 is feeling for someone else’s misfortune or pain: お気の毒に存じます.

Short words. A 略語 drops part of a word, as 高等学校 becomes 高校 and ストライキ becomes スト, or strings initials together, like WHO. バイト is short for アルバイト, from the German Arbeit, “work”, which in Japan settled as the word for part-time work, especially students’. As for initialisms such as AED, SNS and DX, 文化庁’s 2022 survey found 45.1% of people favourable and 54.3% unfavourable; of those in favour, 77.9% said shorter is easier to use.

「実じつは、先せん週しゅう祖父そふが亡なくなって……」「あ、そうでしたか……。」

“Actually, my grandfather died last week…” “Oh… I see…” — the hosts’ advice: say little, gently

Wedding and funeral money

Beyond the lesson: new notes, odd numbers, and why funerals are the opposite

Beyond the lessonNew notes for weddings. The mutual-aid association for weddings and funerals, 全日本冠婚葬祭互助協会, calls clean, crease-free new notes the proper form for wedding money, and says banks will get them for you; forget, and you should at least iron out the creases. Zexy, Recruit’s wedding magazine, explains the feeling: new notes suit the start of a new life, and show you got them ready ahead of the happy day. Still, in a poll of its readers, 80% of brides said the notes needn’t be new.

Odd numbers. Odd amounts such as ¥30,000 or ¥50,000 are preferred, Zexy says, and even ones have been taboo because divisible suggests splitting up, 割り切れ＝別れ. ¥90,000 is out too, because nine, ku, suggests 苦, suffering, while ¥80,000 is welcome, with eight’s widening shape. These days ¥20,000 is generally fine as “a pair”, both Zexy and the association say. Naomi’s trick, one 10,000-yen and two 5,000-yen notes, is real, but Zexy’s etiquette adviser finds it unnatural: it turns the pair into a love triangle, and the couple might think you ran short of 10,000-yen notes. Two 10,000s are neater. For a friend’s wedding, ¥30,000 is the usual gift, the association found.

Funerals: the other way round. Naomi says the notes needn’t be new; the funeral directors’ federation, 全日本葬祭業協同組合連合会, goes further. New notes should be avoided in 香典, condolence money, because they suggest you saw the death coming and got ready; if new notes are all you have, fold each one once. Even amounts and fours and nines are taboo for 香典 too, the mutual-aid association adds, except ¥2,000 and ¥20,000.

Where to get new notes. Not from the Bank of Japan, which replaces damaged notes but doesn’t simply swap notes for new ones. Commercial banks do: Mizuho, for one, exchanges up to 10 notes free once a day, at its branch machines or counters.

結婚式けっこんしきの前まえに、銀行ぎんこうで新札しんさつに両りょう替がえしておこう。

I’ll get some new notes at the bank before the wedding.

Allowances and savings

Beyond the lesson: husbands’ and children’s allowances, and what a time deposit really paid

Beyond the lessonHusbands’ allowances. Shinsei Bank, SBI Shinsei since 2023 (lesson 22), has surveyed office workers’ allowances since 1979. In 2008, the year of this lesson, salarymen averaged ¥45,825 a month; the peak was ¥77,725, in 1990. In the latest survey, from 2024, male employees averaged ¥39,081. Among married men, 55.3% hand their whole pay over to the household, so more than half are on an allowance, お小遣い制, as last year. And in dual-earner households with children, 50.4% of men say their spouse manages the pay and hands them an allowance, against just over a tenth of women: Naomi’s “things are changing” hasn’t gone far yet.

Children. In the 2015 survey by 金融広報中央委員会, the Central Council for Financial Services Information, the most common monthly allowance for high-school students was ¥5,000, with a mean of ¥5,114. Yuichi’s figure exactly.

Was Seiya right? Barely. In June 2008, Japan’s average posted rates were 0.198% a year on ordinary deposits and 0.348% on one-year time deposits under ¥3 million, the Bank of Japan’s figures show: on ¥32,666, about ¥114 a year instead of ¥65, before tax. Then rates all but vanished. The Bank of Japan introduced a negative rate of −0.1% in January 2016, and by January 2024 the averages were 0.001% and 0.005%. It ended negative rates in March 2024 and has raised its policy rate since, to 1.25% in September 2026. In October 2026 the averages were 0.331% and 0.486%, and SMBC’s one-year super time deposit under ¥3 million paid 0.500%.

Still in the bank. At the end of June 2026, Japanese households held ¥1,132 trillion in cash and deposits, 44.9% of their ¥2,519 trillion in financial assets.

金利きんりが上あがる前まえに、定期ていきにしなければよかった。

I wish I hadn’t locked it in a time deposit before rates went up.

Quiz

Pick the word or form that fits. Each answer comes with the reason.

1. 楽たのしかったよ。君きみも＿＿＿よかったのに。

It was fun. You should have come too.

来る → 来れば: the ば form, then よかった.

2. テストの前まえに、もっと＿＿＿よかった。

I wish I’d studied more before the test.

する → すれば: 勉強すればよかった.

3. 売うり切きれか……。もっと早はやく＿＿＿よかったね。

Sold out… We should have bought it sooner.

買う → 買え + ば: the -u becomes -e.

4. 道みちに迷まよった。駅えきで＿＿＿よかったな。

We’re lost. I should have asked at the station.

聞く → 聞けば.

5. おなかがすいた。朝あさごはんを＿＿＿よかった。

I’m hungry. I should have had breakfast.

食べる → 食べれば: る becomes れば.

6. そうなの？もっと早はやく言いえばよかった＿＿＿。

Really? You should have said so sooner!

のに at the end adds frustration: “if only you had…”.

7. 本ほんが届とどいたので、＿＿＿読よんでいる。

The book arrived, so I’m reading it straight away.

早速: at once, without delay.

8. ＿＿＿ができて、行いけなくなった。

Something urgent came up, so I can’t go now.

急用: urgent business, short for 急な用事.

9. 貯金箱ちょきんばこに、10円玉えんだまがたくさん＿＿＿。

Lots of 10-yen coins have built up in the money box.

が marks the coins, so it’s the intransitive 貯まる.

10. 毎月まいつき少すこしずつ、お金かねを＿＿＿いる。

I’m saving a little money every month.

を marks the money, so it’s the transitive 貯める.

11. 家賃やちんや食費しょくひなどの＿＿＿が高たかい。

Living costs, like rent and food, are high.

生活費: the money you live on.

12. 子こどものころ、毎月まいつき500円えんの＿＿＿をもらっていた。

As a child, I got 500 yen a month in pocket money.

小遣い: pocket money, an allowance.

13. 1年ねん間かんおろさないなら、＿＿＿のほうが利息りそくがいい。

If you won’t touch it for a year, a time deposit pays better interest.

定期預金: a time deposit, money left for a fixed term.

14. 続つづきは、＿＿＿にしましょう。

Let’s carry on next time.

次回: next time. 今回 is this time.
