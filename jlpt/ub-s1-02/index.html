Lesson 2 of the JapanesePod101 series in which every dialogue includes a public announcement (lesson 1 was the five o’clock chime). This one has two layers: a formal announcement built on the passive, and a short, casual exchange between Misaki and her father. The notes follow the lesson: the scene and the hosts’ talk after it, the key lines, the vocabulary, ただ今 versus ただいま, the grammar point, and the kanji close-up. Then, beyond the lesson, two passive uses that catch learners out, and how Japan’s tsunami warnings work today, which has changed since the lesson was made. The example sentences are mine unless they’re marked as the lesson’s.

The lesson

6 sections
1

The scene

A siren near the beach

A siren sounds, then an announcement: a tsunami warning has just been issued. Everyone near the coast should evacuate to high ground; a two-metre tsunami is expected. Then the message comes round again in other words — the warning has been issued, hurry to high ground, the tsunami will be two metres — and the siren sounds once more.

A father and his daughter, Misaki, are near the beach. Misaki repeats the word: “Tsunami?” Her father explains that there’s apparently been a big earthquake abroad, so big waves are coming to Japan too. Will they come to our house, Misaki asks. He doesn’t know — but they should get away to the park.

Listen for the way the announcement says everything twice, in different words: 避難してください the first time, the plainer 逃げてください the second. If you miss it once, you get another chance.

After the dialogue

The hosts, Tomoyuki and Jessi, start with the word itself. English took tsunami straight from Japanese, so the dialogue’s 津波警報 is simply a “tsunami warning”. Depending on how serious a natural disaster is, the Japan Meteorological Agency issues either an advisory, 注意報, or a warning, 警報. When the giant earthquake struck northern Japan in 2011, it issued a 大津波警報, a major tsunami warning — 大津波 is a “big tsunami”.

Then the practical point. Some places may make these announcements in English too, but in rural areas with few foreign residents, emergency announcements aren’t translated, and not knowing them is dangerous. Tsunamis aren’t common even in Japan, Tomoyuki adds, but knowing what the announcements mean is reassuring.

2

Key lines

The lines from the lesson that carry its grammar

Five lines from the lesson’s dialogue, quoted for study. The English is my own rendering.

ただ今いま、津波つなみ警報けいほうが発はっ表ぴょうされました。

A tsunami warning has just been issued. — the announcement

Grammar: ただ今, a formal “just now” (section 4); 発表されました, the passive of 発表する (section 5); が, marking a piece of new, unexpected news.

海岸かいがんの近ちかくにいる皆みなさんは、高たかい所ところに避難ひなんしてください。

Everyone near the coast, please evacuate to high ground. — the announcement

Grammar: 海岸の近くにいる皆さん, a whole clause describing 皆さん: “everyone who is near the coast”; 〜てください, a polite instruction.

２メートルの津波つなみが予想よそうされます。

A tsunami of two metres is expected. — the announcement

Grammar: 予想されます, “is expected” — the passive again, with no one named as the doer.

急いそいで、高台たかだいに逃にげてください。

Hurry — get to high ground. — the announcement

Grammar: 急いで, the て-form of 急ぐ used as “quickly, in a hurry”; 高台, “high ground” — the same instruction as the second line, in plainer words.

うん。外国がいこくで大おおきい地震じしんがあったから、日本にほんにも大おおきい波なみが来くるんだって。

Yes. There was a big earthquake in another country, so they say big waves are coming to Japan too. — the father

Grammar: から, “so, because”; 日本にも, “to Japan too”; 来るんだって, casual hearsay — “apparently, they’re saying”.

The rest is very short. Misaki asks whether the waves will come to their house too, ending her question with a soft 〜の？; her father says he doesn’t know (分からないけど, “I don’t know, but…”) and suggests they flee to the park, with the volitional 逃げよう, “let’s get away”.

3

Vocabulary

The lesson’s five words, then five more from the dialogue, each with a new example

ただ今いまadverb · noun

now, at the moment; just now — a formal 今, heard in announcements and at work

ただ今いま戻もどりました。

I’ve just got back. (Said on returning to the office.)

発はっ表ぴょうnoun · する-verb

announcement; publication; a presentation — 発表する, “announce, present”

明日あしたの会議かいぎで、新あたらしい計画けいかくを発はっ表ぴょうします。

I’ll present the new plan at tomorrow’s meeting.

予想よそうnoun · する-verb

expectation, forecast, prediction — 予想する, “expect, predict”

試合しあいは予想よそうどおりの結果けっかになった。

The match turned out just as expected.

津波つなみ警報けいほうnoun

tsunami warning

津波つなみ警報けいほうが出でたら、すぐに海うみから離はなれてください。

If a tsunami warning goes out, get away from the sea at once.

避難ひなんnoun · する-verb

evacuation, taking shelter — 避難する, “evacuate”

地震じしんのあと、近ちかくの小しょう学がっ校こうに避難ひなんした。

After the earthquake, we took shelter at the local primary school.

地震じしんnoun

earthquake

夜中よなかに大おおきな地震じしんがあって、目めが覚さめた。

A big earthquake in the night woke me up.

津波つなみnoun

tsunami

「津波つなみ」という言葉ことばは、英語えいごでもそのまま使つかわれている。

The word “tsunami” is used just as it is in English, too.

高台たかだいnoun

high ground, a hill

この町まちの避難ひなん場所ばしょは、高台たかだいにある神社じんじゃです。

This town’s evacuation site is the shrine up on the hill.

海岸かいがんnoun

coast, seashore, beach

週しゅう末まつは海岸かいがんを散歩さんぽするのが好すきだ。

At weekends I like walking along the shore.

逃にげるる-verb

to run away, escape, flee

猫ねこが窓まどから逃にげてしまった。

The cat got out through the window.

4

ただ今 and ただいま

The same sounds, two jobs, two pitches

ただ今 means the same as 今, “now” or “just now”, but it’s more formal, which is why you hear it so much in announcements and at work. What does the ただ add? Just emphasis, Tomoyuki says — it has no real meaning of its own.

ただ今いま、電車でんしゃが遅おくれております。

The train is currently delayed. (A station announcement; おります is the humble form of います.)

The same sounds are also the everyday greeting ただいま, “I’m home”. The lesson’s tip is that the two are said with a different pitch:

MeaningPitch, as the lesson teaches it
ただ今 “now”starts low, rises on だ, then falls again: た↑だ↓いま
ただいま “I’m home”starts low, rises on だ, and stays high: た↑だいま

ただいま。——おかえり。遅おそかったね。

I’m home. — Welcome back. You’re late.

Watch forPitch accent varies between regions and speakers, and accent dictionaries don’t all agree on these two. Treat the lesson’s description as one standard model and copy the lesson audio. Your device’s ▶ voice may not get the pitch right.

5

The passive: 受身

The lesson’s grammar point: “has been issued”, “is expected”

The lesson’s grammar point is the passive (受身, うけみ), the form for saying that something “was issued” or “is expected”. It starts from the first line of the announcement: 発表されました, “has been issued”, is the passive of 発表する.

With a doer: に

To review how the passive works, the lesson builds one from scratch. 鳥は虫を食べる, “birds eat bugs”, becomes 虫は鳥に食べられる, “bugs are eaten by birds”. The thing acted on becomes the topic, 食べる becomes its passive 食べられる, and the doer is marked with に, meaning “by”. Swap in fish and you get 虫は魚に食べられる. Two of my own:

子こどものころ、近所きんじょの犬いぬにかまれた。

When I was little, I was bitten by a neighbour’s dog.

授じゅ業ぎょう中ちゅうに寝ねていて、先生せんせいに注意ちゅういされた。

I dozed off in class and got told off by the teacher.

Without a doer: the announcement passive

The announcement is different: 津波警報が発表されました has no に at all. Who issued the warning isn’t mentioned, so there’s nothing to mark with “by” — just as in English, “a warning has been issued”. Notice the が too: when you report that something unusual or unexpected has happened, the subject is often marked with が. The announcement’s second passive works the same way. 予想する “expect” becomes 予想される “be expected”, so 津波が予想されます is “a tsunami is expected”.

今こん週しゅうの土曜日どようび、川原かわらで花火大会はなびたいかいが開催かいさいされます。

A fireworks display will be held by the river this Saturday.

試験しけんの結果けっかは来らい週しゅう発はっ表ぴょうされます。

The exam results will be announced next week. (Here は: the results are something we already know about, so they’re the topic.)

Beyond the lessonMaking the form. The lesson reviews the passive rather than teaching how to build it, so here is the recipe:

Verb typeRuleExamples
う-verbschange the final u sound to a, add れる書く → 書かれる、読む → 読まれる、言う → 言われる
る-verbsdrop る, add られる食べる → 食べられる、見る → 見られる
するbecomes される発表する → 発表される、予想する → 予想される
来るbecomes 来られる (こられる)来る → 来られる

Every passive verb is itself a る-verb, so it conjugates as one: 発表される → 発表されました, 予想される → 予想されています. Note that 言う becomes 言われる, with a wa: verbs ending in う take わ, not あ.

Drill — turn it round

Make each sentence passive, then say it aloud: (a) 猫が魚を食べた。 (b) 母が私を起こした。 (c) 市役所が避難場所を発表した。

Answers

(a) 魚は猫に食べられた。 (b) 私は母に起こされた。 (c) 避難場所が発表された。 When an organisation announces something, the doer is usually left out; if you do name it, use the more formal によって rather than に: 市役所によって. によって is also the usual choice with verbs of making, as in この絵はピカソによって描かれた.

Passive traps

Beyond the lesson: two passive uses that catch learners out

Beyond the lesson1. The “suffering” passive. Japanese also uses the passive for things that happen to you, usually unwelcome ones (迷惑の受身, the passive of annoyance). It even works with verbs that can’t be passive in English, like “rain” or “cry”.

帰かえり道みちで雨あめに降ふられた。

I got caught in the rain on the way home. (Literally, “I was rained on.”)

弟おとうとにケーキを食たべられた。

My little brother ate my cake. (And I’m not happy about it.)

夜中よなかに赤あかちゃんに泣なかれて、眠ねむれなかった。

The baby cried in the night, and I couldn’t sleep.

2. Passive or “can”? For る-verbs the passive looks exactly like the potential form: 食べられる can mean “is eaten” or “can eat”, and 見られる “is seen” or “can see”. Context and particles decide: a doer marked with に means passive, while an object marked with が or を means potential. In casual speech the potential often drops the ら — 食べれる, 見れる — and that shortened form is never passive. It’s common in conversation but counts as non-standard; in formal writing and on the JLPT, use the full られる.

納豆なっとうが食たべられますか。——私わたしのパンが犬いぬに食たべられました。

Can you eat natto? (potential) — My bread got eaten by the dog. (passive)

8

Tsunami warnings today

Beyond the lesson: how the system works now

The hosts’ split between advisories and warnings (section 1) still holds. But in 2013, after the experience of 2011, the Japan Meteorological Agency overhauled the system.

Beyond the lessonSince 7 March 2013 the JMA has used three levels, announcing heights in bands rather than exact figures:

大津波警報Major Tsunami Warning

Over 3 m expected. Announced as 5 m, 10 m or over 10 m — or, in the first warning after an earthquake of magnitude 8 or more, just 「巨大」, “huge”.

Leave the coast and river areas at once, for high ground or a tsunami evacuation building. Stay there until the warning is lifted: the waves come again and again.

津波警報Tsunami Warning

1–3 m expected. Announced as 3 m — or, in that first warning, 「高い」, “high”.

The same: evacuate immediately, and stay put until it’s lifted.

津波注意報Tsunami Advisory

20 cm–1 m expected. Announced as 1 m.

Get out of the water and away from the shore.

Warnings usually go out about three minutes after an earthquake. But the JMA’s own leaflet warns that when the earthquake is close to the coast, a tsunami can arrive before any warning. So if you’re near the sea and feel strong shaking — or weak shaking that goes on for a long time — get to high ground without waiting to be told. Along the Sanriku coast, in the northeast, an old saying puts it bluntly: 津波てんでんこ, “tsunami, each on your own” — run at once, without stopping to find the others, and trust them to do the same.

The father’s scenario, a big earthquake abroad bringing waves to Japan, has happened. In 1960 the Chile earthquake, the largest ever recorded, sent a tsunami across the Pacific. It reached Japan about 22 hours later and killed about 140 people there.

Warnings reach you in several ways: on TV and radio, on every phone in the area as an emergency alert (緊急速報メール), and through the town loudspeakers from lesson 1. The free Safety Tips app, backed by the Japan Tourism Agency, sends the same warnings in English and other languages.

Watch forThe lesson’s “2 metres” dates it. It was made in early 2012, under the older system, which announced heights such as 2 m. Today a two-metre forecast falls in the 1–3 m band and would be announced as a 津波警報 of ３メートル.

Quiz

Pick the word that fits. Each answer comes with the reason.

1. ただ今いま、大雨おおあめ警報けいほうが発はっ表ぴょう＿＿＿。

A heavy-rain warning has just been issued.

The warning is what gets issued, and no one is named as the issuer: the passive 発表されました, just as in the lesson’s announcement.

2. 虫むしは鳥とり＿＿食たべられる。

Bugs are eaten by birds.

In a passive sentence, に marks the doer: 鳥に, “by birds” — the lesson’s own example.

3. 明日あしたは大雪おおゆきが予想よそう＿＿＿います。

Heavy snow is expected tomorrow.

The snow isn’t doing the predicting — it’s being predicted: 予想されています.

4. 「書かく」の受身形うけみけいは？

What’s the passive of 書く?

う-verb: change the final u to a and add れる. 書ける is the potential, “can write”; 書かせる is the causative, “make someone write”.

5. 「見みる」の受身形うけみけいは？

What’s the passive of 見る?

る-verb: drop る and add られる. 見れる is the casual potential, and it’s never passive.

6. 隣となりの人ひとに一ひと晩ばん中じゅう＿＿＿、眠ねむれなかった。

The people next door were noisy all night, and I couldn’t sleep.

The “suffering” passive: their noise happened to me. 騒ぐ, “make a noise”, becomes 騒がれる.

7. 台風たいふうが来くる前まえに、家族かぞくで体育館たいいくかんに＿＿＿した。

Before the typhoon arrived, the whole family took shelter in the sports hall.

避難する, “evacuate, take shelter” — and the place you go to takes に.

8. 家いえに帰かえって、「＿＿＿！」

You get home and call out…

ただいま, “I’m home”. Whoever is at home answers おかえり; いってきます is what you say as you leave.

9. 津波つなみが来くたら、すぐに＿＿＿に逃にげてください。

If a tsunami is coming, get to … at once.

Go up, to high ground. The coast is where the wave arrives, and underground spaces flood.

10. 次つぎのうち、一番いちばん危険きけんなときに出でるのは？

Which of these is issued when the danger is greatest?

大津波警報, the Major Tsunami Warning, for waves over 3 m. Below it come 津波警報 (1–3 m) and 津波注意報 (20 cm to 1 m).
