Mustard on everything? That’s a stretch.

Part three of the wasabi mystery. At the wasabi field, the principal claims to be pulling up weeds, then confesses: wasabi is green, it stings your nose, and mustard belongs on everything, sushi included. Then comes a threat. The grammar point is the second use of 〜ていく, a change or an action carrying on from now, as in 話を続けていくのは無駄だ, “there’s no point going on with this”. The lesson also leans on のだ, し and the て-form, and brings five kanji to practise.

The dialogue

The whole scene, retold for review

The lesson’s dialogue, retold in my own wording so you can review it in one place. It keeps the same scene, characters, words and grammar, and carries straight on from my retelling of lesson 6, where Wasabi had just caught the principal at the wasabi field. Every line is rephrased, and one is new: the principal’s plan, in today’s grammar. The lesson’s exact key lines are in section 3. Hide the English with the button at the top to test yourself.

Scene

The same wasabi field at dusk, a moment later.

Principal

わ、わさび君くんじゃないか！こんな山やまの中なかで、いったい何なにをしているんだね？

W-why, it’s Wasabi! What on earth are you doing up here in the mountains?

Wasabi

えっと、ちょっと散歩さんぽを……。校こう長ちょう先生せんせいこそ、何なにをしているんですか？

Um, just out for a walk… What are you doing here, Principal?

Principal

いや、ここら辺へんは雑ざっ草そうがひどいから、少すこし取とっておこうと思おもってね……。

Oh, the weeds round here are terrible, so I thought I’d clear a few…

Wasabi

校こう長ちょう先生せんせい……それ、雑ざっ草そうじゃなくて、わさびです。

Principal… those aren’t weeds. That’s wasabi.

Principal

う……。わさび君くん、正しょう直じきに言いおう。僕ぼくはわさびが大嫌だいきらいなんだ。色いろは緑みどりだし、鼻はなにツンと来くるし……。からしの方ほうが断然だんぜんうまいじゃないか！

Ugh… Wasabi, I’ll be honest. I hate wasabi. It’s green, it goes straight up your nose… Mustard tastes far better, doesn’t it?

Principal

おでんにからし、シュウマイにからし。だったら寿司すしもそばも、からしでいいんだよ！分わかるかね？

Mustard on oden, mustard on shumai. So sushi and soba can have mustard too! Do you see?

Wasabi

校こう長ちょう先生せんせい、さすがにそれは無理むりがありますよ……。

Principal, that really is a stretch…

Principal

なにっ！？まったく、話はなしの分わからん子こだ……。これ以上いじょう君きみと話はなしを続つづけていっても、無駄むだなようだな。

What!? Honestly, you just don’t get it… It seems there’s no point carrying on this conversation with you.

Principal

日本中にほんじゅうのわさびは、これからどんどんからしに変かわっていくのだ……！

All the wasabi in Japan is going to keep turning into mustard, from now on…!

Principal

だが、ここを見みられてしまった以上いじょう、君きみを無事ぶじに帰かえすわけにはいかない。

But now that you’ve seen this, I can’t let you go home in one piece.

Principal

これからどうなるか、分わかっているね？

You know what happens now, don’t you?

Wasabi

こ、校こう長ちょう先生せんせい、何なにをするつもりですか！？

P-Principal, what are you going to do?!

2

The scene

Naomi, Yuichi and Peter, before and after the dialogue

Naomi announces part three, and nobody answers: don’t ignore me (無視しないで下さい), Naomi says; everyone must be tired. Yuichi recaps: Wasabi is a high-school student with a part-time job at a sushi bar; one day the wasabi vanished and all of it turned into からし; and when Wasabi went to a wasabi field, the principal was there (lesson 6). What was the principal doing, Naomi asks? You’re about to find out, says Yuichi. Today’s conversation is between a student and the principal, Peter explains: Wasabi speaks politely, and the principal casually. And the grammar is 〜ていく again, Naomi notes, as in Wasabi part 1, but a different use of it.

After the dialogue

This principal is awfully selfish (自分勝手), Naomi says, and mustard on sushi is disgusting; Yuichi agrees. Has Yuichi ever eaten unusual sushi (変わったおすし)? Yes: pineapple sushi, with whipped cream (生クリーム付き), at a conveyor-belt sushi bar (回転寿司), and in Japan, not America, where Yuichi has never been. Yuichi liked it. Naomi once had ice-cream sushi, with strawberry ice cream on top, in Tottori Prefecture: not that bad. And American sushi is delicious, Peter says: the best is the spicy tuna roll, which Peter hardly ever sees in Japan and eats on every trip home. Those rolls are long, Peter adds: thirty or forty centimetres, against ten or so in Japan. It depends on the width of the nori, Naomi decides.

Phrases worth taking from the chat:

  • 無視しないで下さい — “don’t ignore me”.
  • 皆さんお疲れなんですね — “you must all be tired, then”: 〜んです explaining what Naomi sees (section 6).
  • 今からわかりますよ — “you’re about to find out”.
  • 変わったおすし — “unusual sushi”: 変わった, “odd, unusual”, before a noun.
  • 食べたことがあります — “I’ve eaten it”: 〜たことがある, for experiences.
  • 海苔の幅によるかな — “maybe it depends on the width of the nori”: 〜による, “to depend on”.
3

Key lines

The principal’s three lines that carry the grammar

Three of the dialogue’s eleven lines, quoted exactly; section 1 covers the rest in my retelling. The English is my own rendering.

え！？あ……わさび君くん、僕ぼくはね、わさびが大嫌だいきらいなんだよ！緑みどりだし、ツンと来くるし！からしの方ほうが断然だんぜんおいしいじゃないか！

What!? Oh… Wasabi, you see, I hate wasabi! It’s green, and it stings your nose! Mustard’s far tastier, isn’t it? — the principal

Grammar: わさび君, the principal’s way of addressing a pupil; 僕はね, with ね holding the listener’s attention; 大嫌いなんだよ, のだ explaining, “the thing is, I hate it”; 緑だし、ツンと来るし, し piling up reasons (all in section 6); からしの方が断然おいしい, a comparison made stronger with 断然 (section 4); じゃないか, “isn’t it?”, demanding agreement.

ええええーーー！？まったく、分わからん子こだ……。君きみにこんな話はなしを続つづけていくのは無駄むだだ。

Whaaat!? Honestly, you just don’t get it… It’s pointless to keep on talking to you about this. — the principal

Grammar: 分からん = 分からない, with the older ん negative, as in lesson 5’s すまん; 続けていく, today’s 〜ていく, carrying on from now (section 5); 〜のは無駄だ, “it’s pointless to”.

しかし、こんな所ところを見みられたら君きみを生いかしてかえすわけにはいかない。

But now that you’ve seen me here like this, I can’t let you go back alive. — the principal

Grammar: こんな所を見られたら, the passive + たら: “having been seen in a place like this”; 生かしてかえす, the て-form saying how: “let you live and send you back” (section 6); 〜わけにはいかない, “I can’t possibly” (section 6).

Vocabulary

The lesson’s word list, in its order

The hosts’ notes first, then a card for each word, plus a few more from the lesson notes and the chat, each with an example of my own.

  • ここらへん (ここら辺) — near here, around here: very casual and spoken. Naomi uses このへん more, and so does Peter, who has never used ここらへん. Yuichi does, though, and suggests it may be something men say among friends: ここら辺に、寿司屋さんがあるんじゃないか.
  • おでん — “Japanese hodgepodge”, as the hosts translate it. Yuichi likes it, but you can’t put wasabi on it: oden takes からし. Peter knows some good oden places, but pictures the convenience-store kind, sitting in an open pot by the till: a “bacteria festival” (ばい菌祭り). Before Yuichi came in, Naomi and Peter had a bet: does Yuichi eat convenience-store oden? No, said Naomi, since Yuichi lives with family; yes, said Peter, a poor student would happily eat it, bacteria and all. Yuichi does: it’s tasty somehow. Some bacteria taste good, Naomi agrees: yoghurt, natto, cheese. It’s about ¥100 an item, Yuichi says, and some shops now sell it in summer too. Leftover winter stock, Peter jokes, and proposes a pay rise for Yuichi; when Yuichi takes it up, Peter calls that ずうずうしい, “cheeky”.
  • 雑草 (ざっそう) — weed. 雑 means rough, crude, useless or mixed, Yuichi explains: 雑誌, a magazine, is mixed + articles, and 雑学, trivia, mixed + knowledge.
  • 取る — to take, to pick up. Mind the kanji, Naomi says: とる has many homonyms, written with different kanji for different meanings. 写真を撮る, to take a photo, isn’t today’s 取る.
  • 思う — to think. The quotation particle と comes before it, Yuichi says: おでんはおいしい becomes おでんはおいしいと思う, “I think oden is delicious”, or politely, と思います. But not おでんはおいしいです、と思う, Naomi warns: that sounds very strange. Use the plain form, with だ after nouns and な-adjectives: きれいだと思う; Naomi’s example is Peterさんは、お金持ちだと思う. Peter’s comeback, Naomi先生が、きれいだと思います, earns the reply that you don’t need the と思う there.
  • ツンと来る — pungent; to burn the inside of your nose, especially with wasabi. ツン is a mimetic word (擬態語) describing a state, Peter says: think of a rush. For Naomi, vinegar or ammonia does it too. It depends on the person.
  • 断然 (だんぜん) — absolutely, definitely, used when you compare things, Yuichi says: 私は肉より魚の方が断然好きだ, “I like fish far better than meat.” You can leave it out, Naomi adds, but 断然 adds emphasis, and you can even answer with just 断然好きだ. Naomi’s example: ベルギーのチョコレートは断然おいしい. Peter has never used 断然. Start now, Naomi says.

The lesson notes list a few more from the dialogue: 所, place; 大嫌い, to hate; シュウマイ, shumai dumplings; 無理, unreasonable; 続ける, to continue; and 無駄, waste. The cards below cover them; the kanji sheet’s five characters are in section 7.

Beyond the lessonFive ways to write とる. The government’s guide to kanji that share a reading (異字同訓, 2014) sorts them like this:

  • 取る — hold, get, note down, remove: 本を手に取る, 資格を取る, メモを取る, 年を取る.
  • 採る — gather, hire, adopt: きのこを採る, 新入社員を採る, 決を採る.
  • 捕る — catch: ねずみを捕る, 外野フライを捕る.
  • 撮る — shoot a photo or film: 写真を撮る, 映画を撮る.
  • 執る — wield, take charge: 筆を執る, 指揮を執る.

Some overlap: catching fish, 魚をとる, can be 取る or 捕る.

雑 has three shades in the dictionary: mixed up (混雑, crowding; 乱雑, a mess), miscellaneous, not the main thing (雑貨, sundries; 雑務, odd jobs), and rough, careless (雑な仕事, sloppy work). A 雑誌 is a periodical of mixed articles, and 雑学 a hoard of odds-and-ends knowledge.

つんと, in the dictionary, is a sharp smell that stings the nose, わさびがつんとする, but also a stand-offish manner, つんとすます, and something pointing up: 鼻がつんと高い.

所ところnoun

place, spot — こんな所, “a place like this”; also a point in time: 今のところ, “for now”

こんな所ところに店みせがあったなんて、知しらなかった。

I had no idea there was a shop in a place like this.

ここら辺へんnoun

around here — casual and spoken; この辺 is more common

ここら辺へんで、ちょっと休やすもうか。

Shall we take a break somewhere around here?

雑ざっ草そうnoun

weed — 雑, “mixed, miscellaneous”, as in 雑誌 and 雑学

夏なつは、庭にわの雑ざっ草そうがすぐに伸のびる。

In summer, the weeds in the garden shoot up in no time.

取とるう-verb

to take, pick up, get, pass — not 撮る, for photos

思おもうう-verb

大嫌だいきらいな-adjective

ツンと来くるphrase

無理むりがあるphrase

We kept on practising, even in the rain.

無駄むだな-adjective · noun

自分勝手じぶんかってな-adjective

I don’t want to go travelling with selfish people.

5

〜ていく, from now on

The grammar point: a change or an action that carries on

Today’s grammar point is 〜ていく again, which has several uses, Peter explains. The first, an action carried on as you go, was in Wasabi Kunoichi 1 (lesson 5). The second, today’s, is a state that keeps changing after a point in time, and that point can be now, in the future or in the past. Compare 〜てくる from Wasabi Kunoichi 2 (lesson 6), a change in progress up to a point: てくる is what has been developing, ていく what will go on developing. In the dialogue: こんな話を続けていくのは無駄だ, “there’s no use carrying on with this”. 続ける is to continue; 続けていく, to continue on.

Naomi’s example: 毎日JapanesePod101.comを聞けば、日本語が上手になる, “listen every day and your Japanese will get better”. Yuichi gives it the ていく: 上手になっていく, little by little, day by day, Naomi says; in a continuing fashion, says Peter. Plain になる could be today, tomorrow, someday: it says nothing about time, or how it will happen.

The lesson notes add that 話を続けるのは would mean almost the same, but 続けていく stresses the process: going on talking, non-stop, from now into the future. The starting point can be anywhere in time:

Watch forWhich ていく is it? With a verb of change (なる, 増える, 減る, 変わる) it’s usually about time, today’s use. With a verb of moving or carrying (走る, 持つ, 寄る) it’s usually about going away, lesson 5’s use.

6

のだ, し and the て-form

Three more points from the lesson’s grammar list

Besides 〜ていく, the lesson’s grammar list marks three more points as used in the dialogue, and the principal leans on all of them. The explanations are mine.

1

のだ: the reason is…

Plain form + のだ, in speech んだ, politely んです, presents something as an explanation, or asks for one. 何しているんだね is what is it you’re doing?; 大嫌いなんだよ is the thing is, I hate it. After a noun or な-adjective it becomes なのだ: 大嫌いなんだ.

2

し: and what’s more…

し after a plain form lists reasons, one on top of another: 緑だし、ツンと来るし, “it’s green, and it stings your nose”. The conclusion, “so I hate it”, can be left unsaid. Nouns and な-adjectives take だ first: 緑だし, 静かだし.

3

The て-form: and, -ing

It links actions, or says how one is done: 生かしてかえす, “let you live and send you back”, is sending someone back alive. A sentence can also stop on it, leaving the rest unsaid: 雑草でも取ろうと思って, “I thought I’d pull some weeds, (and so…)”.

Beyond the lessonThree more of the principal’s habits, with the dictionary’s help:

  • 〜わけにはいかない — “I can’t do that; it wouldn’t be right”: not impossible, but out of the question. 生かしてかえすわけにはいかない; in everyday life, 休むわけにはいかない, “I can’t possibly take the day off”. With a negative in front, やらないわけにはいかない is “I have no choice but to do it”.
  • こそ picks out a word and stresses it: Wasabi’s 校長こそ何しているんですか, “what are you doing, Principal?”; 今こそ, “now, if ever”. As a whole reply, こちらこそ: “no, it’s me who should (thank you)”.
  • くん for a girl. Dictionaries give くん as a friendly suffix for an equal or a junior, used mainly by men, with no rule that the person must be a boy. Japan’s Diet still calls every member 〜君: on 26 March 2026 the Speaker of the House of Representatives called on 内閣総理大臣高市早苗君, Prime Minister Takaichi Sanae.

実じつは、来月らいげつ引ひっ越こすんです。

Actually, I’m moving next month.

この部屋へやは広ひろいし、駅えきにも近ちかいし、とても気きに入いっている。

This flat is roomy, it’s close to the station: I really like it.

雨あめが降ふってきたので、走はしって帰かえった。

It started to rain, so I ran home.

Kanji close-up

The five characters from the lesson’s kanji sheet

The lesson’s kanji sheet picks five characters from the dialogue, 所, 取, 思, 来 and 続, with a reading and a meaning for each and a grid to practise writing them. The other readings, the words and the notes here are mine. Copy each one a few times, as the sheet suggests.

所ところplace · 8 strokes

所placeショ・ところ

The principal’s こんな所, “a place like this”. In compounds it’s しょ, or voiced, じょ: 住所 じゅうしょ, address; 近所 きんじょ, the neighbourhood; 教習所 きょうしゅうじょ, driving school. And ところ turns into どころ in 台所 だいどころ, kitchen. It is built from 戸 and 斤, an axe; why, scholars disagree.

取とるtake · 8 strokes

取takeシュ・と(る)

A hand (又) holding an ear (耳). The traditional story is a left ear taken as proof of a kill, from game or, in a darker telling, a fallen enemy. Read シュ in 取材 しゅざい, reporting; と(る) in 受け取る うけとる, to receive, and 取り消す とりけす, to cancel. Its look-alike verbs are in section 4.

思おもうthink · 9 strokes

思thinkシ・おも(う)

The top looks like 田, a field, but began as a sketch of the top of the head; underneath is 心, the heart. Read シ in 思想 しそう, thought, ideology, and 不思議 ふしぎ, mysterious; おも(う) in 思い出 おもいで, a memory.

ツンと来くるcome · 7 strokes

来comeライ・く(る)

It began as a picture of a wheat plant, and was borrowed very early for “come”, a word that sounded the same. Read ライ in 来週 らいしゅう, next week, and 将来 しょうらい, the future. As a verb, its reading shifts with the form: 来る くる, 来ます きます, 来ない こない.

続つづけるcontinue · 13 strokes

続continueゾク・つづ(く)・つづ(ける)

Thread (糸), for things joined in a line, plus a sound part now written like 売. 続く is intransitive, “to go on”, and 続ける transitive, “to keep something going”: 雨が続く, 話を続ける. Read ゾク in 連続 れんぞく, in a row; つづ in 手続き てつづき, paperwork, procedures.

Oden at the konbini

Beyond the lesson: Peter’s “bacteria festival”, and the principal’s mustard

Beyond the lessonBy the till since 1979. Seven-Eleven says it was the first chain to sell oden as everyday fast food. After test sales in the Kanto area in its early years, it developed an oden warmer in 1979 to sell oden at the counter, and in 1982 added bottled stock so that every shop’s tasted the same. The season starts somewhere between late August and late September, depending on the chain and the year.

Peter’s bacteria. The open pot did cause trouble. Around 2016, a man filmed himself poking the oden in a shop with his finger, the “oden poking” case (おでんツンツン); he was arrested, though not charged. In February 2019, a video filmed at a Seven-Eleven in Yokohama showed a piece of shirataki going into someone’s mouth and back into the pot, and the company apologised. Then COVID-19 changed the pot for good. In 2020 Lawson put an acrylic shield in front of it and kept the lid shut, and FamilyMart had staff serve every item, moved the pot behind the counter at most shops, and added pouches, cups and microwave oden.

The price. Yuichi’s “about ¥100 an item” is from 2008. In 2025, Seven-Eleven’s oden cost about ¥131 to ¥150 an item with tax, though on 23 September 2026 Lawson Store 100 launched ¥108 packs of oden.

Not always からし. Oden usually comes with からし, as Yuichi says: even Shizuoka’s dark, skewered oden, sprinkled with green laver (青のり) and fish powder (だし粉), gets a dab of it. In Aichi, though, oden is dipped in a sweet red-miso sauce, and oden bought at a convenience store there comes with a packet of miso sauce.

Shumai and spicy tuna. Japan’s best-known shumai is Kiyoken’s シウマイ from Yokohama, made since 1928 with pork and dried scallop, and since 1955 sold with soy sauce in a little porcelain bottle called ひょうちゃん. And Peter’s spicy tuna roll is usually said to have been created in the United States in the 1980s, often in Los Angeles, though a Seattle restaurant also claims it.

Quiz

Pick the word or form that fits. Each answer comes with the reason.

1. これから、だんだん暖あたたかく＿＿＿でしょう。

It will gradually get warmer from now on.

A change from now on: 〜ていく. A change up to now would be 〜てきた.

2. 最近さいきん、日本語にほんごのニュースが分わかるように＿＿＿。

Lately I’ve started to understand the news in Japanese.

A change that has built up until now: 〜てきた.

3. この仕事しごとを、これからも＿＿＿つもりです。

I mean to keep doing this job from now on.

Carrying on from now into the future: 続けていく.

4. 人じん口こうは、この先さきも＿＿＿と言いわれている。

They say the population will keep falling from here on.

この先も, “from here on”: a change continuing into the future, 〜ていく.

5. この店みせは安やすい＿＿＿、おいしいし、よく来くるんです。

This place is cheap, the food’s good, so I come here a lot.

し lists reasons, one after another: 安いし、おいしいし.

6. 「どうしたの？」「財布さいふをなくした＿＿＿。」

“What’s wrong?” “I’ve lost my wallet.”

Explaining what’s going on: 〜んです, the spoken のです.

7. 明日あしたは雨あめ＿＿＿と思おもいます。

I think it’ll rain tomorrow.

Before と思う, a noun or な-adjective takes だ, never です.

8. 「いつもありがとう。」「こちら＿＿＿、ありがとう。」

“Thanks for everything.” “No, thank you.”

こそ puts the stress on what comes before it: こちらこそ, “it’s me who should thank you”.

9. 私わたしは、夏なつより冬ふゆの方ほうが＿＿＿好すきだ。

I like winter far more than summer.

断然: by far, in a comparison.

10. いくら言いっても＿＿＿だよ。彼かれは聞きかないから。

It’s no use, however much you tell him. He won’t listen.

無駄: pointless, a waste.

11. 旅行りょこうで、写真しゃしんをたくさん＿＿＿。

I took lots of photos on the trip.

Photos and films are 撮る. 取る is taking in general; 採る, gathering or hiring.

12. 棚たなの上うえの箱はこを＿＿＿くれる？

Could you get the box down from the shelf?

Taking hold of something: 取る.

13. 「＿＿＿に、コンビニある？」「駅えきの前まえにあるよ。」

“Is there a convenience store around here?” “There’s one by the station.”

ここら辺: around here, casually. この辺 means the same.

14. 大事だいじな会議かいぎだから、休やすむ＿＿＿。

It’s an important meeting, so I can’t possibly take the day off.

〜わけにはいかない: you can’t, because it wouldn’t be right. The principal’s 帰すわけにはいかない.
