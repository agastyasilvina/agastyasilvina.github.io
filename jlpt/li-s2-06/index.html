The dialogue

The whole scene, retold for review

The lesson’s dialogue, retold in my own wording so you can review it in one place. It keeps the same scene, characters, words and grammar, and carries on from my retelling of lesson 5, where the boss would use nothing but real wasabi. Every line is rephrased, and a few are new, including the principal’s last one. The lesson’s exact key lines are in section 3. Hide the English with the button at the top to test yourself.

Scene

Back at the sushi bar. The boss has been out since dawn, hunting for real wasabi.

Boss

ただいま……。

I’m back…

Wasabi

あ、おかえりなさい！どうでした、大たい将しょう？市場いちばに本ほんわさび、ありましたか？

Oh, welcome back! How did it go, boss? Did they have real wasabi at the market?

Boss

市場いちばまで行いってきたんだが、どうやら伊豆中いずじゅうのわさびが消きえちまったらしい。

I went all the way to the market and back, but it looks like the wasabi’s vanished from the whole of Izu.

Boss

隣となりのそば屋やも、わさびが全部ぜんぶからしに変かわってたそうだ。

Apparently the soba place next door found all its wasabi turned to mustard too.

Boss

いったい誰だれの仕業しわざなんだ……。このままじゃ、店みせを開あけられねえよ。

Who on earth is behind this? At this rate, I can’t open the shop.

Wasabi

大たい将しょう、私わたし、ちょっと出でかけてきます！

Boss, I’m just popping out for a bit!

Boss

お、おう？どこへ行いくんだい、わさびちゃん！暗くらくなる前まえに帰かえってくるんだぞ！

Eh? Where are you off to, Wasabi? Be back before dark, you hear!

Scene

Dusk, at a wasabi field deep in the mountains of Izu.

Wasabi

犯人はんにんは、きっとこのわさび畑ばたけにも来きているはず……。

The culprit is bound to have come to this wasabi field too…

Principal

まったく、どこもかしこもわさびだらけだ……。一本いっぽん残のこらず捨すててやる！

Honestly, there’s wasabi everywhere… I’ll throw out every last root!

Wasabi

あっ……こ、校こう長ちょう先生せんせい！？

Ah… P-Principal?!

Principal

しまった……見みられたか！

Blast… I’ve been seen!

2

The scene

Naomi, Yuichi and Peter, before and after the dialogue

This time Naomi is joined by Yuichi, in a Lower Intermediate lesson for the first time, with Peter. Naomi brings everyone up to speed: Wasabi is a high-school student with a part-time job at a sushi bar, and last time all the shop’s wasabi disappeared and turned into からし (lesson 5). What does Yuichi think of the story? It sounds really interesting, Yuichi says. Really? Naomi asks, and Peter doesn’t believe it either: that wasn’t Yuichi’s 本音. What’s 本音, Naomi asks, a real opinion? What someone really thinks, Peter says: their true feeling. So if it isn’t your 本音, are you lying? No, Yuichi protests, it’s true. うそっぽいな, says Naomi: sounds like a lie.

After the dialogue

So who was the culprit (犯人)? Actually, Yuichi says, it was the principal: 校長先生. But why? Maybe the school is short of money, Peter suggests. Naomi doesn’t know, but has an opinion about the very last line: Wasabi says あ、校長！, and that’s rude. It needs 先生 after it. Even if the principal is the villain, Peter agrees, the title still needs its honorific. And Yuichi, asked who it was, said 校長先生 straight off, without thinking (思わず). Yuichi agrees with Naomi: you have to add 先生. Even for someone bad, then, Peter concludes. That’s why you call me Naomi Sensei, Naomi says. And even if I don’t like you, Peter teases, I still add 先生: no choice, Naomi-sensei. At the very end Yuichi signs off with a plain さよなら, and Peter calls that cold (冷たい).

Phrases worth taking from the chat:

  • 今までのストーリーをちょっと見ていきたいと思います — “let’s go over the story so far”: 見ていく, looking through things one by one, with lesson 5’s 〜ていく.
  • 本音じゃない — “that’s not what you really think”.
  • うそっぽいな — “sounds like a lie”: 〜っぽい, “-ish, like”.
  • 思わず言いましたね — “you said it without thinking”: 思わず, “in spite of yourself”.
  • 先生を付けなきゃいけない — “you have to add 先生”: 〜なきゃいけない, the casual 〜なければいけない.
  • 好きではなくても — “even if I don’t like you”: 〜ても, “even if”.

Beyond the lesson本音 and 建前. Dictionaries define 本音 as words that come from your true feelings, often the kind you hesitate to say out loud, and pair it with 建前: the principle you officially stand by, the view you show in public. In the hosts’ teasing, Yuichi’s “it sounds really interesting” is 建前; what someone admits over a drink is 本音.

Why さよなら sounds cold. さようなら began as さようならば, “if that is so, then…”, and it’s the first goodbye textbooks teach. In everyday life it can sound final, as if you won’t meet again soon, so friends say じゃあね or またね, and colleagues leaving work say お先に失礼します. The hosts usually end with それでは、また.

Key lines

Three lines that carry the grammar

Three of the dialogue’s nine lines, quoted exactly; section 1 covers the rest in my retelling. The English is my own rendering.

どうやら伊豆中いずじゅうのわさびが消きえちまったらしい。隣となりのソバ屋やのわさびも全部ぜんぶからしに変かわったそうだ。

It looks like the wasabi has vanished from all over Izu. Apparently all the wasabi at the soba place next door has turned into mustard too. — the boss

Grammar: どうやら〜らしい, “it would seem that” (section 6); 伊豆中, “the whole of Izu”, with the suffix 〜中 (section 4); 消えちまった, a rough way of saying 消えてしまった, which is why the lesson notes print it as 消えち（てし）まった; 変わったそうだ, “I hear it changed”: plain form + そうだ passes on what someone said (section 6).

大たい将しょう、ちょっと出でかけてきます。

Boss, I’m popping out for a bit. — Wasabi

Grammar: 出かけて + きます: go out, and come back. The lesson notes give it as the example of today’s 〜てくる (section 5).

きっと、ここにもわさび泥棒どろぼうが来きているはず……。

I bet the wasabi thief has been here too… — Wasabi

Grammar: きっと, “surely”; ここにも, “here too”; わさび泥棒, “wasabi thief”; 来ている, “has come”: with verbs of moving, 〜ている describes the result; はず, “it must be so”, worked out rather than seen (section 6).

Watch forWasabi’s 来ている isn’t today’s 〜てくる: it’s 来る + 〜ている, “has come (and may still be here)”. In the same way, 行っている is “has gone, is away”, and 帰っている, “is back home”.

Beyond the lessonThe boss talks as roughly as in lesson 5 (section 7 there), and the principal joins in:

  • 消えちまった — ちまう is a rough てしまう, and the older form of today’s everyday ちゃう.
  • 開ける事ができねぇ — ない roughened to ねぇ.
  • どこへ行くんだい — 〜んだい, a gruff but friendly way to ask.
  • 全部捨ててやる — 〜てやる with strong will: “I’ll throw the lot out, just you watch”.
4

Vocabulary

The lesson’s word list, in its order

The hosts’ notes first, then a card for each word, plus a few more from the dialogue and the chat, each with an example of my own.

  • 〜中 (じゅう) — a suffix, “everywhere in”, as in the dialogue’s 伊豆中, “the whole of Izu”. Also 日本中, all over Japan, and, Yuichi supplies, 世界中, all over the world. The meaning shifts a little with words for time, Peter notes. Naomi: “なになにじゅう” is used a great deal.
  • となり (隣) — next to, next door. Naomi asks Yuichi about the film となりのトトロ, My Neighbor Totoro: every Japanese person knows it, Yuichi says. Peter asks for the story in thirty seconds. Naomi has forgotten it, and Yuichi doesn’t remember it well enough to explain. Everyone knows it, Peter concludes, but nobody knows what happens in it. A Totoro turns up, Naomi offers.
  • 変わる — to change, intransitive; the transitive partner, Yuichi says, is 変える. 季節が変わります: the seasons change. Nobody changes them, Peter points out: they change on their own, with no direct object. With 変える: 髪形を変えます, “I change my hairstyle”, or with its subject, 私は髪形を変えます. Both at once: 季節が変わったので、髪形を変えます, “the season has changed, so I’m changing my hairstyle”, with を marking the object.
  • わさび畑 (わさびばたけ) — a wasabi field. 畑 on its own is はたけ, but after another word it becomes ばたけ: 花畑, a flower field; 麦畑, a wheat field. Naomi brings up The Catcher in the Rye, which is ライ麦畑でつかまえて in Japanese.
  • 泥棒 (どろぼう) — thief, burglar, robber. どろ is mud, Yuichi says, and ぼう a stick: a bar, stick or pole, Naomi adds. They’re ateji (当て字), Peter explains: kanji picked for their sound rather than their meaning. Naomi’s proverb: うそは泥棒のはじまり, “show me a liar and I’ll show you a thief”. Peter teases Naomi for knowing so much about the subject.
  • 校長 (こうちょう) — principal, head teacher. 校 is school and 長 the head, the chief, Yuichi explains, and 長 works as a suffix: 社長, company president, and, Naomi adds, 市長, mayor.

The lesson notes list a few more from the dialogue: 店, shop; 売る, to sell; 消える, to vanish; ソバ屋, a soba shop; 〜の仕業, someone’s doing; 開ける, to open; ちょっと, a little; 全部, all; and 捨てる, to throw away. The cards below include the ones most worth learning.

Beyond the lessonTwo readings of 中. Read じゅう after a word, 中 means throughout: a whole stretch of time (一日中, all day; 一年中, all year), a whole place (日本中, 世界中, 伊豆中), or everyone in a group (学校中, the whole school). Read ちゅう, it means in the middle of doing something: 授業中, in class; 工事中, under construction. It can also set a deadline, 今週中, “by the end of this week”: the shift with time words that Peter mentions.

From はたけ to ばたけ is rendaku (連濁): in a compound, the second word’s first sound often turns voiced, as when たこ, octopus, becomes おおだこ, a big octopus, or すし becomes まきずし. It doesn’t happen when the second word already has a voiced sound such as ぜ or げ: やま + かぜ stays やまかぜ, and おお + とかげ stays おおとかげ. Linguists call this Lyman’s law, after Benjamin Lyman, who described it in 1894.

泥棒’s kanji. The big dictionaries simply list two spellings, 泥棒 and 泥坊. The word’s origin is uncertain, and an etymology dictionary agrees with the hosts that the kanji were chosen for their sound. Naomi’s proverb usually goes 嘘つきは泥棒の始まり: someone who lies without a second thought will soon think nothing of stealing.

Beyond the lessonThe thirty-second summary. となりのトトロ, My Neighbor Totoro, is Studio Ghibli’s film by Miyazaki Hayao, released on 16 April 1988. With their mother in hospital, two sisters, Satsuki and four-year-old Mei, move with their father, an archaeologist, to a house in the clean country air, where they meet Totoro. Totoro is in the studio’s logo.

ライ麦畑でつかまえて is the title Nozaki Takashi (野崎孝) gave J. D. Salinger’s novel in his 1964 translation. The first Japanese version, in 1952, was called 危険な年齢, “The Dangerous Age”, and Murakami Haruki’s translation of 2003 simply put the English title into katakana: キャッチャー・イン・ザ・ライ.

5

〜てくる

The grammar point: go, do something, and come back

Today’s grammar point is 〜てくる. It has several meanings, Peter explains, and this lesson takes the easiest; the lesson notes cover the others. Here it means doing something and then coming back, as in 行ってきます, the everyday goodbye when you leave somewhere you mean to come back to. The hosts take it apart:

1

行って

The て-form of 行く, to go: the first action.

2

きます

来る, to come: the second action. So literally go, and come: go, then come back.

3

Any verb in the middle

Yuichi’s example: お弁当を買ってきます, “I’ll go and buy a bento, and come back”. Naomi’s: ちょっとランチ食べてきます. To name the place, add it: 銀行へ行ってきます, “I’m off to the bank”. You leave, do something, and come back, Peter sums up.

The lesson notes go further. With 来る, the movement is towards the speaker, the opposite of 行く, and after a て-form, くる has four uses. Today’s is the second.

UseExample (mine)
1. Coming here, after or while doing something昼ひるごはんは食たべてきた, I had lunch before I came; 駅えきから走はしってきた, I ran here from the station
2. Going to do something, and coming backちょっと出でかけてきます, I’m popping out; 傘かさを取とってくる, I’ll go and get an umbrella
3. An action aimed at the speaker母ははが荷物にもつを送おくってきた, my mother sent me a parcel; 夜中よなかに電話でんわをかけてきた, they phoned me in the middle of the night
4. A change in progress雨あめが強つよくなってきた, the rain has been getting heavier; だんだん慣なれてくるよ, you’ll get used to it

The dialogue opens with the other half of this: ただいま, “I’m back”. The set phrases come in pairs.

Watch forWhere you are decides between てくる and ていく. Coming back to where you are now: 買ってくる. Taking it somewhere else: 買っていく. Lesson 5, section 6 has the pairs side by side.

Hearsay and hunches

Beyond the lesson: the boss’s らしい and そうだ, and Wasabi’s はず

Beyond the lessonNobody in the dialogue has seen the thief, so three lines report what they’ve heard or worked out, each with a different ending. Together they’re a core set for N3.

EndingWhat it says
どうやら〜らしいit seems, it would appear: a conclusion from what you’ve heard or seen. 伊豆中のわさびが消えちまったらしい
plain form + そうだI hear, they say: passing on what someone told you. 全部からしに変わったそうだ
きっと〜はずit must be, it should be: what you expect, worked out from what you know. ここにも来ているはず
  • らしい comes from evidence, often second-hand, and どうやら in front makes it a careful “it would seem”. It’s also a suffix meaning “typical of”, as in 〜らしくない, “not like you”.
  • Hearsay そうだ often names its source with 〜によると, “according to”. It follows a plain form: 降るそうだ, 変わったそうだ.
  • はず is your own reasoning. Its negative, はずがない, is “there’s no way”.

どうやら風邪かぜを引ひいたらしい。頭あたまが痛いたい。

I seem to have caught a cold. My head hurts.

ニュースによると、来月らいげつから電車でんしゃの運賃うんちんが上あがるそうだ。

According to the news, train fares are going up next month.

鍵かぎは、かばんに入いれたはずなんだけど……。

I’m sure I put the key in my bag…

あの真面目まじめな人ひとが、うそをつくはずがない。

Someone as honest as that would never lie.

Watch forそうだ has two lives. After a plain form it’s hearsay: 雨が降るそうだ, “I hear it’s going to rain”. After the ます-stem, or an adjective’s stem, it’s how things look: 雨が降りそうだ, “it looks like rain”; おいしそう, “looks delicious”; lesson 4’s 嬉しそうに, “happily”.

7

Titles as names

Beyond the lesson: why Wasabi should have said 校長先生

Beyond the lessonThe hosts are right: in Japanese a title often does the job of a name, and it carries its own respect. Teachers, doctors and members of parliament, among others, are 先生, said on its own like “you”, or after a name: 田中先生. A head teacher is 校長先生, and pupils say it without thinking, just as Yuichi did. At work, the title is how most people address the boss: in the Agency for Cultural Affairs’ language survey published on 2 October 2026, asked what a subordinate calls a section chief (課長), 62.9% said plain 課長, 25.5% surname + さん, and only 6.4% 課長さん, a choice that is fading and now most common among people over 70.

Two traps. Talking to someone from outside your company, you lower your own side: your boss Tanaka becomes plain 田中, or 部長の田中, not 田中部長. And since a title is already respectful, business-etiquette guides say not to add 様 to it: 社長様 doubles up, so write 山田社長, or on an envelope, 代表取締役社長　山田様. A letter to a teacher goes to 山田一郎先生, with no 様.

The 長 of 校長 makes a whole family of heads:

社しゃ長ちょうa company: its president
会かい長ちょうa company’s board, or an association: its chair
部ぶ長ちょうa department; also a school club’s captain
課か長ちょうa section, inside a department
店てん長ちょうa shop or restaurant: its manager (lesson 5)
市し長ちょうa city: its mayor
院いん長ちょうa hospital or clinic

I’ve brought you the papers for tomorrow’s meeting. — to one’s department head, addressed by title

田中たなか先生せんせい、ちょっと質問しつもんしてもいいですか。

Tanaka-sensei, may I ask a quick question?

院いん長ちょう先生せんせいは、今日きょうはお休やすみです。

The head doctor is off today.

8

Izu, wasabi country

Beyond the lesson: why the thief went to Izu

Beyond the lessonWhy Izu. The thief picked the right place. Wasabi growing began about 400 years ago at Utōgi (有東木), in the hills above Shizuoka City, and spread from there to the Izu Peninsula, where it is grown in the cold spring water of the Amagi mountains (天城). The 畳石式 method, terraced beds built up from layers of stones, was developed in Izu around 1892.

A world-heritage farm. When the UN’s Food and Agriculture Organization recognised Shizuoka’s traditional wasabi growing in 2018 (lesson 5, section 8), the area it covered was three cities and four towns: Shizuoka City in the Southern Alps, and Izu City, Shimoda, Higashi-Izu, Kawazu, Matsuzaki and Nishi-Izu on the peninsula. In 2016 that area grew 227.5 tonnes of wasabi root, over 40% of Japan’s crop.

Who grows the most? It depends what you count. Japan grew 1,476 tonnes of wasabi in 2025, mainly in Nagano, Shizuoka and Iwate, and by total weight Nagano comes first. But of the root, the part you grate, Shizuoka grew 53% of the country’s 397 tonnes in 2024, and earned 63% of the money from wasabi.

To see it: at Jōren Falls (浄蓮の滝) in Izu City, a 25-metre waterfall in the Amagi range, terraced wasabi beds line the stream below, and the visitor centre’s speciality is wasabi soft-serve, one of the wasabi treats the hosts recommended in lesson 5.

Quiz

Pick the word or form that fits. Each answer comes with the reason.

1. （家いえを出でるとき、家族かぞくに）「＿＿＿！」

(Leaving home, to your family) “I’m off!”

行ってきます: “I’ll go, and come back.” 行ってらっしゃい is the reply from whoever stays home.

2. ちょっとコンビニで飲のみ物ものを＿＿＿ね。

I’m just going to grab a drink from the convenience store.

Go, buy it, and come back: 買ってくる. 買っていく is buying something to take away with you.

3. 寒さむかったので、セーターを＿＿＿。

It was cold, so I came wearing a sweater.

You arrived here wearing it: てくる, towards where you are now.

4. 昔むかしの友達ともだちが、急きゅうに電話でんわを＿＿＿。

An old friend suddenly called me.

An action aimed at you: 電話をかけてくる, “to call me”.

5. だんだん寒さむく＿＿＿ね。

It’s been getting colder, hasn’t it?

A change that has built up until now: 〜てきた.

6. 夏休なつやすみは、一日いちにち＿＿＿家いえでゲームをしていた。

In the summer holidays, I played games at home all day long.

一日中, いちにちじゅう: all day long. Read ちゅう, as in 会議中, 中 means “in the middle of”.

7. 季節きせつが＿＿＿と、店みせのメニューも変かわる。

When the season changes, so does the shop’s menu.

Nobody changes the seasons: intransitive 変わる, with が.

8. 新あたらしい学校がっこうで、髪形かみがたを＿＿＿。

At my new school, I changed my hairstyle.

You change it: transitive 変える, with を.

9. 隣となりの家いえに、＿＿＿が入はいったらしい。

Apparently the house next door was burgled.

泥棒が入る: a burglar breaks in. And らしい: “apparently”.

10. 天気てんき予報よほうによると、明日あしたは雪ゆきが＿＿＿そうだ。

According to the forecast, it’s going to snow tomorrow.

Hearsay そうだ follows the plain form: 降るそうだ, “I hear it’ll snow”. 降りそうだ is “it looks like snow”.

11. このケーキ、とても＿＿＿。

This cake looks delicious.

Looks like: the stem + そう, おいしそう. おいしいそうだ means “I hear it’s delicious”.

12. 八時はちじに家いえを出でたから、もう駅えきに着ついている＿＿＿。

They left home at eight, so they should be at the station by now.

はず: what you expect, worked out from what you know.

13. 生徒せいとが校こう長ちょうを呼よぶときは、「＿＿＿」と言いう。

When pupils address the principal, they say “＿＿＿”.

Pupils say 校長先生, as the hosts insist: even to a villain.

14. うそつきは＿＿＿の始はじまり。

Lying is the first step to thieving.

The proverb: うそつきは泥棒の始まり. Naomi says it as うそは泥棒のはじまり.
