I’d like to open an account, but…

Part 1 of a new story, “Mo’ Money, Mo’ Problems”, set in a bank. Taiga, blond, handsome and Japanese, flirts all the way to the counter and opens an ordinary savings account. The grammar point is けど at the end of a sentence: 口座を開きたいんだけど, “I’d like to open an account…”, the request left for the listener to hear. Plus the polite が, the bank’s honorifics, seals, ID and receipts, and why the post office calls your savings 貯金.

Scene

A bank on a weekday morning. Taiga, blond and very pleased with life, strolls up to the floor guide.

Taiga

やあ、君きみ、かわいいね。銀行ぎんこうの仕事しごとって楽たのしい？あ、僕ぼく、ここで口座こうざを作つくりたいんだけど。

Hi there, aren’t you cute. Is bank work fun? Oh, I’d like to open an account here…

Guide

いらっしゃいませ。本日ほんじつは、外がい国こく人じん登とう録ろく証しょうとご印鑑いんかんはお持もちでしょうか。

Welcome. Do you have your alien registration card and your seal with you today?

Taiga

いやいや、お嬢じょうさん。髪かみはブロンドだし、ハンサムだけど、僕ぼく、日本人にほんじんなんだよ。

No, no, miss. Blond hair, yes, and handsome, sure, but I’m Japanese.

Guide

失礼しつれいいたしました。では、こちらの番号札ばんごうふだをお取とりになって、お呼よびするまでお待まちください。

My apologies. Then please take this number ticket and wait until we call you.

Announcement

89番ばんの番号札ばんごうふだをお持もちのお客きゃく様さま、3番ばんの窓口まどぐちまでお越こしください。

Customer with ticket 89, please come to window 3.

Announcement

90番ばんの番号札ばんごうふだをお持もちのお客きゃく様さま、1番ばんの窓口まどぐちまでお越こしください。

Ticket 90, window 1, please.

Announcement

91番ばんの番号札ばんごうふだをお持もちのお客きゃく様さま、2番ばんの窓口まどぐちまでお越こしください。

Ticket 91, window 2, please.

Taiga

91番ばん……あ、僕ぼくだ！はーい、91番ばん、ここでーす！

91… oh, that’s me! Here! Number 91, over here!

Taiga

どうも。あの、口座こうざを作つくりたいんですけど。

Hello. Um, I’d like to open an account…

Teller

普通預金ふつうよきんの口座こうざでよろしいでしょうか。

Would an ordinary savings account be all right?

Taiga

ふつう……よきん？

Ordinary… savings?

Teller

普通預金ふつうよきんは、お好すきなときに、お金かねの預あずけ入いれも引ひき出だしもできる口座こうざでございます。

An ordinary savings account is one you can pay money into and take it out of whenever you like.

Taiga

そうそう、それそれ。その普通預金ふつうよきんでお願ねがいします。

That’s it, that one. The ordinary savings, please.

Teller

では、運うん転てん免めん許きょ証しょうか健康保険証けんこうほけんしょうなどの身分証明書みぶんしょうめいしょと、公こう共きょう料りょう金きんの領りょう収しゅう証しょう、それからご印鑑いんかんをお願ねがいいたします。

Then may I have some ID, such as a driving licence or health insurance card, a utility bill receipt, and your seal?

Taiga

はいはい、全部ぜんぶありますよ。どうぞ。

Sure, I’ve got them all. Here you are.

Taiga

……ところで、お仕事しごと、何時なんじに終おわるのかなーって思おもってるんだけど。

…By the way, I was just wondering what time you finish work…

Teller

こちらの用紙ようしに、ご記入きにゅうをお願ねがいいたします。

The scene

Naomi, Yuichi and Peter, before and after the dialogue

“More Money, More Problems”, part 1. Does Naomi know Biggie Smalls, Peter asks? A very famous American rapper: ラッパー, Naomi corrects, since ラッパ is a trumpet; you hold the last vowel, Peter notes. The song goes “the more money I come across, the more problems I see”, in Peter’s Japanese あればあるほど問題が増える (lesson 17’s 〜ば〜ほど). But can’t money solve problems, Naomi asks? Not that, says Peter: the more you have, the more people want to borrow it, get jealous, or have plans for it. お金がなくてよかったです, says Naomi: “just as well I haven’t got much.” Spoken like someone without money, says Peter.

A new series begins, with lots of useful phrases for the bank; the grammar should be fairly easy, Naomi says. The story: Taigaさんという人が、銀行で口座を開こうとしています, Yuichi says. Taiga is talking to the bank staff, Naomi adds, so the Japanese is polite. If you live in Japan, or plan to, Peter says, these phrases are very useful.

After the dialogue

Writing this story was hard work, Naomi says: online research, phone calls, and even a trip to a bank during working hours just to hear the announcements. Superb research, Yuichi and Peter agree. Taiga is Japanese, Naomi points out, but foreigners opening an account need different things: the registration card, Peter adds, and depending on the bank a seal, 印鑑, something like a registered seal, Naomi suggests, though Peter doesn’t think it has to be registered. Peter has about five accounts (up to no good, Naomi wonders?): certain places want an account with a certain bank, さすが日本, and every bank has its own rules. Four of the five wanted a seal, and Peter’s is in katakana, ギャランテ: Yuichi has never seen a katakana はんこ, since they’re usually in kanji, but Peter in kanji would be strange, Naomi says. More places now let you open an account with just a signature, Peter adds; check before you go, Naomi advises.

How many accounts do they have? Naomi has three: the post office, Sumitomo Mitsui, and a smaller regional bank. Yuichi has two, at Mitsubishi UFJ and Mizuho. Peter’s quiz: which is Japan’s biggest bank? The Bank of Japan, says Naomi; you can’t deposit money there, Yuichi notes, but probably that. Peter says the post office is bigger: the world’s biggest bank is Japan’s postal savings. But it isn’t a bank, Naomi objects; it does exactly what a bank does, Peter says, and yet isn’t one. Its words are different too, Naomi says: not 預金 but 貯金, and for transfers not 振り込み but 送金.

Phrases worth taking from the chat:

  • あればあるほど — “the more you have, the more…”: 〜ば〜ほど.
  • わざわざ — “all the way, specially”: わざわざ銀行に行きました.
  • さすが日本ですね — “that’s Japan for you”.
  • 行く前に調べた方がいい — “better check before you go”.
  • 社会勉強になりました — “a lesson in how the world works”, Yuichi’s verdict in section 4.

Key lines

The opening line, the correction, and the bank’s shopping list

The lesson’s dialogue is fifteen lines long, three of them the same announcement, so these three are quoted exactly; section 1 covers the rest in my retelling. The English is my own rendering.

ねぇ、君きみ。かわいいね。銀行ぎんこうの仕事しごとは楽たのしいかい？僕ぼく、この銀行ぎんこうに口座こうざを開ひらきたいんだけど。

Hey, you. Aren’t you cute. Enjoying the bank work? I’d like to open an account at this bank… — Taiga

Grammar: 君, a familiar “you”; 楽しいかい, with the friendly, rather masculine かい (lesson 18); 口座を開く, to open an account; 開きたいんだけど, the grammar point: “I’d like to…”, with the request left unsaid (section 5).

お嬢じょうさん、確たしかに、僕ぼくは、ブロンドでハンサムだけど、外国人がいこくじんじゃないんだよ。

Miss, I’ll grant you I’m blond and handsome, but I’m not a foreigner. — Taiga

Grammar: お嬢さん, “miss”, a little cheeky here; 確かに…けど, “true, … but”, granting a point before the objection; ブロンドでハンサム, two descriptions joined with で; 外国人じゃないんだよ, the explaining んだ plus よ.

本日ほんじつ、運うん転てん免めん許きょ証しょうや健康保険証けんこうほけんしょうなどの身分証明書みぶんしょうめいしょと公こう共きょう料りょう金きんの領りょう収しゅう証しょう、それからご印鑑いんかんをお持もちですか。

Do you have with you today some ID, such as a driving licence or a health insurance card, a receipt for a utility bill, and your seal? — Teller

Grammar: 本日, the formal 今日; 〜や〜など, “such as… and so on”; AとB、それからC, a list; ご印鑑, your seal, with the polite ご; お持ちですか, a respectful 持っていますか.

Vocabulary

The hosts’ words, in their order

The hosts’ notes first, then a card for each word, plus more from the dialogue and the chat, each with an example of my own.

  • こうざ (口座) — a bank account: 銀行口座, a bank account; オンライン口座, an online account; and with 番号, number, 口座番号, the account number.
  • ふつうよきん (普通預金) — an ordinary savings account, formally 普通預金口座: 普通, ordinary; 預金, savings; 口座, account. And 預金 or 貯金? 預金 is the bank’s word and 貯金 the post office’s, Naomi says, news to Yuichi; but in conversation 貯金 is the usual word for savings. A piggy bank is a 貯金箱, Yuichi adds, never a 預金箱: it isn’t in a bank, says Naomi.
  • あずけいれ (預け入れ) — a deposit: 預ける, to deposit, and 入れる, to put in. Japanese ATMs are very advanced, Peter says: you can feed in fifty notes at once, maybe a hundred, Naomi thinks. When Peter first came to Japan, Peter put them in one at a time. すごい迷惑, says Naomi: a real nuisance for the queue.
  • はらいもどし (払い戻し) — paying back: 払う, to pay, and 戻す, to return. Naomi’s example: 普通預金はいつでも預け入れや払い戻しができる口座です.
  • ひきだし (引き出し) — a withdrawal: 引く, to pull, and 出す, to take out. Yuichi’s example: 普通預金はいつでも預け入れや引き出しができる口座です.
  • こうきょうりょうきん (公共料金) — utility charges, 電話、水道、ガス、電気などの料金, Yuichi explains. If you don’t pay, Naomi has heard, the phone is cut off first, then the gas, then the electricity, and the water last. Has Peter ever been cut off? No comment… then yes: the electricity, recently, while Peter’s wife was away and Peter, buried in work, forgot to pay and ignored the reminders. Pay, and it comes straight back on, Peter says; everyone should try it once. No thanks, says Yuichi.
  • りょうしゅうしょう (領収証) — a receipt: 領収する, to receive, and 証, a certificate or proof; or 書, writing: 領収書 and 領収証 mean the same. Ask for one and they’ll ask for the あてな, the name to make it out to: お名前は？ You can give your company’s name, or the usual 上様, Peter says. Naomi just says 何も書かないで, “leave it blank”, and has never been refused; some places do refuse, Peter says, for expensive things like electronics, but if you hand over a business card, 名刺, they’ll copy it. 社会勉強になりました, says Yuichi.
  • いんかん (印鑑) — a personal seal; the dialogue adds the honorific ご, ご印鑑. In Japan a seal is often used instead of a signature, Naomi says, though foreigners may be told a signature will do: ここにご印鑑をお願いします. When Peter first came to Japan and heard ご印鑑お願いします, Peter heard ご意見お願いします and gave an opinion.
  • みぶんしょうめいしょ (身分証明書) — ID: 身分, one’s position in society; 証明, proof; 書, a document. 身分証明書をお持ちですか？, “do you have any ID?”, where お持ちですか is an honorific 持っていますか.
  • うんてんめんきょしょう, けんこうほけんしょう and がいこくじんとうろくしょう (運転免許証, 健康保険証, 外国人登録証) — a driving licence, a health insurance card, an alien registration card: all of them work as ID, Naomi says.

The lesson notes add お嬢さん, “young lady, miss”, and their sample sentence uses it for someone else’s daughter. The cards below cover them all, with more words from the dialogue.

5

〜けど, left unsaid

The grammar point, from the lesson notes: say what you want, and let them offer

The grammar point is けど at the end of a sentence, as in この銀行に口座を開きたいんだけど. けど is the casual form of けれども, “but”, which normally joins two clauses. At the end of a sentence it can follow what you want to do, when you’d like permission or help to do it. Taiga only states a wish, the lesson notes explain, but it implies a request: please show me how.

1

ます-stem + たいんだけど

食べたいんだけど, 帰りたいんですけど. “I want to…”, and the rest is for the listener: can I? could you help?

2

が, more polite, say the notes

口座を開きたいんですが, 見たいのですが. The same unfinished request, one step up.

3

With んだ or んです

開きたいんですけど. It explains your situation, and that is what invites the listener to help.

The notes’ examples, all at a bank: 口座の残高を知りたいんだけど, “I’d like to check my balance”; 銀行口座を解約したいんですけれど, “I’d like to close my account”; 口座からお金を引き出したいのですが, “I’d like to take some money out of my account”, each with an unspoken “please show me how”.

ExampleFeel
〜んだけど開ひらきたいんだけどcasual: friends, family
〜んですけど開ひらきたいんですけどpolite, conversational
〜んですが開ひらきたいんですがmore polite; fine in writing too
〜のですが開ひらきたいのですがmore formal, a little stiff

Beyond the lessonExplain, then stop. The Japan Foundation’s course いろどり teaches the same move: end with 〜んですが…, and you explain your situation and ask for help without spelling out the request; 〜たいんですが… states a wish and asks a favour. Say the ending softly, it adds, with the pitch falling. The んです is doing real work: a Japan Foundation column calls the basic meaning of のだ／んだ “explaining the situation”, with a bank example of its own, 午後銀行へ行きたいんですが、かまいませんか, “I’d like to go to the bank this afternoon. Is that all right?”

How casual? A grammar module from the Tokyo University of Foreign Studies ranks them: けれども, けれど, けど, each more conversational than the last, while が is used in writing as well as speech. The same column finds のです more formal and stiffer than んです. And the trailing-off use is common in questions and requests, the module says, because it leaves the listener to work out the rest: すみません、パソコンが動かないのですけれども, “excuse me, my computer isn’t working…”

すみません、この荷物にもつを送おくりたいんですが。

Excuse me, I’d like to send this parcel.

ちょっと相談そうだんしたいことがあるんだけど。

There’s something I’d like to ask your advice about…

このシャツ、もう少すこし大おおきいサイズが欲ほしいんですけど。

I’d like this shirt in a slightly bigger size…

けど, が and the bank’s keigo

Beyond the lesson: sentences left unfinished, and how the bank talks to you

Beyond the lessonWhat the dictionaries say. At the end of a sentence, けれども avoids saying something flatly and softens it: あすなら行けるんですけれども, “I could go tomorrow, though…”; or it hopes for something while half doubting it: このままお天気が続くといいんですけれども, “I hope this weather holds…”. けど does the same, わたしですけど, “it’s me…”. が can trail off too, when you hesitate to say something outright: こちらのほうがよろしいと思いますが, “I think this one would be better, but…”; that use, the dictionary notes, comes from stopping at が and dropping the rest. けど is the younger form: けれど and けど appeared in the early Edo period with a casual feel, and in speech けど has been the commoner since the Taishō era.

How the bank talks to you. The guide’s お持ちですか is a respectful form, お + ます-stem + です, which the Japan Foundation’s course calls the respectful version of 〜ています, so a polite 持っていますか. The government’s 2007 guide to keigo warns against お持ちしますか here: that’s humble, for your own actions, and the right form for the customer is お持ちになりますか. お越しください uses お越し, a respectful word for coming or going. And ご印鑑 puts the respectful ご on the customer’s seal: お or ご in front of a noun raises its owner, and as a rule お goes on native Japanese words and ご on words from Chinese, like 印鑑.

お嬢さん is a less formal お嬢様, the dictionary says, and is also used to call out to a young woman, which is how Taiga uses it. お嬢様 is someone else’s daughter, or a woman raised in such comfort as to know nothing of life’s hardships.

すみません、道みちに迷まよってしまったんですけど……。

Excuse me, I seem to have got lost…

Seals, ID and receipts

Beyond the lesson: what has changed since 2008

Beyond the lessonWhy a utility receipt? ID without a photo, such as a health insurance card, isn’t enough on its own to open an account, the Japanese Bankers Association explains: you also show another document, or one with your address on it, such as a utility receipt from the last six months, or the bank posts papers to your home. That’s the 公共料金の領収証 in the dialogue.

Two of the cards are gone. The 外国人登録証 that the guide asks Taiga for no longer exists: the Alien Registration Act was abolished on 9 July 2012, and foreign residents now carry a 在留カード (lesson 11). Under the old system even short-stay visitors could register, to get a seal certificate or open a bank account; now stays of three months or less get no card at all. And no new 健康保険証 have been issued since 2 December 2024: the system is now built on the My Number card, the old cards all expired on 1 December 2025, and people without a My Number health card are sent a 資格確認書 instead. The rules banks follow for ID checks dropped health insurance cards too.

印鑑, はんこ, 印章. In everyday speech 印鑑 is the stamp itself, but strictly, one encyclopedia says, the law calls the tool 印章 and keeps 印鑑 for the impression filed in advance with an office or a bank, for checking against. はんこ comes from はんこう; the kanji 判子 are only there for the sound. A 実印 is registered at the city office, one per person, for important deals; any other personal seal is a 認印, which carries the same legal weight. For banks, the Bankers Association calls it the 届出印, needed to open an account and to withdraw money, though some banks now open accounts with ID alone: MUFG announced seal-free accounts at all its branches in 2016, a first for a Japanese bank. And in 2020 the government reviewed every procedure that needed a seal: of 15,611, it dropped the seal from 15,493.

Receipts and 上様. To the dictionaries, 領収書 and 領収証 are the same word. 上様, read うえさま or じょうさま, is written in place of the buyer’s name, and the tax agency still accepts it: shops, restaurants, taxis and similar businesses can issue a simplified invoice without the buyer’s name, and its sample receipt notes that 上様 is fine. A business receipt for ¥50,000 or more needs a ¥200 revenue stamp (up to ¥1 million); below that it’s tax-free, a line that stood at ¥30,000 until March 2014.

8

預金 or 貯金?

Beyond the lesson: the post office, the Bank of Japan, and the order the bills get cut off

Beyond the lessonWas the post office a bank? Yes and no. Japan’s postal business was privatised on 1 October 2007, and its savings arm opened that day as ゆうちょ銀行, Japan Post Bank, a bank, seven months before this lesson; the post offices themselves act as its agents, so in that sense Naomi was right. What it couldn’t yet do in May 2008 was send ordinary transfers to other banks: that came in January 2009. At the end of March 2008 it held ¥181.7 trillion in savings, and economists called it the largest bank in the world by deposits. Today it holds about ¥186 trillion, roughly a fifth of Japan’s household savings.

Why 貯金? Legally its 貯金 are the same as any bank’s 預金, a news glossary explains; it simply kept the old name. The dictionary adds that ゆうちょ銀行, JA Bank and JF Marine Bank all say 貯金, since they were historically savings banks for individuals, while everyday 貯金 is just saving money, 貯金箱 and all. Piggy banks spread in Japan with the postal savings system, set up in 1875, says an encyclopedia. As for transfers, ゆうちょ’s own pages file them under 送金, call a transfer between two ゆうちょ accounts 振替, and keep 振込 for sending money to other banks.

The Bank of Japan takes no deposits from individuals or ordinary companies: its accounts are for the government, other central banks, international bodies and financial institutions, since its job is to keep payments between banks running smoothly. It is sometimes called the bankers’ bank.

Which bill gets cut off first? No official source gives the hosts’ order, but water really does come last. Tokyo Gas may stop the gas, with notice, 50 days after the meter reading; Tohoku Electric can cut the power, with notice, 20 days after its payment date, also about 50 days in; Yokohama’s water department only stops supply after three or four months of arrears. In 2012, after people living in poverty were found dead with their power and gas cut off, the health ministry asked water utilities to avoid automatic cut-offs for unpaid bills.

Quiz

Pick the word or form that fits. Each answer comes with the reason.

1. すみません、駅えきに行いきたいんです＿＿＿。

Excuse me, I’m trying to get to the station…

Say what you want and leave the request unsaid: 〜んですけど.

2. 口座こうざからお金かねを引ひき出だしたいのです＿＿。

I’d like to withdraw some money from my account.

〜のですが: the politer way to leave it unsaid.

3. この靴くつ、もう少すこし小ちいさいサイズが＿＿＿んですけど。

I’d like these shoes in a slightly smaller size…

The plain form goes before んですけど: 欲しいんですけど.

4. ちょっと話はなし＿＿＿んだけど、今いまいい？

There’s something I want to talk about. Is now OK?

ます-stem + たい, then んだけど: 話したいんだけど.

5. 銀行ぎんこうで＿＿＿を開ひらいた。

I opened an account at the bank.

口座を開く: to open an account. 窓口 is a service window.

6. ATMで、お金かねの＿＿＿をした。

I paid some money in at the ATM.

預け入れ: paying in. 引き出し is taking money out.

7. 旅行りょこうの前まえに、口座こうざからお金かねを＿＿＿。

Before the trip, I took money out of my account.

引き出す: to withdraw. 預ける is to deposit; 貯める, to save up.

8. ＿＿＿を見みせてください。

Please show me some ID.

身分証明書: ID. 領収書 is a receipt; 申込書, an application form.

9. ＿＿＿の宛名あてなは、会社名かいしゃめいでお願ねがいします。

Please make the receipt out to the company name.

領収書 (or 領収証): a receipt, made out to an 宛名.

10. 電気でんきやガスなどの＿＿＿は、口座こうざから払はらっている。

I pay my utility bills, like electricity and gas, from my account.

公共料金: utility charges. 手数料 is a fee; 入場料, an admission charge.

11. ここにご＿＿＿をお願ねがいします。

Your seal here, please.

ご印鑑: your seal. ご意見 would be your opinion, Peter’s famous mishearing.

12. 保険証ほけんしょうは＿＿＿ですか。

Do you have your insurance card with you?

お + ます-stem + です: お持ちですか, a respectful 持っていますか.

13. 2番ばんの窓口まどぐちまで＿＿＿ください。

Please come to window 2.

お越しください: please come, the respectful form.

14. ＿＿＿は、おいくつですか。

How old is your daughter?

お嬢さん: someone else’s daughter, politely. 娘 is for your own.
