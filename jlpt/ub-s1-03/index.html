The air is dry tonight — please mind your fires.

It’s seven in the evening, and a fire engine is patrolling the streets with a warning: the wind is strong, the air is dry, take care with anything that could start a fire. Misaki copies the announcement, plays fire engine — and puts out Dad’s cigarette with a bucket of water. The lesson teaches the extra-polite request of announcements and signs, ご協力ください and ご注意ください, and reviews おります, the humble “to be”.

2

The scene

Seven in the evening, at home

Misaki and her father are at home, at around seven in the evening, when a fire engine’s bell sounds outside and an announcement follows. It is the local fire station: today the wind is strong and the air is dry, so please be careful with anything that could start a fire, and cooperate in preventing fires. 火の用心！火の用心！

Misaki repeats it, then turns it into a game: there’s a fire on the balcony, here comes the fire engine, please watch out, it’s turning right — and the “fire” turns out to be Dad, smoking on the balcony, who gets a soaking. Daddy’s drenched, he complains; you mustn’t smoke, she answers, you’ll start a fire. Then he sneezes.

After the dialogue

Jessi assumes Misaki was copying an announcement from the fire station, but Tomoyuki puts it right: it came from a fire engine (消防車からの放送). In autumn and winter the air gets dry and fires increase, so fire engines come round residential areas on patrol. 火の用心 means 火に気をつけてください, “be careful with fire” or “beware of fire”.

Is there anything like it in America? Not really, Jessi says — you see signs like that at campgrounds, and that’s about it. Where Tomoyuki lives, it isn’t a fire engine but members of the neighbourhood association (町内会) who walk round with the children calling 「火の用心」; Jessi has seen it, people clapping wooden sticks together as they go. And for Tomoyuki, hearing 「火の用心」 is a sign the seasons have turned: “ah, autumn is here”.

Key lines

The lesson’s own lines that carry its grammar

Five lines from the lesson’s dialogue, quoted exactly for study. The English is my own rendering.

本日ほんじつは風かぜが強つよく、空気くうきが乾かわいております。

Today the wind is strong and the air is dry. — the announcement

Grammar: 本日, a formal 今日; 強く、 = 強くて、, the formal way to join two clauses (section 7); 乾いております = 乾いています, extra-polite (section 5).

火ひの元もとに注意ちゅういし、防火ぼうかにご協きょう力りょくください。

Please take care with anything that could start a fire, and help us to prevent fires. — the announcement

Grammar: 注意し、 = 注意して、; ご協力ください, the extra-polite request (section 6); に marks what you take care with, and what you cooperate in.

消しょう防ぼう車しゃが通とおります。ご注意ちゅういください。

A fire engine is coming through. Please take care. — Misaki, playing fire engine

Grammar: 通る, “pass, go through”; ご注意ください, the formal “please be careful” of announcements.

コラ、美咲みさき！パパ、びしょびしょになっちゃったよ。

Hey, Misaki! Daddy’s soaked! — the father

Grammar: コラ, a telling-off; びしょびしょ, “soaking wet”; なっちゃった = なってしまった, a result nobody wanted. Japanese parents often call themselves パパ or ママ when talking to their children.

パパ、タバコ吸すっちゃダメ。火事かじになっちゃう。

Daddy, you mustn’t smoke. It’ll start a fire. — Misaki

Grammar: 吸っちゃダメ = 吸ってはだめ, “mustn’t smoke” (lesson 1, section 6); なっちゃう = なってしまう, “it’ll end up”.

5

おります and 火の元

The lesson’s two word notes

おる, おります — a humble “to be”

おる is the humble form of いる, “to be” or “to exist”, and おります is its ます-form. Swap います for おります and a sentence becomes more formal still. The lesson’s examples: 弟がいます becomes 弟がおります, “I have a younger brother”, and 雨が降っています becomes 雨が降っております, “it’s raining”. That second pattern, 〜ております, is the announcement’s 空気が乾いております. Two of my own:

父ちちは今いま、出でかけております。

My father is out at the moment. (On the phone to someone outside the family.)

本日ほんじつは大変たいへん混こみ合あっております。

We are very busy today. (A shop or restaurant, to its customers.)

火の元 — where a fire could start

火 is fire and 元 is the origin or source, so 火の元 is anything that could cause a fire: matches, cigarettes, the kitchen stove, heaters. In the dialogue, the announcement asks you to take care with them: 火の元に注意し.

台だい所どころのコンロは、一番いちばん気きをつけたい火ひの元もとだ。

The kitchen hob is the source of fire to watch most closely.

Watch forTwo false friends from English. In Japanese a ストーブ is a room heater, gas or kerosene; the thing you cook on is a コンロ, or a ガスコンロ. So the lesson’s “kitchen stoves or heaters” are コンロ and ストーブ.

6

ご〜ください: the formal request

The lesson’s grammar point

The lesson’s focus is an extra-formal request: the honorific prefix ご, then a noun that names an action, then ください. It asks someone to do that action. Its two examples:

する-verbTake off する, add ごAdd くださいMeaning
注意するご注意ご注意くださいplease be careful; please watch out
協力するご協力ご協力くださいplease cooperate; we ask for your kind cooperation

注意してください means “please be careful” too, but when speaking very formally ご注意ください is better — which is why you hear it in stations, shops and announcements.

The lesson then takes the announcement’s sentence apart: 火の元 “sources of fire” · に · 注意し “take care, and” · 防火 “fire prevention” · に · ご協力ください “please cooperate”. And at the very end it points out the hardest part of the announcement: 本日は風が強く means 今日は風が強くて, “today the wind is strong, and…”, and 空気が乾いております is 空気 “air” plus 乾く “to dry”: “the air is dry”.

More of the same pattern, in my own examples:

ドアが閉しまります。ご注意ちゅういください。

The doors are closing. Please take care. (On a train.)

内容ないようをご確認かくにんください。

Please check the details.

駐ちゅう車しゃ場じょうは裏うらの入口いりぐちをご利用りようください。

For the car park, please use the entrance at the back.

Drill — make it formal

Turn each into a ご〜ください request: (a) 連絡してください (b) 確認してください (c) 乗車してください (d) 遠慮してください.

Answers

(a) ご連絡ください, “please get in touch”. (b) ご確認ください, “please check”. (c) ご乗車ください, “please board”. (d) ご遠慮ください, “please refrain” — the one on no-smoking signs.

お or ご?

Beyond the lesson: the prefix rule, formal joins, and an おります trap

Beyond the lessonお or ご? The prefix follows the word’s origin. Words built from Chinese-derived readings take ご: ご注意、ご協力、ご確認、ご利用、ご遠慮、ご連絡. Native Japanese words take お: お名前、お気持ち. Native verbs make their formal request with お + the verb’s stem + ください: 待つ → お待ちください, 入る → お入りください, 気をつける → お気をつけください. A few everyday Chinese-derived words take お anyway, and are worth learning as exceptions: お茶、お菓子、お時間、お食事、お電話 — and a couple of native ones take ご, like ごゆっくり, “take your time” (敬語の指針, 2007; デジタル大辞泉).

少しょう々しょうお待まちください。

Please wait a moment.

店内てんないでの喫煙きつえんはご遠慮えんりょください。

Please refrain from smoking in the shop. (The sign Misaki would approve of.)

Formal joins. The announcement’s 風が強く、 and 注意し、 are the formal way to join two clauses. Instead of the て-form, a verb uses its stem (注意して → 注意し) and an い-adjective its く-form (強くて → 強く). You hear it in announcements and on the news, and read it everywhere in written Japanese:

明日あしたは雨あめが降ふり、風かぜも強つよくなるでしょう。

Tomorrow it will rain, and the wind will pick up too.

Watch forおります is a special kind of humble word. Japan’s official keigo guidelines, the 敬語の指針 (2007), group おる with 参る, 申す and いたす as 謙譲語Ⅱ, or 丁重語: “courteous” words that show respect to the listener rather than raising or lowering anyone in the sentence. That is why an announcement can use them about things — the guidelines’ own example is バスが参りました, “the bus has arrived” — and why 空気が乾いております works. But they must never go on the person you’re respecting: 先生はいらっしゃいますか, not 先生はおりますか. And when someone outside calls, you use it even for your own colleague or parent: 田中はおりません, “Tanaka isn’t in.”

8

Fire safety in Japan

Beyond the lesson: the patrols, the season, and what to say if it’s real

Beyond the lessonThe patrols. The walks Tomoyuki describes are called 夜回り, “night rounds”: neighbourhood groups take turns walking the streets on winter evenings, clapping a pair of wooden blocks — 拍子木, ひょうしぎ — as they go (Nipponica). Many neighbourhoods do it with their children; in Tokyo’s Kudan, for one, children have joined the patrol since 1986 (Tokyo Metropolitan Government). A common call adds a reason — 「火の用心、マッチ一本火事の元」, “beware of fire: one match can start a fire” — though nobody seems to know who first said it.

The season. Fires in Japan peak from winter into early spring, when the air is driest: in 2025 the busiest months were February, March and January. The national fire-prevention weeks match. Spring’s runs from 1 to 7 March, and autumn’s from 9 to 15 November, starting on 119番の日, “119 Day” (FDMA, 2026). And Misaki has a point about the cigarette. In both 2024 and 2025, tobacco was the most common single cause of fires in Japan, ahead of bonfires, electrical appliances and kitchen hobs — though arson and suspected arson, counted separately, add up to more (FDMA statistics).

If it’s real: 119. In Japan, fire and ambulance share one number, 119; the police are 110. The operator answers 「119番、消防署です。火事ですか、救急ですか。」 — fire, or ambulance? — so the first thing to say is Misaki’s line, 「火事です」. Then come the questions: where (the address, the floor, a landmark nearby), what is burning, whether anyone is trapped, and your name and phone number (FDMA). If smoke or flames are coming your way, get out first and call from somewhere safe.

火事かじです。住じゅう所しょは青山あおやま一丁目いっちょうめです。台だい所どころが燃もえています。

It’s a fire. The address is Aoyama 1-chōme. The kitchen is on fire.

Quiz

Pick the word that fits. Each answer comes with the reason.

1. 本日ほんじつは空気くうきが乾燥かんそうして＿＿＿。

The air is dry today.

For something neutral like the weather, formal announcements use おります. いらっしゃいます is for people you respect, and まいります means “go” or “come”.

2. 足元あしもとに＿＿＿ください。

Please watch your step.

注意 is a Sino-Japanese word, so it takes ご, and the pattern is ご + noun + ください, with no して.

3. 募金ぼきんへの＿＿＿をお願ねがいします。

We ask for your support for the fundraising.

協力 takes ご; ご協力をお願いします is another formal request.

4. 少しょう々しょうお＿＿＿ください。

Please wait a moment.

Native Japanese verbs make the formal request with お + the verb’s stem + ください: 待つ → お待ちください.

5. 店内てんないでのおたばこは＿＿＿ください。

Please refrain from smoking in the shop.

ご遠慮ください, “please refrain”, is how a sign says “don’t” politely.

6. （電話でんわで）父ちちはただ今いま、出でかけて＿＿＿。

(On the phone) My father is out at the moment.

Talking about your own family to someone outside it, you lower your own side: おります.

7. 先生せんせいは研けん究きゅう室しつに＿＿＿か。

Is the professor in the office?

For someone you respect, use the honorific いらっしゃる. おります is for your own side, or for neutral things.

8. 雨あめで、びしょびしょに＿＿＿。

I got soaked in the rain.

なっちゃった = なってしまった: it happened, and nobody wanted it to.

9. 台だい所どころの＿＿＿は、火ひの元もとになりやすい。

The kitchen ___ can easily start a fire.

コンロ is the hob you cook on; ストーブ is a room heater.

10. 火事かじのときに電話でんわする番号ばんごうは？

Which number do you call for a fire?

119 is for fire and ambulance in Japan; 110 is the police; 911 is the US number.
