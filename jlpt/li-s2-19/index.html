Roppongi? Speaking of Roppongi…

Part 2 of “Going on a Date”. Yuuko and Takao talk hobbies over coffee, see a samurai film at the weekend, agree it was… 微妙, and head to Roppongi for sake. The grammar point is なら taking up a topic someone has just raised, “speaking of…”: 六本木なら、日本酒のおいしい店を知ってるよ. Plus と言えば, 〜たり〜たり and how Japan spends its free time, the film 壬生義士伝 and the Shinsengumi, and 日本酒.

Scene

The café, an hour later. The coffee is long gone; the Murakami talk isn’t.

Yuuko

えっ、全部ぜんぶ読よんだの？そんなに村上春樹むらかみはるきが好すきなんだ。すごいね！

What, you’ve read them all? You really do love Murakami. Impressive!

Takao

ゆうこちゃんは、他ほかにどんな趣味しゅみがあるの？

What other hobbies have you got, Yuuko?

Yuuko

うーん、ダーツをしたり、お酒さけを飲のんだり……。あ、映画えいがを見みるのも好すきかな。

Hmm, darts, drinking… Oh, and I like watching films, I suppose.

Takao

ダーツはやったことないなあ。でも、酒さけを飲のんだり映画えいがを見みたりするのは、俺おれも好すき。

I’ve never played darts. But drinking and films, I like those too.

Takao

じゃあ、今度こんどの週しゅう末まつ、暇ひまだったら、一緒いっしょに映画えいがでも見みに行いかない？

So, if you’re free this weekend, fancy going to a film or something together?

Yuuko

うん、行いく行いく！

Yes, I’m in!

Scene

The weekend, outside the cinema. They have just seen 『壬生義士伝』.

Yuuko

『壬生義士伝みぶぎしでん』、どうだった？面白おもしろかった？

So, how was When the Last Sword Is Drawn? Did you enjoy it?

Takao

え？う、うん……まあまあかな。歴史れきしものって、ちょっと難むずかしいよね。

Huh? Oh, er, yeah… it was OK. Period films are a bit hard going, aren’t they?

Yuuko

正しょう直じき、微妙びみょうだったよね……。

Honestly, it was a bit meh, wasn’t it…

Takao

いやいや、そんなことないって！面白おもしろかったよ！

No, no, not at all! It was good!

Yuuko

ふーん。で、この後あとどうする？六本木ろっぽんぎでも行いく？

Hmm. So, what shall we do now? Roppongi or something?

Takao

六本木ろっぽんぎなら、日本酒にほんしゅのおいしい店みせを知しってるんだ。行いってみない？

Speaking of Roppongi, I know a place with great sake. Want to try it?

Yuuko

いいね！行いこう行いこう！

Sounds great! Let’s go!

Takao

……あ、でも、店みせでは時代劇じだいげきの話はなしは禁止きんしね。

…Oh, but no samurai-film talk in the bar, OK?

Yuuko

了りょう解かい。村上春樹むらかみはるきの話はなしなら、いくらでも聞きくよ。

Deal. But Murakami, I’ll listen to all night.

The scene

Naomi, Yuichi and Peter, before and after the dialogue

The second “Going on a Date” lesson, デートのレッスンの2回目. Again the story is told from the woman’s side, Peter says, with a bonus track from the man’s side, so you can compare male and female speech, a very important part of Japanese. The grammar point is なら, “speaking of”. Naomi recaps: last time, a woman called out to a man and finished with もしよかったら、喫茶店でコーヒーを飲みながら、村上春樹の作品の話でもしませんか？ Now the two seem to be in the café. And their Japanese? Casual, Naomi guesses. Speaking casually to a stranger would be a bit much, Peter says, and quick (早いですね); but this is probably an hour later, the hosts reckon, and they got to know each other walking to the café. They’re already good friends: もう仲良しですね.

After the dialogue

Time jumps, Yuichi notes: first the café, then Takao asks Yuuko to a film, so this time it’s the man doing the inviting. Is a film where nobody has to talk the reason men invite women to the cinema? The hosts joke about it, and about the three of them going to a movie together. The two saw the film the weekend after they met, and afterwards they talk over their impressions, 感想; mostly they just say 微妙, so it seems it was boring. 壬生義士伝 really exists, the hosts point out, written by the well-known novelist 浅田次郎. Takao still says 面白かった: guys like history, Peter suggests. Does Yuichi? Yes. And Peter, asked the same, wants to know how far back history goes.

Phrases worth taking from the chat:

  • 仲良し — good friends, close: もう仲良しですね.
  • 誘う — to invite, ask out: 映画に誘う, to ask someone to a film.
  • 感想 — your impressions, what you thought of something.
  • 勉強になりました — “I learned something”, back from lesson 15.

Key lines

Listing hobbies, a polite verdict on the film, and changing the subject

The lesson’s dialogue is eleven lines long, so these three are quoted exactly; section 1 covers the rest in my retelling. The English is my own rendering.

そうねぇ、ダーツをしたり、お酒さけを飲のんだり、あ、あと映画えいがを見みるのも好すきかな。

Let me see… I play darts, I go for drinks, oh, and I like watching films too, I suppose. — Yuuko

Grammar: そうねぇ, “let me see”; 〜たり〜たり, naming a few things you do among others (section 6); あと, “and also”; 映画を見るの, “watching films”, with の turning the verb into a noun; かな, “I suppose”, softening it.

う、、、うん、、、まぁまぁね。でも、歴史れきしって難むずかしいよね。

Uh… yeah… it was all right. But history’s hard going, isn’t it? — Takao

Grammar: まぁまぁ, so-so; 歴史って, with って raising a topic casually, “history, I mean”; 難しいよね, “hard, don’t you think?”, inviting agreement.

そんなことより、この後あとどうする？六本木ろっぽんぎに日本酒にほんしゅのおいしいお店みせを知しってるんだけど、行いかない？

Never mind that, what shall we do now? I know a place in Roppongi with great sake. Fancy it? — Takao

Grammar: そんなことより, “never mind that”, changing an awkward subject; この後, after this; 日本酒のおいしいお店, “a place where the sake is good”, with の standing in for が inside a description; 知ってるんだけど, “I know a place, so…”, the けど leading into a suggestion; 行かない？, a casual invitation.

Vocabulary

The hosts’ words, in their order

The hosts’ notes first, then a card for each word, plus more from the dialogue, each with an example of my own.

  • しゅみ (趣味) — a hobby. Yuichi likes basketball, and Naomi says you can also ask 趣味は何ですか？ The dialogue’s 他に趣味はありますか？ means “any other hobbies?”: 他に is “other”. So, besides basketball? ピーターさんと話すことです, says Yuichi, talking with Peter; not much of a hobby, says Peter. 趣味がありますか？ or ご趣味は何ですか？ can sound a bit uncool and old-fashioned, the hosts admit, though they’re still handy when you meet people. In English it’s more “what do you like to do?”; textbooks always translate it as “what’s your hobby?”, which would sound odd said to a friend, Peter says. In Japanese the phrases are common in conversation, and the very polite ご趣味は？ sounds like a blind date or something, Peter adds.
  • つり (釣り) — fishing, and it has two meanings, the hosts point out: fishing, 魚釣り, and the change you get back, お釣り, from 釣り銭, where 銭 means money, coins. 釣りといえば, speaking of fishing, Naomi’s friend’s family runs a fishing spot in Ōtsuki, in Yamanashi Prefecture, where you can barbecue too; they won’t give you a Japanese lesson, though. River fishing is hard, the hosts agree; one of them has fished for crayfish, ザリガニ, and Yuichi is amazed to hear they’re eaten in New Orleans.
  • びみょう (微妙) — when something is complicated and hard to comment on, Peter explains, you can say 微妙, “hmm”. It usually has a negative feel, like “no comment”, or “I’d rather not talk about it”.
  • ろっぽんぎ (六本木) — Roppongi: long ago there were six trees there, the hosts say, hence the name. Is that true? Who knows: 微妙ですね, the hosts agree.

The lesson notes add 村上春樹, 週末, the film’s title 壬生義士伝, 歴史 and 日本酒. The cards below cover them all, with more words from the dialogue.

Beyond the lesson微妙, then and now. The dictionary’s older senses are “subtle” and “too close to call”: セーフかアウトか微妙な判定, a call that could go either way. Its newest, marked as slang and sometimes written ビミョー, is a roundabout way to sound negative, or to avoid committing yourself: 「テストできた？」「微妙」, “Did the test go well?” “Hmm.” In 文化庁’s language survey of early 2005, 57.8% of people said they use 微妙 when they can’t decide whether something is good or bad, including 95.6% of men and 97.4% of women aged 16 to 19. Ten years later the figure was 66.2%, and 97.0% among people in their twenties.

Six trees, or six names? Minato City, where Roppongi is, says the name may come from six pine trees, or from the mansions of six daimyo families with tree names: 上杉, 朽木, 高木, 青木, 片桐 and 一柳. But it isn’t certain, the city says. The hosts’ 微妙ですね was about right.

The notes’ Murakami figure. The lesson notes give ノルウェイの森 4.5 million copies, but the count was already higher. Published by Kodansha in two volumes in September 1987, it sold 2.7 million in its first year, one encyclopedia says; in July 2008 the Yomiuri reported 8.78 million copies printed in Japan, and in 2009 Kodansha put the total past 10 million. Kodansha’s own phrase for the stir it caused is 村上春樹現象, “the Murakami phenomenon”.

5

〜なら, “speaking of”

The grammar point: taking up a topic that has just come up

Today’s grammar point, Peter explains, is なら, a particle that makes a topic. The usual topic marker is は, but if you want to stress the topic a little more, you can use なら, roughly “speaking of”. In the version told from the man’s side, 六本木でも行きませんか？, “shall we go to Roppongi?”, gets the answer 六本木なら、日本酒のおいしいお店を知っているんですけど, “speaking of Roppongi, I know a place with lovely sake.” Yuichi’s reading of it gets teased for sounding as if Yuichi doesn’t want to go at all. Another example: これから新宿に行くんだ, “I’m off to Shinjuku now”; 新宿ならタクシーで行けばいいよ, “if it’s Shinjuku, take a taxi.” It’s often used when giving advice, Naomi says. And two more from the hosts: 日本語なら、Naomi先生に習えばいいよ, and 日本語なら、Japanesepod101.comを聞くといいよ.

1

Noun + なら takes up a topic

六本木なら… The topic has just come up, usually from the other person: “if it’s Roppongi…”, “speaking of Roppongi…”.

2

Then say something about it

新宿なら、タクシーで行けばいいよ. Advice, a recommendation, something you know: “for that, try this”.

3

と言えば, when the talk moves on

六本木と言えば… works too. But only と言えば can lead somewhere new.

The lesson notes say なら is for when you want to say more about a topic that has just been raised, as Take, the man in that version, does with 六本木なら. と言えば, “speaking of”, works the same way, but it’s also used when the topic shifts a little from what the other person said: told 昨日、洋介に会ったよ, you can answer 洋介と言えば、彼は本当にサッカー上手いよね, “speaking of Yosuke, isn’t Yosuke good at football”, but not 洋介なら. This is lesson 15’s なら after a noun: building on what you’ve just heard.

ExampleWhat it does
は六本木ろっぽんぎは遠とおいthe plain topic: Roppongi is far
なら六本木ろっぽんぎなら、いい店みせがあるtaking up what was said: if it’s Roppongi you mean…
と言えば六本木ろっぽんぎと言いえば、…speaking of Roppongi, and the talk can move on

Beyond the lessonWhat the dictionary says. After a noun, なら takes something up as the topic, “as for…, speaking of…”: 母なら間もなく帰ると思います, “Mum? Should be back any minute.” The linguist 野田尚史 spells out what sets it apart from は: なら, and its cousins だったら and でしたら, add the sense that you’re taking up, as your topic, something from the other person’s words: 「プロジェクタ，ないかな？」「プロジェクタだったら，そこにあるよ。」, “Isn’t there a projector?” “A projector? There’s one over there.”

And と言えば. The dictionary’s と言えば takes up something from the conversation and goes on to something related, “often to take the talk in another direction”: 事件と言えば昨日の新聞は読みましたか, “speaking of the incident, did you read yesterday’s paper?” That’s the lesson notes’ point about 洋介と言えば. It can also name the best-known example of something: 酒と言えば灘, “when it comes to sake, it’s Nada”, Kobe’s brewing district.

って, the casual topic. Takao’s 歴史って難しいよね uses って, which the dictionary describes as taking something up as the topic, “…は, …というのは”: あなたって親切な人ね, “you’re such a kind person.” Noda adds that it introduces a description of what the topic is like.

「京都きょうとに行いきたいな。」「京都きょうとなら、秋あきがいちばんきれいだよ。」

“I’d love to go to Kyoto.” “If it’s Kyoto, autumn is the most beautiful time.”

「パソコンが壊こわれちゃった。」「パソコンなら、兄あにが詳くわしいよ。」

“My computer’s broken.” “For computers, my brother’s the one to ask.”

「最近さいきん、ギターを始はじめたんだ。」「ギターと言いえば、田中たなかさんがバンドをやってるらしいよ。」

“I’ve just taken up the guitar.” “Speaking of guitars, I hear Tanaka-san is in a band.”

〜たり and free time

Beyond the lesson: listing what you do, and what Japan does with its spare time

Beyond the lesson〜たり〜たり. A grammar module from the Tokyo University of Foreign Studies teaches it as Vたり、Vたりします, for naming a few actions as examples, with a hint that there are others. The dictionary adds that it lists actions or states side by side, 泣いたり笑ったりする, “laugh and cry by turns”, and can pick out one example to suggest others: 車にひかれたりしたらたいへんだ, “it’d be awful if you got hit by a car or something.”

One たり or two? In writing, newspapers repeat it: the Japan Newspaper Association’s usage guide, quoted by the Mainichi’s copy desk, says a list should run …たり…たり, not 飲んだり歌って, though one example with たりして is fine. Speech is looser, and the dictionary notes that dropping the second part goes back to the late Edo period. Yuuko’s key line in section 3 gives two たり and then moves on without a する; Takao’s reply in the lesson uses only one. My retelling gives Takao the full 酒を飲んだり映画を見たりするのは.

How Japan spends its free time. In the government’s 2021 survey of time use, 86.3% of people aged 10 and over had taken part in some hobby or pastime during the year. Top was listening to music on CDs, phones and so on (53.5%), then watching films somewhere other than a cinema (52.7%), and games on phones or consoles (42.9%). Films at the cinema fell to 29.8%, from 39.6% five years before, and karaoke to 13.5%, from 30.7%: the survey year took in two COVID-19 states of emergency. Fishing counts as a sport there, at 7.8%.

And darts. Electronic soft-tip boards, developed in the US in 1982, reached Japan in 1999, according to the darts-machine maker DARTSLIVE, whose history page also recalls that the Japan Darts Association began in Roppongi in 1970.

休やすみの日ひは、映画えいがを見みたり、本ほんを読よんだりしています。

On my days off I watch films, read, that kind of thing.

古ふるい電気でんきが、ついたり消きえたりしている。

The old light keeps flickering on and off.

7

壬生義士伝

Beyond the lesson: the novel, the film, and the Shinsengumi

Beyond the lessonThe novel. 壬生義士伝 is a historical novel by 浅田次郎, serialized from 1998 to 2000 and published as a book by Bungeishunjū in April 2000; it won the 13th Shibata Renzaburō Prize that year. Its hero is 吉村貫一郎, a samurai of the Nanbu domain, also called the Morioka domain, who leaves it out of poverty and joins the Shinsengumi, the band known as 壬生浪.

The film. Shochiku released the film on 18 January 2003, directed by 滝田洋二郎, with 中井貴一 as Yoshimura. At the 27th Japan Academy Film Prize in 2004 it won Best Picture, with Best Actor for 中井貴一 and Best Supporting Actor for 佐藤浩市. Takao’s 面白かった has the Academy on its side, whatever Yuuko thought.

Was Yoshimura real? The name appears in Shinsengumi records, so Yoshimura did exist, says an answer by Iwate’s prefectural library in the National Diet Library’s reference database. But the Morioka domain’s own records have no Yoshimura Kan’ichirō, one theory identifies Yoshimura with a Morioka samurai of another name, and 浅田次郎 said the Nanbu-deserter detail couldn’t be relied on.

壬生 and the Shinsengumi. 壬生 is a district of Kyoto, home to the temple 壬生寺 and the site of the Shinsengumi’s quarters. The group was formed there in 1863 as the 壬生浪士組 and took the name 新選組 after its part in the coup of the 18th day of the 8th month that year; Kyoto feared them as the Mibu rōnin. And 義士, in the title, is a man who firmly keeps to what is right: a man of principle.

8

日本酒

Beyond the lesson: 酒, 日本酒 and 清酒, a long decline, and a wedding toast

Beyond the lesson酒, 日本酒, 清酒. 酒 is any drink with alcohol in it, and also, in particular, sake; the dictionary adds that “sake” works in English too. Takao’s 酒を飲んだり is the general sense. 日本酒 is alcohol made by Japan’s traditional brewing method, especially 清酒, the clear kind, and it’s 清酒 that the liquor tax law defines: under 22% alcohol, made from rice, rice kōji and water, fermented and then filtered. Since 25 December 2015 “日本酒” has also been a protected geographical indication: only 清酒 made in Japan, from Japanese rice alone, may use the name.

A long decline. Taxable volumes of 清酒 peaked in fiscal 1973 at 1.77 million kilolitres; by fiscal 2024 they were 0.38 million, under 30% of the peak, says the National Tax Agency. Not every kind is falling, though: ordinary sake is, while 純米酒 and 純米吟醸酒 are growing.

The wedding toast. The notes’ wedding sentence describes 三々九度: at a Japanese-style wedding, bride and groom pass a set of three cups, three times each, nine in all, the dictionary says, and today it’s done mostly at Shinto weddings, sealing the marriage bond. The sake is the 御神酒 offered at the altar, basically 日本酒, says the Association of Shinto Shrines. The Shinto wedding itself is younger than it looks: the Association traces it to the wedding of the Crown Prince, later Emperor Taishō, on 10 May 1900, and says the first such wedding for ordinary people was held the following year at 日比谷大神宮, today’s 東京大神宮.

この店みせには、全国ぜんこくの日本酒にほんしゅが百ひゃく種しゅ類るい以上いじょうある。

This bar has more than a hundred kinds of sake from all over Japan.

2. 「昨日きのう、田中たなかさんに会あったよ。」「田中たなかさん＿＿＿、来月らいげつ結婚けっこんするらしいね。」

“I saw Tanaka-san yesterday.” “Speaking of Tanaka-san, I hear they’re getting married next month.”

The talk moves on from the meeting to other news: と言えば. The lesson notes mark なら wrong in a shift like this.

3. 「ラーメンが食たべたいな。」「ラーメン＿＿＿、駅えきの近ちかくの店みせがおいしいよ。」

“I fancy some ramen.” “If it’s ramen you want, the place near the station is good.”

Taking up what the other person wants, with advice: なら.

4. 休やすみの日ひは、本ほんを読よん＿＿＿、音楽おんがくを聞きいたりしている。

On my days off I read, listen to music and so on.

たり follows the た-form, and 読んだ gives 読んだり.

5. 週しゅう末まつは、掃除そうじをしたり、買かい物ものに行いっ＿＿＿します。

At weekends I do things like cleaning and going shopping.

〜たり〜たりする: the second たり comes before する.

6. 「＿＿＿は何なんですか。」「読書どくしょです。」

“What are your hobbies?” “Reading.”

趣味: a hobby. 興味 is an interest, and 意味 a meaning.

7. 「あの映画えいが、どうだった？」「うーん、ちょっと＿＿＿だった。」

“How was that film?” “Hmm, a bit meh.”

微妙: in casual talk, so-so or not great.

8. 「テスト、どうだった？」「＿＿＿かな。」

“How was the test?” “So-so, I guess.”

まあまあ: so-so. まだまだ is “still a long way to go”, and そろそろ “soon, about time”.

9. ＿＿＿何なにか質問しつもんはありますか。

Are there any other questions?

他に: else, besides.

10. 今度こんどの週しゅう末まつ、＿＿＿？

Are you free this weekend?

暇: free time, free. 忙しい is the opposite, busy.

11. ＿＿＿、お腹なかすかない？

Never mind that, aren’t you hungry?

そんなことより: dropping the subject for something that matters more.

12. お米こめから作つくる＿＿＿は、冷ひやしても温あたためてもおいしい。

Sake, made from rice, is good chilled or warmed.

日本酒 is made from rice.

13. よかったら、＿＿＿映画えいがでも見みに行いかない？

If you like, shall we go and see a film or something together?

一緒に: together. 一人で is “alone”, 別々に “separately”.

14. 歴史れきし＿＿＿、難むずかしいよね。

History’s hard, isn’t it?

って: a casual way to raise a topic, as in Takao’s 歴史って難しいよね.
