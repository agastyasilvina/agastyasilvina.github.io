Don’t tell a soul. Pinky swear.

The last part of Tom and Junko, and the big reveal. Junko came to America to study English (or to meet Tom Cruise, just kidding), and Tom hides behind an enormous mask so as not to be recognised, because Tom, it turns out, is a famous actor. The grammar point is 〜ために, “in order to”, and its answer form 〜ためです: 英語を勉強するためです. Plus ために or ように, the pinky-swear chant, and why so many people in Japan wear masks.

The dialogue

The whole scene, retold for review

The lesson’s dialogue, retold in my own wording so you can review it in one place. It keeps the same scene, characters, words and grammar, and finishes the story from my retelling of lesson 12. Every line is rephrased except the chant, which is the traditional one, and the last line is new. The lesson’s exact key lines are in section 3. Scene

The same café. Tom is wearing an enormous mask.

Tom

そういえば、じゅんこさんは何なんのためにアメリカに来きたんですか？

By the way, Junko, what did you come to America for?

Junko

ハリウッドスターに会あうためです。……なんちゃって。本当ほんとうは、英語えいごの勉べん強きょうのためです。

To meet a Hollywood star. …Just kidding. Really, it’s to study English.

Junko

トムさんこそ、どうしてそんなに大おおきいマスクをしているんですか？

What about you, Tom? Why are you wearing such a big mask?

Tom

実じつは僕ぼく、ちょっと有名ゆうめいな俳優はいゆうなんです。顔かおがばれないように、外そとではいつもマスクをしているんです。

Actually, I’m a rather famous actor. I always wear a mask when I’m out, so that nobody recognises my face.

Junko

えっ、本当ほんとうですか？じゃあ、ちょっとだけマスクを外はずしてみてください！

What, really? Then take your mask off, just for a second!

Scene

Tom lowers the mask.

Junko

う、うそ……トム・クルーズ！？本物ほんものですか！？

N-no way… Tom Cruise!? The real one!?

Tom

しーっ！声こえが大おおきいですよ。このことは、誰だれにも内緒ないしょにしてくださいね。

Shh! You’re too loud. Please keep this a secret from everyone.

Tom

絶対ぜったいにばらさないように、指切ゆびきりしましょう。

So you never let it out, let’s pinky swear.

Both

指切ゆびきりげんまん、うそついたら針はり千本せんぼん飲のます、指切ゆびきった！

Pinky swear: if you lie, you’ll swallow a thousand needles. Sealed!

Junko, thinking

（……しまった。みさに、知しらない番号ばんごうの電話でんわには出でないように言いっちゃった……。）

(…Oh no. I told Misa not to answer calls from numbers she doesn’t know…)

2

The scene

Yuichi, Naomi and Peter, before and after the dialogue

Part five, and the last, Naomi says: Tom and Junko’s story ends today (今日で終わりです). The grammar point is ために, “so that”, “in order to”, for a purpose. It’s Tom and Junko again, as ever (相変わらずですね), Peter says, and Ibaraki dialect, Naomi adds: いばらぎ, no, いばらき. That was on purpose, surely, Peter says (わざとでしょ？), and Yuichi’s own version lands somewhere between き and ぎ; Naomi is making complete fools of them, Peter complains, which Naomi denies. (Lesson 9 has the story of the name.) Yuichi recaps: Tom asked for Junko’s number and got a fake one; last time Tom asked Junko to introduce a friend, who didn’t pick up. But today, Yuichi promises, we find out who strange Tom really is: Tom’s 本当の姿. Strange people are multiplying, Peter remarks, and Naomi has a proverb for it: 類は友を呼ぶ, birds of a feather flock together. Peter and Yuichi, for example, Naomi suggests: “not me”. And Tom, Peter adds. Yuichi is shocked; Peter, speechless.

After the dialogue

Is that the end? It seems so, says Yuichi, and Peter hopes so; what Peter likes about this one is that they can focus on the Japanese. First, 指切りげんまん, and the studio has a singer: Yuichi sings it (任せて下さい, “leave it to me”). Good or bad doesn’t come into it, says Yuichi; so-so, says Peter. Naomi takes it apart. 指 is finger and 切る is cut, so 指切り is cutting the finger, the little finger, 小指; げんまん is ten thousand punches; うそ is a lie, つく tell, たら if; 針 is a needle, 千本 a thousand of them, and 飲ます a short form of 飲ませる, make you swallow. Line by line: I’ll cut off your finger and punch you ten thousand times, and if you lie to me, I’ll make you swallow a thousand needles. A kids’ game, of course, Peter says, like the English “cross my heart and hope to die, stick a needle in my eye”: a pinky swear. Children do it; adults, Peter says, sign contracts.

It’s said to have started in the 18th century, Naomi says, with courtesans: to show a lover they meant “I’ll love you forever”, they cut off a finger. That’s a Japanese story, not a Western one, and the reason, Naomi says, some yakuza still cut off fingers. So is Tom going to lose a pinky? Naomi thinks Junko would be the one cutting; old-fashioned thinking, Peter teases, it’s the 21st century. It’s background knowledge, Naomi concludes; people don’t usually think about what the words mean. Then Yuichi asks Peter about なんちゃって: “just kidding”. Women don’t use it, do they? They do, says Naomi: なーんちゃって. Isn’t it a bit old, Peter asks? Maybe a little, Naomi admits, but people do say it, and Yuichi thinks it’s cute. (Section 7 checks the chant’s history.)

Phrases worth taking from the chat:

  • 今日で終わりです — “it ends today”: で marks the point where something finishes.
  • 相変わらずですね — “same as ever”: 相変わらず, “as always, still”.
  • 本当の姿 — “someone’s true self, what they’re really like”: 姿, a figure or form.
  • 類は友を呼ぶ — “birds of a feather flock together”: like attracts like, a saying traced back to the Chinese classic 易経, the Book of Changes.
  • 任せて下さい — “leave it to me”: 任せる, to leave something in someone’s hands.
  • 勉強になりました — “that was educational”, a stock way to thank someone for teaching you something. Peter’s full version adds 何の勉強かわかりませんが, “though what it taught, who knows”.
  • 頭古いですね — “what old-fashioned thinking”: Peter’s tease, 頭が古い.

Key lines

Junko’s answer, Tom’s confession, and the promise

The lesson’s dialogue is nine lines long, so these three are quoted exactly; section 1 covers the rest in my retelling. The English is my own rendering.

トムクルーズに会あうためです。なんちゃって。英語えいごを勉べん強きょうするためです。

To meet Tom Cruise. Just kidding. It’s to study English. — Junko

Grammar: 会うためです and 勉強するためです, a purpose given as the answer to “why?” (section 5); なんちゃって, “just kidding”, tacked on after the joke.

ぼく、実じつは有名ゆうめいな俳優はいゆうなんです。ばれないようにいつもこうしてるんです。

Actually, I’m a famous actor. I always do this so that nobody finds out. — Tom

Grammar: 実は, “to tell the truth”; 〜なんです, explaining; ばれないように, “so as not to be found out”, with ように, not ために, because being found out isn’t something Tom chooses (section 5); こうしてる, “I do it like this”, short for こうしている.

誰だれにも言いわないように、指切ゆびきりげんまんしよう。

So you don’t tell anyone, let’s pinky swear. — Tom

Grammar: 誰にも言わない, “not tell anyone”, the 誰も family from lesson 12 with に squeezed in; 〜ように, “so that”; しよう, the plain “let’s”.

Vocabulary

The lesson’s word list, in its order

The hosts’ notes first, then a card for each word, plus a few more from the dialogue and the chat, each with an example of my own.

  • マスク — a mask. マスクをする is to wear one; you don’t say マスクを着る, Naomi warns. Naomi’s example: かぜをひいたので、マスクをした, “I caught a cold, so I wore a mask.” Peter will never forget the masks everywhere on arriving in Japan for the first time, and only slowly worked out why people wore them. First, so as not to pass your cold on to others (うつさないように). Second, Yuichi says, hay fever, 花粉症: Japan has a lot of cedar pollen, スギ花粉, especially in spring, and it’s a big, big problem, Naomi agrees. Naomi’s own reason: not to show a runny nose (鼻水を見せないため). Some people wear one so they don’t catch colds (かぜをひかないように); celebrities, to hide their faces; and robbers, Peter adds, before a robbery, 強盗. What kind of world are we living in, Naomi wonders.
  • ばれる — to leak out, to be exposed: 私のうそがばれた, “my lie was found out”; 秘密がばれた, “the secret came out”. It’s intransitive; its transitive partner is ばらす, to let out, as in Peter’s Naomi先生の秘密をばらしました. Naomi claims to have no secrets. Not any more, says Peter.
  • じつは (実は) — in fact, to tell the truth: Naomi’s 実は私子供がふたりいるんです, “the truth is, I have two kids”, which isn’t true. Yuichi thought it was a secret coming out (ばれてしまいましたよ); stop it, says Naomi, it sounds real.
  • はいゆう (俳優) — actor, performer. Doesn’t 俳優 only mean men, Yuichi asks? An actress is usually 女優, Naomi says, but a check in the dictionary turned up 女優 defined as a female 俳優, so 俳優 can be either. It’s said the word 女優 appeared because most actors were men; think of kabuki, nearly all male, though Naomi says there are a few women now, depending on the play. Kabuki began with women, Yuichi adds, until a scandal got them banned; Yuichi checked beforehand.
  • ほんもの (本物) — the real thing, the real deal. The opposite is にせもの, from lesson 11’s fake phone number. Yuichi’s example: このダイヤ、本物？にせものでしょ？, “is this diamond real? It’s a fake, right?”
  • ないしょ (内緒) — a secret, privacy. Add 話 for 内緒話, a private talk: 私の目の前で内緒話をしないで下さい, “please don’t whisper secrets right in front of me.” And there’s always someone, Naomi says, who tells everyone これは内緒ですから誰にも言わないで下さい. Saying 内緒で, Peter notes, is asking people not to pass it on; if Peter told Naomi これ内緒だよ！, Naomi would tell everybody. That’s 秘密をばらす, says Yuichi.

The lesson notes add 声, voice; 指切りげんまん, a pinky promise; うそ, a lie; and 針, a needle or a stitch. The cards below cover them, with a few more from the dialogue and the chat.

Beyond the lessonNaomi’s dictionary was right. 俳優 is anyone who acts for a living, on stage, film or television (歌舞伎俳優, a kabuki actor); 女優 is defined as 女性の俳優, a female 俳優, and 男優 as a male one. One dictionary’s earliest example of 女優 is from Mori Ōgai’s story 『舞姫』 (1890).

Yuichi’s kabuki history. Kabuki began around 1603 with Okuni, a performer claiming to be a shrine maiden from Izumo Taisha, who danced in Kyoto dressed as a man while a male actor played the teahouse woman: cross-dressing both ways from the start. Women’s kabuki was banned in 1629 for corrupting public morals. Brothels ran many of the troupes and fights broke out over the performers, so it was less one scandal than a whole scene. Boys’ kabuki took over until it too was banned, in 1652, and men have played the women’s roles ever since, as 女方 (also written 女形). The National Theatre’s guide says kabuki grew up as a theatre in which men play every role. Women do appear as guests now and then: the actress Terajima Shinobu played a wife at the Kabukiza in October 2023 and again in December 2025. There are women’s troupes too, such as Nagoya’s むすめ歌舞伎, which began in 1983 as an all-women company. So Naomi’s “a few women, depending on the play” is about right, as guest actresses rather than kabuki actors.

実は and 本物, kabuki style. Kabuki role names use 実は for a character in disguise: 関兵衛 実は大伴黒主, “Sekibei, actually Ōtomo no Kuronushi”, which is Tom’s move exactly. And backstage, a prop that’s an everyday object used as it is counts as 本物; one made specially is a 拵え物.

Beyond the lesson内緒 is a sound change of 内証 (ないしょう), which is also a Buddhist word for enlightenment within one’s own heart; its second sense is keeping something private. ばらす has other jobs: to take apart (機械をばらして修理する, take a machine apart to fix it) and to kill (裏切り者をばらす), and the dictionary notes that 殺す is sometimes given the reading ばらす. Letting a secret out is only its third sense.

なんちゃって comes from なんて言っちゃって, “and so I went and said…”: you add it to make light of a slip, an exaggeration or a lie. Before a noun it means imitation: なんちゃってブランド, a knock-off brand; なんちゃって制服, a look-alike school uniform. Peter has a point about its age. In 1977–78, Japan went wild for the ナンチャッテおじさん, a middle-aged man said to cry or groan on Tokyo trains until the other passengers grew worried, then raise both arms into a ring overhead and say なーんちゃって. It all began with a listener’s letter to a late-night radio show, and the station itself says nobody can now be sure it was true.

5

〜ために

The grammar point: a verb + ために, for a purpose

ために shows a purpose, Peter explains: after a verb in the plain non-past, it means “so that” or “in order to”. Yuichi’s example is Junko’s answer, トム・クルーズに会うためです, “to meet Tom Cruise”. On its own, Naomi says, that isn’t clear enough: it needs a subject. So: トム・クルーズに会うために、私はアメリカに来ました, “I came to America in order to meet Tom Cruise”, and likewise 英語を勉強するために、私はアメリカに来ました. In everyday English, Peter adds, “in order to” sounds a little stiff, so it’s often just “to”: “I came to America to study English”, with the purpose still in there.

In Japanese, Naomi says, ためです is a very common way to explain why. Why do you jog? 健康のためです, “for my health”, Yuichi answers, or, Naomi adds, やせるためです, “to lose weight”.

1

Verb + ために + what you do

英語を勉強するために、アメリカに来た. The first verb is plain non-past; you do the second in order to do the first.

2

Noun + のために

健康のために, “for my health”; 家族のために, “for my family”. After a noun, add の.

3

…ためです, as an answer

「どうして？」「やせるためです」. The purpose on its own, as the reason.

The lesson notes fill in the rest. ため itself is a noun meaning something useful, something to someone’s advantage. In the full pattern, both verbs have the same subject, and the first is usually an action, something you want or are willing to do.

ために or ように?

Both translate as “so that”, and lesson 11 taught the ように one, so the notes compare them.

ためにように
The first verban action you choose to doa state: a potential, a negative, something that happens
The subjectsthe sameoften different
Example英語えいごを勉べん強きょうするために、アメリカに来きたみんなが見みえるように、大おおきく書かいた

The notes’ own example: a teacher writes in big letters, みんなが見えるように, “so that everyone can see”, never 見えるために, because it’s the class who will see, and seeing isn’t something you do on purpose. Even with one subject, a good outcome you hope for, such as not catching a cold, sounds more natural with ように: 風邪を引かないように、毎日しょうがを食べます, “I eat ginger every day so I won’t catch a cold”; the notes mark the ために version as doubtful. Tom’s ばれないように works the same way. Lesson 11’s section 6 has the longer comparison.

留りゅう学がくするために、アルバイトでお金かねを貯ためている。

I’m saving up from my part-time job to study abroad.

「どうしてそんなに早はやく起おきたの？」「始発しはつの電車でんしゃに乗のるためだよ。」

“Why did you get up so early?” “To catch the first train.”

子こどもの将しょう来らいのために、毎月まいつき少すこしずつ貯金ちょきんしている。

We put a little aside every month for our child’s future.

Watch forA purpose takes the plain non-past. After a past form, ため gives a cause instead, and sounds formal: 寝坊したため、遅刻しました, “I was late because I overslept.” Section 6 has more.

ため, three ways

Beyond the lesson: for someone’s good, for a purpose, and because of

Beyond the lessonThe notes call ため a noun meaning something useful, and that’s the key to all its uses: what something is for, who it’s good for, and what brought it about.

MeaningAfterExample
For the good ofnoun + の家族かぞくのために働はたらく
work for your family
In order toverb, non-past合格ごうかくするために勉べん強きょうする
study to pass
Because ofnoun + の, or any verb大雨おおあめのため中止ちゅうし
cancelled because of heavy rain
  • Doing someone good. ためになる is “to do you good, to be instructive”: ためになる本, a book you learn from. あなたのためを思って言っているの, “I’m saying this for your own good”, is the classic parent’s line.
  • The cause. In speech you’d say 雨だから or 雨なので; 雨のため belongs to notices, the news and station announcements, such as 強風のため、運転を見合わせています, “services are suspended because of strong winds.”

Beyond the lessonWhat the dictionary says. One dictionary lists four senses of ため: benefit (為にならない本, a book that does you no good; 子供の為を思う, to think of a child’s good), cause (雨の為に延期する, to postpone because of rain), purpose (健康の為に運動をする, to exercise for your health), and “as far as someone is concerned” (私の為には叔父にあたる, “who is an uncle to me”). Another says the purpose sense grew out of the benefit one: you act for what you hope to gain.

Choosing to do it. A Japan Foundation column for teachers puts the rule simply: purpose ために follows verbs for things you can decide to do, and ように things you can’t. You can ask about a purpose with it, too: 何のために働きますか, “what do you work for?”

With or without に. In writing, the same column says, ために can shrink to ため with no change in meaning: 生きがいを見つけるため働く, “I work to find something to live for”. But in a study of agricultural research papers, about 70% of plain ため gave a cause and about 70% of ために a purpose, and the author reads 来年アメリカへ行くため貯金している as leaning towards “because”. For a purpose, keep the に.

The cause is formal, and factual. The cause ため belongs to a formal style, and beginners’ textbooks usually leave it out; it’s common in academic writing. It reports causes as facts, so it can’t lead into an invitation or a request: 映画の切符が2枚ありますので、一緒に行きませんか, “I’ve got two cinema tickets, so shall we go?”, works with ので or から, never ため. And it tends to come with bad news.

先輩せんぱいのアドバイスは、いつもとてもためになる。

My senior’s advice always does me a lot of good.

事故じこのため、電車でんしゃが三さん十じゅっ分ぷんほど遅おくれております。

Because of an accident, trains are running about thirty minutes late.

7

指切りげんまん

Beyond the lesson: what the chant says, and where it came from

Beyond the lessonWhat the words say. げんまん is 拳万, “ten thousand fists”: break the promise and you get punched ten thousand times, or so the dictionaries say, hedging with “it is said”; one calls it a children’s word from the Kantō region. 飲ます is another form of 飲ませる, “to make someone drink”, recorded since the 16th century, and one dictionary uses this very chant as its example. The 針千本 is disputed: a folklorist reads it as a thousand sewing needles, while a dictionary of Edo-period children’s games says it’s the spiny pufferfish, ハリセンボン. (The fish, for the record, has 400 to 500 spines.)

Other versions. The encyclopedia that records the familiar chant labels it as Kanagawa’s, and gives a Tokyo version, 指切り、かまきり、嘘いうものは、地獄の釜へぽったりしょ, “liars plop into the cauldron of hell”, and one from Aichi, いびきり、いびきり、3年過ぎたら、乳から下へくされよ, “after three years, rot from the chest down”.

Naomi’s courtesans. The dictionaries do give 指切り a second sense: a woman, especially a courtesan, cutting off the tip of a little finger and giving it to a man as proof of sincerity. It’s older than the 18th century. One dictionary’s first example is a 1692 play that already calls it the fashion, and a 1678 guide to the pleasure quarters lists finger-cutting among the ways to prove devotion, along with written vows, cut hair, tattoos and torn-off nails, all of which grew formulaic and commercial. Did the children’s promise come from it? Some reference books say so, with “it is said”. But the same dictionary finds the children’s sense earlier, in a verse of 1638, and there are other theories: that 指切り is 手切り, which wore down into 契り, a pledge; or that it carries on an ancient gesture for sealing a contract.

And the yakuza? Yakuza cut off a finger to apologise or take the blame (指を詰める, or エンコヅメ). The same folklorist says this, too, is said to come from the courtesans; an encyclopedia instead traces finger-cutting to a punishment in medieval law, which seems to have lived on afterwards as private justice.

In English, too. New York children were hooking little fingers by 1848, when John Russell Bartlett’s Dictionary of Americanisms recorded them “interlocking the little finger of each other’s right hands” to seal a bargain, chanting: “Pinky, pinky, bow-bell, / Whoever tells a lie, / Will sink down to the bad place, / And never rise up again.” Today it’s a pinky swear, which Cambridge defines as two people, especially children, promising something by connecting their little fingers.

Keeping and breaking promises

  • 約束する — to promise; 約束を守る, to keep a promise; 約束を破る, to break one.
  • うそをつく — to tell a lie; うそつき, a liar. On its own, うそ！ is “no way!”, as Junko says in section 1.
  • 飲ます — the chant’s short causative, another form of 飲ませる, “to make someone drink” (the causative is in lesson 3).

一度いちどした約束やくそくは、必かならず守まもるようにしている。

Once I’ve made a promise, I make sure I keep it.

約束やくそくを破やぶったら、針はり千本せんぼんだよ。

Break the promise, and it’s a thousand needles.

Masks in Japan

Beyond the lesson: Peter’s question, why so many people wear them

Beyond the lessonWhich verb? Naomi’s rule fits what linguists describe. A 2003 study of Japanese verbs for putting things on splits what we wear into the essentials, which have verbs of their own (着る for a shirt, はく for trousers), and accessories, which mostly take verbs of putting something in place. It lists マスク under つける, with watches and earrings, and notes that する works for accessories in general. To take one off: はずす or とる. Official notices say 着用, as in マスクの着用.

A hundred years of masks. During the 1918–20 flu pandemic, the Home Ministry’s hygiene bureau put out posters urging masks and gargling. One, printed in its 1922 report 『流行性感冒』, shows masked passengers on a train under the banner 汽車電車人の中では「マスク」せよ, “on trains, on trams, in crowds: wear a mask”, and 外出の後は「ウガヒ」忘るな, “after going out, don’t forget to gargle”. Above them, a man sick in bed thinks 「マスク」をかけぬと…, “if you don’t wear a mask…”. Note the verb: かける, as with glasses.

Yuichi’s cedar pollen. A 2019 national survey of ear, nose and throat doctors and their families found 38.8% allergic to cedar pollen, and 49.2% with allergic rhinitis of some kind, with a sharp rise among teenagers. The government quotes the same survey: hay fever went from 19.6% in 1998 to 29.8% in 2008 and 42.5% in 2019, up about ten points a decade. In 2023 it set out to cut Japan’s 4.31 million hectares of planted cedar by about a fifth by 2033, enough, it says, to bring even a heavy pollen year down to a normal one, and to halve pollen output in about thirty years.

After COVID. Since 13 March 2023, the health ministry has left masks to the individual: 個人の判断が基本. It still recommends one at the doctor’s, when visiting hospitals or care homes full of people at high risk, and on packed trains and buses at rush hour, and it asks that nobody be made to put one on or take one off against their will. Many kept them on anyway. In a September 2023 survey of 3,360 people around Tokyo, Nagoya and Osaka, about six in ten of those still wearing masks thought taking them off was fine. A market-research firm’s weekly tracking found 70.7% wearing a mask in public in March 2023, falling to a low of 38.2% in August 2025, and back above half in the flu season after.

Not just for germs. A 伊達マスク is a mask worn for some reason other than its real purpose, like 伊達眼鏡, glasses worn for looks. In a December 2022 survey of 813 people by Tohoku University graduate students, reluctance to stop wearing a mask went with fear of infection most of all, but also with unease about showing one’s bare face, 素顔を見られる不安.

花粉症かふんしょうの季節きせつは、マスクが手放てばなせない。

In hay-fever season, I can’t do without a mask.

写真しゃしんを撮とるので、マスクを外はずしてもらえますか。

We’re taking a photo, so could you take your mask off?

Quiz

Pick the word or form that fits. Each answer comes with the reason.

1. 新あたらしいパソコンを買かう＿＿＿、アルバイトを始はじめた。

I started a part-time job to buy a new computer.

A purpose you work towards yourself, with an action verb: ために.

2. 後うしろの席せきの人ひとにも聞きこえる＿＿＿、大おおきい声こえで話はなした。

I spoke up so that the people in the back seats could hear too.

聞こえる isn’t something anyone chooses to do, and the subjects differ: ように.

3. 家族かぞく＿＿＿、一いっ生しょう懸けん命めい働はたらいている。

I work hard for my family.

After a noun, add の: 家族のために, “for my family”.

4. 「どうして毎朝まいあさ走はしっているんですか。」「やせる＿＿＿。」

“Why do you run every morning?” “To lose weight.”

Giving the purpose as an answer: verb + ためです.

5. 大雨おおあめの＿＿＿、電車でんしゃが止とまっています。

The trains have stopped because of the heavy rain.

Noun + の + ため, “because of”: formal, the language of announcements. ので and から would need 大雨なので or 大雨だから.

6. この本ほんは、とても＿＿＿になった。

This book taught me a lot.

ためになる: to do you good, to be instructive.

7. 電車でんしゃに乗のるときは、いつもマスクを＿＿＿います。

I always wear a mask when I take the train.

Masks take する (or つける). 着る is for clothes on your body, はく for shoes and trousers.

8. うそが、すぐに＿＿＿しまった。

My lie was found out straight away.

ばれる: a secret or a lie comes out. ばらす is someone letting it out.

9. 友達ともだちが、私わたしの秘密ひみつをみんなに＿＿＿。

My friend told everyone my secret.

Someone lets a secret out: ばらす, which takes を.

10. このサイン、＿＿＿だったらすごいけど、たぶん偽物にせものだね。

If this autograph were genuine it’d be amazing, but it’s probably a fake.

本物: the real thing, the opposite of 偽物.

11. このことは、みんなには＿＿＿にしておいてね。

Keep this a secret from everyone, OK?

内緒にする: to keep something secret.

12. ＿＿＿、まだ宿しゅく題だいが終おわっていないんです。

Actually, I haven’t finished my homework yet.

実は: “actually, to tell the truth”, before a confession.

13. 子こどもに＿＿＿をつくのは、よくない。

It’s not good to lie to children.

うそをつく: to tell a lie.

14. もう夜よる遅おそいので、大おおきい＿＿＿で話はなさないでください。

It’s late, so please don’t talk loudly.

大きい声で話す: to talk loudly. 音 is a sound or a noise, not a voice.
