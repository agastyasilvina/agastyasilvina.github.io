Scene

Back at the bank. A chime, and a number lights up.

Announcement

103番ばんの番号札ばんごうふだをお持もちのお客きゃく様さま、3番ばんの窓口まどぐちまでお越こしください。

Would the customer holding ticket 103 please come to window 3.

Teller

いらっしゃいませ……げっ。（また、あのちんちくりんのホストだ……）

Good afternoon… Ugh. (That short little host again…)

Taiga

あれ、偶然ぐうぜん！また君きみだね。やっぱり運命うんめいを感かんじるなあ。

Hey, what a coincidence! It’s you again. This has to be fate.

Teller

（コホン）……本日ほんじつは、どういったご用件ようけんでしょうか。

(Ahem.) …And what can I do for you today?

Taiga

この小銭こぜにをお札さつに替かえてほしいんですけど、両りょう替がえって手数料てすうりょうかかります？

I’d like these coins changed into notes. Is there a fee for that?

Teller

硬貨こうかが50枚まいまでなら無料むりょうですが、51枚まいからは315円えんの手数料てすうりょうがかかります。

Up to 50 coins it’s free, but from 51 coins there’s a ¥315 fee.

Teller

501枚まい以上いじょうですと630円えんです。……硬貨こうかは、たくさんお持もちですか。

And for 501 coins or more, it’s ¥630. …Do you have a lot of coins?

Taiga

ええ、それはもう、たっぷりございますよぉ。

Oh, I have plenty, believe me.

Sound

A heavy bag thumps onto the counter.

Teller

それでは、お待まちの間あいだに、こちらの用紙ようしにご記入きにゅういただけますか。

Then while you wait, could you fill in this form?

Teller

（袋ふくろを持もち上あげて）うっ……重おもっ！

(Lifting the bag.) Oof… heavy!

Teller

虎とら畑ばたけ様さま、虎畑大河とらばたけたいが様さま、窓口まどぐちまでお越こしください。

Torabatake-sama, Taiga Torabatake-sama, please come to the window.

Taiga

だからさあ、大河たいがでいいって言いってるじゃないですか。

Look, I keep telling you, Taiga is fine.

Teller

大変たいへんお待またせいたしました。合計ごうけいで6万まん5332円えんでございました。

Thank you very much for waiting. It came to ¥65,332 in total.

Taiga

じゃあ、その半額はんがくを普通ふつう預金よきんの口座こうざに入いれてもらえます？

Then could you put half of that into my ordinary savings account?

Teller

3万まん2666円えんを、普通ふつう預金よきんにお預あずけ入いれということで、よろしいですね。

So that’s ¥32,666 paid into your ordinary savings account, correct?

Teller

かしこまりました。お手数てすうですが、こちらの用紙ようしにもご記入きにゅうをお願ねがいいたします。

Certainly. Sorry to trouble you, but please fill in this form as well.

Taiga

（ため息いき）えー、また書かくの？

(Sighs.) What, more writing?

Teller

ちなみに、お急いそぎのお客きゃく様さまには、ATMでのお預あずけ入いれもおすすめしております。

By the way, for customers in a hurry, we also suggest paying in at the ATM.

Taiga

ATMじゃ、君きみに会あえないでしょ。

I can’t see you at an ATM, can I?

2

The scene

Naomi, Yuichi and Peter, before and after the dialogue

“More Money, More Problems”, part 4, and more useful words for the bank, Naomi says. Yuichi gives the story: Taigaさんがまた銀行にいます, Taiga is back at the bank, and 理由はまだわかりません, we don’t know why yet. It’s a conversation between Taiga and the person at the counter, 受付の人, and it’s in polite Japanese.

After the dialogue

The conversation was meant to be polite, Naomi says, but 僕は運命を感じるな is casual, and so is Taiga’s last line, また？ Why? Probably Taiga’s personality showing through, Naomi thinks, and partly Taiga thinking out loud. Peter puts it as two reasons: one line is Taiga musing about luck and fate, and with the other, caught off guard, Taiga drops the polite mask and speaks from the heart. Normal people don’t say 運命を感じるな much, Yuichi notes: ちょっとおかしいですね. そういうキャラですね, Peter says: that’s the character. Hence Taigaって呼んで下さい to a bank teller, Naomi adds.

Did Naomi check any facts for this lesson, Peter asks? 両替の手数料はすべて合ってますね: the exchange fees are all correct, at least at Mitsui Sumitomo; every bank has its own rules, and Mizuho might differ. Peter is impressed by the service itself. In America, a long time ago, the bank wouldn’t take your coins unless you rolled them yourself, pushing them into little paper wrappers. In Japan it’s clear plastic rolls, Yuichi says, and we never do it ourselves, Naomi says. Peter was really happy to find that in Japan you could just bring your coins to the bank and get the amount back.

One warning for foreigners living in Japan, though, Peter says: don’t mix in coins from your own country. It might be taken for a crime, Naomi suggests. No, Peter says, the bank just gets angry, very angry, Naomi agrees, because it damages the machines.

Phrases worth taking from the chat:

  • 理由はまだわかりません — we don’t know why yet.
  • そういうキャラ — that’s the kind of character Taiga is: キャラ, short for キャラクター.
  • 〜は全部合っています — … are all correct.
  • 気を付けた方がいい — you’d better watch out.

Grammar: AをBに両替する, “change A into B”; 〜たいんですけど, opening a request and leaving it hanging, from lesson 21; 手数料がかかる, “there’s a fee”, with は for the question.

Grammar: お待ちの間に, the grammar point again: “while you are waiting”, with に for “during”; ご記入をお願いいたします, a very polite request, from lesson 22.

Vocabulary

The hosts’ words, in their order

The hosts’ notes first, then a card for each word, plus more from the dialogue, each with an example of my own.

  • ちんちくりん — very short. Its original meaning is very short, Naomi says, and it’s also used when something isn’t quite right, out of balance or odd: あの人のズボンはちんちくりんだ, “that person’s trousers are too short”, so you can see the ankles and socks, Yuichi says. In the dialogue, the teller doesn’t mean “very short” so much as “weird”, they agree.
  • こぜに (小銭) — coins, small change: literally “small money”, Yuichi says, so coins, or a small amount of money. You can also say 硬貨, a coin: 1円硬貨, 5円硬貨, 100円硬貨, and the 500-, 50- and 10-yen coins too. The one-yen coin is made of aluminium, Naomi says, very light, and worthless, Yuichi jokes. In conversation it’s usually 1円だま: だま or たま is a ball or circle, as in お年玉, Yuichi points out. In 硬貨, the first kanji is 硬い, hard, and the second is currency: “hard currency”. Notes are お札 or 紙幣, paper money: 1000円札 or 1000円紙幣, and the 2000-, 5000- and 10,000-yen notes. Is the 2000-yen note still in use? It is, Naomi says: the post office tries to use them up, and gives Naomi change all in 2000-yen notes. The government’s stance is a bit vague, Peter thinks. 超使えないですね、2000円札, says Naomi: super useless. It was made in 2000, Yuichi adds.
  • りょうがえ (両替) — changing money: AをBに両替する, exchange A for B, as in 円をドルに両替する; in the dialogue, coins into notes. Or すみません。500円硬貨に両替して下さい, “could you change this into 500-yen coins?”
  • てすうりょう (手数料) — a service charge: 手数, trouble, and 料, a fee. 手数 is literally the number of hands, Naomi says, so the amount of trouble. The dialogue has お手数ですが、こちらの用紙にご記入をお願いします: “I know it’s a bother, but…”, Peter says. Business people use it even when they don’t think it’s any trouble, Naomi says, and it’s very polite, says Yuichi: not just 電話を下さい but お手数ですが電話を下さいますか？ Peter first met it in an email from a very polite person and had no idea what it meant: お手数ですが添付した文章をCheckして下さい. If you see お手数ですが, you’re about to be asked to do something, Peter says; and when you ask for something in business, adding it is the safe choice, Naomi says.
  • はんがく (半額) — half price, half the amount: 半, half, and 額, amount, so the kanji give it away; the whole amount is 全額, from lesson 23. ビール半額, half-price beer; 半額セール, a 50%-off sale. You see it all over town, Naomi says. Peter hasn’t noticed it much, which Naomi puts down to Peter never going shopping.
  • ようけん (用件) — business, the matter at hand, often with the polite ご: どのようなご用件ですか？, “how may I help you?” You hear it when answering the phone at work, Naomi says, and at home too, when a stranger calls and you can’t tell what they want, such as a sales call: 「どのようなご用件ですか？」「じゅうたんいりませんか？」「あ、結構です。」 — “How can I help you?” “Would you like a carpet?” “Ah, no thank you.” That’s 結構です from lesson 22.

The lesson notes add 偶然 (by chance), 運命 (fate), 記入 (filling in, from lesson 22) and 以上 (… or more). The cards below cover them all, with more words from the dialogue.

Beyond the lessonWhat the dictionaries say. ちんちくりん has two senses: short in height, a word for mocking short people, first recorded in 1660; and clothes too short for the wearer, first recorded in 1916, in Natsume Sōseki’s 明暗. Neither dictionary lists a general “odd” sense, and the first sense is plainly an insult.

一円玉 is the everyday name for the one-yen aluminium coin, with a young tree on its face, issued since 1955. The 玉 is added to nouns for round things, as in 十円玉 or 玉ねぎ, an onion. The お年玉 Yuichi mentions is written with the same kanji but explained differently: 大辞泉 glosses 年玉 as “the year’s gift”, and an encyclopedia says folklorists favour a gift from the god of the new year. Giving children pocket money at New Year is, on the whole, a modern custom, the 日本国語大辞典 adds.

お持ちのお客様

The grammar point: お + ます-stem + の + noun

Today’s target phrase is お持ちのお客様, “the customer who has something”, Naomi says: カードをお持ちのお客様、カウンターまでお越し下さい. The structure, Peter explains, is the polite prefix お, plus the ます-stem of a verb, plus の and a noun. Another example is お待ちの間, “while you’re waiting”. These expressions show the speaker’s respect for someone when describing that person’s action or state, and you’ll meet them all the time as a customer, since the company or whoever serves you will talk to you very respectfully.

Take 帰る客, a customer who is going home. The respectful version is お帰りのお客様, and Peter and Naomi build it step by step. The verb 帰る becomes its ます-stem, 帰り; put お in front, お帰り; follow it with の; then the noun 客, raised with an honorific prefix and suffix to お客様. All together: お帰りのお客様, “the customer who is leaving”, an extremely polite way to say 帰る客. You probably won’t use these yourself unless you work in a hotel, a restaurant or business, Peter says, but you will hear them, so it’s important to know what’s being said to you.

The lesson notes compare it with お〜になる, from lesson 22: both raise the person you’re talking about.

Plainお〜になるお〜の
持もっているお客きゃく様さまお持もちになっているお客きゃく様さまお持もちのお客きゃく様さま
待まっている間あいだお待まちになっている間あいだお待まちの間あいだ

The notes add more examples of the pattern: お泊りのお客様, guests staying with us; お気づきの場合, if you notice; お申し出の件, the matter you raised. And in a sentence: お帰りのお客様は、こちらへどうぞ, “customers who are leaving, this way, please.”

1

The shape

お + ます-stem + の + noun. 持つ → 持ち → お持ちのお客様; 待つ → 待ち → お待ちの間.

2

Someone else’s action or state

It stands in for 〜ている + noun, with respect. Never for yourself: not お持ちの私.

3

Mostly heard, not said

Announcements, shops, hotels, banks. Recognise it; use it when you serve customers.

お急いそぎの方かたは、こちらの窓口まどぐちへどうぞ。

If you’re in a hurry, please come to this window.

お探さがしの本ほんは、こちらでしょうか。

Is this the book you’re looking for?

お使つかいのスマートフォンで、QRコードを読よみ取とってください。

Please scan the QR code with your smartphone.

お〜の and お手数

Beyond the lesson: where お〜の fits in the keigo guide, and お手数 in the dictionary

Beyond the lessonWhere お〜の fits. The government’s 2007 keigo guide, from lesson 22, lists お(ご)〜だ among the general respectful forms, with お読みだ and 御利用だ as its examples. It’s mostly used with です, as お(ご)〜です, the guide notes, and the same cautions apply as for お〜になる; 御存じだ, for instance, is the respectful 知っている. The guide doesn’t discuss the lesson’s version, but the logic carries over: お持ち behaves like a noun, so it can take です, お持ちですか, “do you have it?”, or join another noun with の, お持ちのお客様.

A humble verb inside? One of the notes’ examples, お申し出の件, hides a verb you normally use only about yourself: 申す, the humble “say”. The guide takes up exactly this worry about お申し出ください and 御持参ください, which contain 申す and 参る. Inside these expressions, it says, they have lost their humble force, so they’re fine for the other person’s actions.

お手数. 手数 is the number of steps a job takes, 手数のかかる料理, “a dish that takes a lot of work”, and also the trouble you go to for someone else, 大辞泉 says: お手数をかけて恐縮です, “I’m sorry to put you to such trouble”, or お手数ですが、よろしくお願いします. お手数 is its respectful form. The 日本国語大辞典 quotes Natsume Sōseki’s 道草 (1915): 何うも御手数でした、ありがたう, “thanks for all your trouble.” And a 手数料 is, in 大辞泉’s words, money paid in return for the trouble taken.

お手数てすうをおかけしますが、よろしくお願ねがいいたします。

I’m sorry to put you to the trouble, and thank you in advance. — closing a request by email

Coins and notes

Beyond the lesson: the 2000-yen note, the one-yen coin, three 500-yen coins and the twenty-coin rule

Beyond the lessonThe 2000-yen note was first issued on 19 July 2000, with Okinawa’s Shureimon gate on the front and scenes from the Tale of Genji picture scrolls on the back; the Kyushu–Okinawa summit opened in Nago two days later. It isn’t a summit souvenir, though, the Bank of Japan’s Naha branch says: like the other notes, it’s a permanent note meant for everyday use.

Naomi’s verdict shows in the figures. At the end of September 2026 there were ¥184.4 billion of 2000-yen notes in circulation, about 92 million notes, against about 4.49 billion 1000-yen notes: roughly 49 of those for every 2000-yen note. At the peak, in August 2004, there were ¥1.03 trillion of them. The Bank of Japan ordered 770 million in fiscal 2000 and 110 million in fiscal 2003, and none since, and the redesign of 3 July 2024 covered only the 10,000-, 5,000- and 1,000-yen notes. But it is still issued and still legal tender: a note keeps that status unless a law specifically says otherwise.

The one-yen coin is pure aluminium, 20 mm across and exactly 1 gram, and has been issued since 1955.

Three 500-yen coins. The notes’ 古い500円硬貨 has a history: the coin has changed twice. It was cupronickel from 1982 and nickel-brass from 2000, a coin the Finance Ministry says used the latest anti-counterfeiting technology, and since 1 November 2021 it has been a bicolour coin of three metals. All three are still legal tender, so when a vending machine won’t take an old one, that’s the machine, not the law; in June 2008, “old” meant the 1982 coin. Foreign coins had caused trouble in Japanese machines just before then: police reported 821,654 altered Korean 500-won coins used to rob vending machines in 1999 alone.

Twenty coins. Japan’s currency law makes coins legal tender only up to twenty times their face value: in other words, the Finance Ministry explains, a payment of up to 20 coins of a kind, since storing and counting more is a burden. You can pay with more if the other side agrees. A shop could turn down Taiga’s bag of coins; the bank, for a fee, will take it.

この500円玉えんだま、古ふるいけど、まだ使つかえますよ。

This 500-yen coin is old, but it’s still good.

8

Coins at the bank

Beyond the lesson: money changers, and what banks charge for coins today

Beyond the lessonMoney changers came first. Changing money for a fee is older than banking in Japan. An encyclopedia dates the first money changer’s shop, opened by 天王寺屋五兵衛, to 1628, and has the great Edo house 三井両替店 becoming a full money changer in 1689. Money changers charged fees for exchanging and selling coins and made huge profits, and quite a few of them later turned into banks. In medieval Italy, money changers set out tables in the town squares to weigh and swap coins, and the Italian for such a table, banco, is where the word bank comes from.

The fees, then and now. Naomi checked the lesson’s figures at SMBC in 2008, but its archived fee pages from the time don’t list exchange fees, so I couldn’t confirm them. Today, at the counter, SMBC charges ¥220 to exchange up to 10 pieces for account holders (¥330 for others), ¥770 for 11 to 500, ¥1,540 for 501 to 1,000, and ¥770 for each 500 beyond, counting whichever is larger, the pieces you bring or the pieces you take away. Paying coins into an account at the counter is free up to 100 coins a day and ¥550 for 101 to 500; SMBC’s own ATMs take coins free, and convenience-store ATMs don’t take them at all. MUFG’s counter fees for large numbers of coins run on similar lines: free up to 100, then ¥550, ¥1,100 and ¥1,650.

The post office stops being free. ゆうちょ銀行 started charging for coins on 17 January 2022. At first its counters were free only up to 50 coins; today they’re free up to 100, then ¥550 for 101 to 500 and ¥1,100 for 501 to 1,000, and the bank may turn away large amounts of coins altogether. Its ATMs charge ¥110 to ¥330 to pay in up to 100 coins, and ¥110 to take coins out. The bank said it wanted to keep its services stable, with fees matched to each channel’s convenience and cost, while it pushes digital, cashless and paperless banking.

The clear rolls Yuichi mentions are 棒金, “coin bars”. A patent for coin wrapping describes them as stacks of 50 coins, wrapped either in clear cellophane or plastic, so you can check the type and number of coins, or in paper that can carry the bank’s name. At SMBC’s change machines, getting rolls takes the machine’s own dedicated card.

今いまは、窓口まどぐちで硬貨こうかを入にゅう金きんすると、手数料てすうりょうがかかることがある。

These days, paying coins in at the counter can cost you a fee.

Quiz

Pick the word or form that fits. Each answer comes with the reason.

1. 103番ばんの番号札ばんごうふだを＿＿＿のお客きゃく様さま、窓口まどぐちまでお越こしください。

Would the customer holding ticket 103 please come to the counter.

お + ます-stem + の + noun: お持ちのお客様.

2. ＿＿＿の間あいだに、こちらの用紙ようしにご記入きにゅうください。

While you wait, please fill in this form.

お + ます-stem + の: お待ちの間, “while you’re waiting”.

3. ＿＿＿のお客きゃく様さまは、こちらへどうぞ。

Customers who are leaving, this way, please.

The ます-stem is 帰り, so it’s お帰りのお客様.

4. ＿＿＿の方かたは、ATMをご利用りようください。

If you’re in a hurry, please use the ATM.

お急ぎの方: those who are in a hurry.

5. ＿＿＿のお客きゃく様さまにご案内あんないいたします。

An announcement for our customers.

利用 is a word from Chinese, so it takes ご: ご利用のお客様.

6. 1万円札まんえんさつを千円札せんえんさつに＿＿＿してもらった。

I had a 10,000-yen note changed into 1,000-yen notes.

AをBに両替する: to change A into B.

7. この＿＿＿を、お札さつに両りょう替がえしたいんですけど。

I’d like to change these coins into notes.

小銭: coins, small change. 紙幣 are notes already.

8. ＿＿＿ですが、こちらにご記入きにゅうをお願ねがいします。

Sorry to trouble you, but please fill this in.

お手数ですが: a polite cushion before a request.

9. 閉店へいてん前まえは、お弁当べんとうが＿＿＿になる。

Boxed lunches go half price before closing time.

半額: half price. 全額 is the full amount.

10. どのようなご＿＿＿でしょうか。

How may I help you?

ご用件: what someone has come about.

11. 硬貨こうか501枚まい＿＿＿は、手数料てすうりょうが630円えんです。

501 coins or more cost ¥630.

以上: that number or more. 以下 is that number or less.

12. 駅えきで＿＿＿、昔むかしの友ともだちに会あった。

I ran into an old friend at the station by chance.

偶然: by chance.

13. ＿＿＿いくらになりますか。

How much is that altogether?

全部で: altogether, in total.

14. この自販機じはんきは、古ふるい500円えん＿＿＿が使つかえない。

This vending machine doesn’t take old 500-yen coins.

硬貨: a coin. 紙幣 and お札 are notes.
